0:00–0:20 — Project 19. One sentence: we are building the arm that tells you, in newtons, how hard it is pushing on a patient.

The idea

Imagine leaning on a machine and knowing exactly how hard it pushes back.

A frail patient needs steadying. A rigid robot can hurt them on contact. Every soft alternative we found asserts its contact force. None of them measure it.

0:20–0:50 — Set the hook, then land the real gap: the field has demos, not instruments. Don't say "soft = safe" — slide 10 is where the argument lands.

The customer

Buyer

Clinics and care facilities

Physical therapy clinics and elder-care facilities. They are short on therapist hours, not on patients.

End user

Patients

Need low-force steadying, guided reach and postural support — repeated hundreds of times, not once.

End user

Therapists

One therapist, several patients. A passive gravity-balancing orthosis (T-WREX) produced sustained gains on about 4 minutes of therapist attention per 1-hour session.

Patients need repetitions. Clinics need them delivered without a therapist inside every one.

The problem and why it matters

Dose

Therapy is rationed by therapist time

Conventional therapy delivers a mean 32 upper-limb repetitions per session (95% CI 20–44). Instrumented protocols have hit 322.

RATULS (n = 770) was null against dose-matched therapy — so the defensible thesis is dose, not kinematics.

Safety

No standard covers this contact

ISO 13482 publishes no contact limits, and ISO/TS 15066 states it does not apply to non-industrial robots.

The pain data behind both comes from healthy young volunteers. Pressure-pain threshold drops significantly over age 62 — not the intended user.

Gap

Nothing lives between 4 N and 600 N

The only published compliant-arm payload we found is 4.4 N (GummiArm). Patient-handling robots lift 60–80 kg on 140–230 kg of rigid machine.

The band in between is empty.

Support is needed at a force nobody has characterised, under a standard that does not cover it.

Value proposition

For clinics and care facilities, a compliant support arm that delivers a characterised, instrumented contact-force envelope for low-force steadying and guiding — measured, not asserted.

Unlike rigid handling robots

A series spring makes interaction force readable from deflection, and bounds it when the actuator is unpowered. For a frail user that is a stronger guarantee than "it stops fast".

Unlike the soft humanoids already built

The deliverable is the measurement, not the hug. We publish the force envelope, the test method and the conditions it holds under.

Directions we weighed

Occupied, and fails the number

Freestanding soft torso and two arms

HuggieBot already ran 48 subjects on it. And fully inflatable dies on M = PπD³/8: a Ø150 mm arm at 20 kPa folds at ~26 N·m against the ~100 N·m a 200 N lean applies at 0.5 m.

Crowded, wrong load path

Soft wearable suit

The one group delivering measured assistance to actual patients. It routes load through the patient's skeleton, which is not what the brief asks for.

Selected

One instrumented series-elastic support arm

Rigid grounded load path, compliance in the actuation, designed soft covering at contact. 28 N of assist, force read from spring deflection.

Scope for this academic year

1 · Design

Down-select the actuator

Against 28 N and ≈10 N·m — not the tens of N·m the brief implies. At that target, actuator families that looked disqualified come back into range.

2 · Build

One instrumented arm

Series-elastic arm on a base that reacts ≥330 N at hand height without tipping, with an explicit braked state for a sustained lean.

3 · Test

Measure the envelope

Force output, deflection-based force estimate, contact pressure, and unpowered residual force. Plus an approachability study with student volunteers.

Out of scope, deliberately: sit-to-stand lift (>121 N·m at the hip — a commercial lower-limb exoskeleton already fails it), unsupervised operation, and contact with frail participants. This is a bench instrument, not a clinical product.
