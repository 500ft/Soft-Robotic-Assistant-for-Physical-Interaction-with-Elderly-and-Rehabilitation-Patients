# F1 — Comparable systems: what has been built, what it achieved, where it fell short

**Scope:** prior art for a *freestanding compliant soft-robotic upper body (torso + two arms)* delivering measurable assistive force to elderly / rehabilitation patients under uncontrolled physical contact.

**Verification rule used:** every number below was read off a page I fetched in this pass. The fetched URL is named in each entry. Anything I could not confirm by fetching is written **not published / not verified** rather than filled in from memory. Where two sources disagree, both numbers are given.

---

### HuggieBot 2.0 / 3.0 — Block, Seifi, Hilliges, Gassert & Kuchenbecker, 2023 (ETH Zurich / MPI-IS)

- **Citation:** Block, A. E., Seifi, H., Hilliges, O., Gassert, R., & Kuchenbecker, K. J. (2023). In the Arms of a Robot: Designing Autonomous Hugging Robots with Intra-Hug Gestures. *ACM Transactions on Human-Robot Interaction*, 12(2), 1–49.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1145/3526110 — **fetched** the arXiv full text (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.09935) for hardware detail; publisher HTML (dl.acm.org) returned 403. Crossref record verified.
- **Open access:** yes (arXiv preprint 2202.09935; ACM THRI is OA).
- **What it is:** A custom, freestanding, human-sized hugging robot: two arms on a rigid V-shaped frame, a **custom inflated PVC torso ("HuggieChest")** that both softens the robot and senses contact, a face screen, and autonomous hug behaviours.
- **Hard numbers:** 2 × **6-DOF Kinova JACO arms** mounted horizontally on a custom metal frame; **angle and torque sensors at every joint**; torso = two heat-sealed/glued sheet-PVC chambers, rear chamber instrumented with an Adafruit BME680 barometric pressure sensor + MAX4466 electret microphone amp, streaming to ROS at ~45 Hz; heating via two Thermophore MaxHeat pads (35.6 × 68.6 cm); arms covered in foam padding; arm speed limited in firmware and arms "can be mechanically overpowered by the user"; gesture classification 88% accuracy. Mass, reach, payload, assistive force: **not published**. Certification: **not published**. Human testing: **n = 32** (gesture corpus study) + **n = 16** (validation study) — healthy adults, not patients.
- **Design relevance:** This is the closest existing thing to the proposed concept and it should be read line-by-line. Copy: the inflated-chamber-as-both-compliance-and-sensor trick (one pressure sensor + one microphone gives omnidirectional contact detection over a whole soft surface for ~$20 of parts); torque-threshold arm stopping as the "adjust to any body size" mechanism; firmware speed limits plus back-drivable arms as the safety story. Avoid: their arms are **rigid COTS Kinova units wearing foam**, i.e. they solved compliance at the *surface*, not in the *actuator* — if this project claims intrinsic compliance it must beat that, not match it.
- **Evidence grade:** B (lab prototype, extensive hardware results, 48 human subjects, no patients).
- **Where it fell short:** The paper documents its own torso failure modes: seams tearing and the resealable inflation valve popping open, both of which "allow air to escape, change the feel of the robot's torso, and require re-inflation" — they rebuilt the chamber for 3.0 with heat seal + HH-66 vinyl cement. Front-chamber sensors picked up so much cross-talk they were deleted in 3.0. No assistive force of any kind is delivered or measured; this is an affective-touch machine, not a load-bearing one.

### King Louie (and "Grub") — Best, Wilson & Killpack, 2015 (BYU RAD Lab / Pneubotics–Otherlab)

- **Citation:** Best, C. M., Wilson, J. P., & Killpack, M. D. (2015). Control of a pneumatically actuated, fully inflatable, fabric-based, humanoid robot. In *2015 IEEE-RAS 15th International Conference on Humanoid Robots (Humanoids)*, 1133–1140.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/HUMANOIDS.2015.7363495 — **fetched** the author PDF (http://radlab.byu.edu/uploads/4/4/7/0/4470886/humanoids_2015.pdf) and extracted the text directly.
- **Open access:** partial (author-hosted PDF is free; IEEE version paywalled).
- **What it is:** A **fourteen-DOF fully inflatable fabric humanoid** built by Pneubotics (an Otherlab affiliate), with a 1-DOF single-joint test article ("grub"). Structure *is* the pressurised bladder — there is no rigid skeleton. First published high-level control (MPC/LQR) for a fully inflatable soft humanoid.
- **Hard numbers:** **14 DOF** total (two arms, 5 DOF each per the related literature, plus torso joint); construction entirely **ballistic nylon fabric with internal bladders**; main structural bladder pressurised **1–2 psi gauge (≈7–14 kPa)**; antagonistic joint actuators rated **0–25 psi gauge**, but the authors ran only **0–17 psi "because of pinching effects in the main chamber at higher pressures"**; demonstrated task = pick a board off a chair and place it in a box, **8/10 successes** using joint-space MPC. Mass, payload, contact force, reach: **not published in this paper**. Certification: none. Patient testing: **none**.
- **Design relevance:** This is the existence proof that a freestanding, fully soft, dual-arm humanoid upper body can be built and controlled — so the project cannot claim that as novel. Copy: antagonistic pairs per joint on a common structural bladder; the low-pressure-structure / higher-pressure-actuator split. Avoid: assuming you can turn the actuator pressure up to spec — they lost a third of their pressure range to bladder pinching, which is exactly the failure that caps soft-arm force output.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware prototype, quantitative control results).
- **Where it fell short:** No force, payload or safety numbers are reported at all — a soft humanoid with no published payload is not evidence that soft humanoids can assist anyone. Control is the contribution; capability is undemonstrated. Pneubotics as a commercial soft-robot arm venture did not produce a marketed assistive product.

### RIBA / RIBA-II / ROBEAR — Mukai et al., 2010–2015 (RIKEN-TRI, then RIKEN-SRK)

- **Citation:** Mukai, T., Hirano, S., Nakashima, H., Kato, Y., Sakaida, Y., Guo, S., & Hosoe, S. (2010). Development of a nursing-care assistant robot RIBA that can lift a human in its arms. In *2010 IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS)*, 5996–6001.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/IROS.2010.5651735 (Crossref-verified). Specs **fetched** from RIKEN press releases: RIBA https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2009/20090827/ ; RIBA-II https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2011/20110802_2/ ; ROBEAR https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2015/20150223_2/
- **Open access:** no (IROS paper paywalled); press releases open.
- **What it is:** The three-generation Japanese patient-transfer robot line — a bear-faced humanoid upper body on a wheeled base that physically picks a person up and moves them bed↔wheelchair. The only lineage in this table that actually lifts humans.
- **Hard numbers:** **RIBA:** lifts a patient **up to 61 kg** bed→wheelchair and back; human-like arms with high-precision tactile sensors; **soft exterior of urethane foam**. **RIBA-II:** lifts **up to 80 kg**; added base and lower-back joints so it can crouch and lift from a futon at floor level; **Smart Rubber capacitance tactile sensor sheets** (first all-rubber capacitance tactile sensors) on arms and chest; arms described as high-rigidity / high-output-torque; mass **230 kg**. **ROBEAR:** mass **140 kg**; "actuator units with a very low gear ratio"; three sensor types including **torque sensors**; Smart Rubber capacitive tactile sensors. DOF for all three: **not published in the sources I could fetch**. Certification: **not published**. Patient testing: RIBA-II testing with partner nursing-care facilities was **planned**; for ROBEAR the RIKEN release states **"none have yet been deployed in care-giving facilities."**
- **Design relevance:** This is the force target. 61–80 kg lift = **~600–785 N** of sustained assistive force. Copy: foam-over-structure plus full-coverage tactile skin on every surface that touches the patient; the Smart Rubber sheet-sensor idea is directly transferable to a soft torso. Avoid: the mass trajectory. They needed 230 kg of robot to lift 80 kg of person, then spent an entire generation getting back down to 140 kg and still shipped nothing.
- **Evidence grade:** A for RIBA/RIBA-II (lifting real humans in demonstrations, care-facility partnership), B for ROBEAR.
- **Where it fell short:** RIKEN states plainly that the original RIBA had "functional limitations preventing commercialization." The **RIKEN-SRK Collaboration Center was closed at the end of March 2015**, one month after ROBEAR was announced; project lead Dr. Mukai moved to Meijo University; no unit was ever deployed in a care facility. Fifteen years, three generations, zero deployments — the most important negative result in this entire table.

### RoNA (Robotic Nursing Assistant) — Ding et al., 2014 (HStar Technologies) — *industry*

- **Citation:** Ding, J., Lim, Y.-J., Solano, M., Shadle, K., Park, C., Lin, C., & Hu, J. (2014). Giving patients a lift — the robotic nursing assistant (RoNA). In *2014 IEEE International Conference on Technologies for Practical Robot Applications (TePRA)*, 1–5.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/TePRA.2014.6869137 (Crossref-verified). IEEE Xplore blocked automated fetch (HTTP 418); specs **fetched** from a secondary conference paper describing the system: http://ieomsociety.org/ieom_2015/papers/671.pdf (Mahrouche, IEOM 2015) — treat these numbers as second-hand.
- **Open access:** no (primary); the secondary description is open.
- **What it is:** A commercial-intent mobile patient-lift robot: humanoid upper torso with two lifting arms on a modified Segway holonomic base, nurse-teleoperated, intended to remove manual patient handling from nursing work.
- **Hard numbers:** Lift capacity **up to 500 lb (≈227 kg)**, quoted elsewhere as 150 lb one arm / 250 lb two arms / up to 500 lb; actuation is a **Worm Series Elastic Actuator (WSEA)** — one large WSEA per arm provides primary shoulder lift, three small WSEAs form lower shoulder and elbow; **small WSEA torque capacity 678 N·m**, ~50% of the large unit's strength; lower arm is the patient contact surface and is specified for "increased force-fidelity and physical compliance"; also rated for transporting items **< 5 kg**. Total DOF, mass, reach: **not published in the sources I fetched**. Certification: **not published**. Patient testing: **not published / not verified**.
- **Design relevance:** RoNA is the proof that **series elasticity scales to patient-lift torques** — 678 N·m in a compact worm-SEA module. It is also the only system here that explicitly partitions the arm into a high-force proximal segment and a compliant, high-fidelity distal segment that touches the patient. That proximal-stiff / distal-compliant split is a directly copyable architecture and it is the right answer to "how do you get force and safety in the same arm."
- **Evidence grade:** C–B, **I** (industry prototype; no peer-reviewed patient trial found).
- **Where it fell short:** HStar Technologies never brought RoNA to market; there is no published clinical trial, no certification, and no independent verification of the 500 lb figure. Specs circulate only via the company and via secondary summaries — a warning about the reliability of industry numbers generally.

### Blower-powered soft inflatable joints / inflatable hugging robot — Niiyama, Seong, Kawahara & Kuniyoshi, 2021 (Univ. of Tokyo)

- **Citation:** Niiyama, R., Seong, Y., Kawahara, Y., & Kuniyoshi, Y. (2021). Blower-Powered Soft Inflatable Joints for Physical Human-Robot Interaction. *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, 8, 720683.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.3389/frobt.2021.720683 — **fetched** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2021.720683/full
- **Open access:** yes (CC BY 4.0).
- **What it is:** Very-low-pressure, blower-inflated fabric joints with internal tendon drive, built specifically for physical human–robot interaction, demonstrated on an **inflatable hugging robot**.
- **Hard numbers:** Operating pressure **50–300 Pa** (two orders of magnitude below King Louie); fabric ≈ **70 g/m² nylon**; range of motion **45–90°** depending on internal patch configuration; joint torque "proportional to internal pressure and to the square of the radius"; prototypes from **100 mm to 3 m**; demonstrated **5+ independent actuators** in one system; contact detection from a **single internal pressure sensor**; four contact patterns tested (strong hug, light hug, thumping, handshake). Mass, payload, assistive force: **not published**. Certification: none. Patient testing: **none**.
- **Design relevance:** This is the honest ceiling-setter for "how soft can you go." At 50–300 Pa the structure is intrinsically incapable of hurting anyone and equally incapable of assisting anyone mechanically — the torque scaling law (∝ P·r²) tells the team exactly which knob to turn: if pressure must stay low for safety, **diameter is the only remaining lever**, and that is a torso-scale design decision, not an actuator-selection decision. Copy the single-internal-pressure-sensor contact detection. Copy the continuous-blower architecture (a puncture leaks instead of exploding).
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Where it fell short:** Authors state fabric hysteresis limits precision, buckling constrains maximum deflection, and scaling to larger systems needs further investigation. No force output, no payload, no human-subject protocol beyond contact-pattern demos.

### Soft robotic hand and arm system — Alspach, Kim & Yamane, 2018 (Disney Research)

- **Citation:** Alspach, A., Kim, J., & Yamane, K. (2018). Design and fabrication of a soft robotic hand and arm system. In *2018 IEEE International Conference on Soft Robotics (RoboSoft)*, 369–375.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/ROBOSOFT.2018.8404947 (Crossref-verified) — **fetched** and text-extracted the Disney-hosted PDF (https://studios.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Design-and-Fabrication-of-a-Soft-Robotic-Hand-and-Arm-System.pdf).
- **Open access:** partial (publisher-hosted author PDF free).
- **What it is:** A **6-DOF conventionally-actuated arm** wrapped in air-filled force-sensing modules and an inflated outer cover, with a 3D-printed hand with air-filled force-sensing fingertips. Explicitly "soft skin on a hard robot" rather than a soft robot.
- **Hard numbers:** **6 DOF** arm; **two air-filled force sensing modules** (heat-sealed polyurethane) on two links, each tubed to a pressure sensor for contact force feedback and passive impact absorption; inflated **polyethylene outer cover**, heat-sealed, with elastic relief features; internal projector displays through the translucent cover; arm dimensions given in the paper's kinematic figure (links of 214.6 mm and 373.15 mm, overall envelope 457.2 mm). Mass, payload, force range, reach: **not published**. Certification: none. Patient testing: **none** (validated by grasping a soft object and by bending the arm inside the cover).
- **Design relevance:** The paper's own framing is the most useful thing in it: they cite King Louie, Grub, Sanan's inflatable manipulator and Marchese's soft continuum manipulator and then deliberately reject soft actuation because it "requires complex control schemes," choosing instead to outfit a hard robot with soft, easy-to-integrate sensing. That is the mainstream engineering position this project is arguing against, and the team should be able to answer it. Also note their earlier 3D-printed skin modules failed because the rubber-like material was porous and could not hold pressure for extended periods — a direct materials warning for any inflated torso.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Where it fell short:** No force numbers, no payload, no human trial; the soft layer is cosmetic-plus-sensing, not load-bearing. Disney did not carry it into a product.

### GummiArm — Stoelen, de Azambuja, López Rodríguez, Bonsignorio & Cangelosi, 2022

- **Citation:** Stoelen, M. F., de Azambuja, R., López Rodríguez, B., Bonsignorio, F., & Cangelosi, A. (2022). The GummiArm Project: A Replicable and Variable-Stiffness Robot Arm for Experiments on Embodied AI. *Frontiers in Neurorobotics*, 16, 836772.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.3389/fnbot.2022.836772 — **fetched** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2022.836772/full
- **Open access:** yes (CC BY; hardware CC BY-SA at github.com/GummiArmCE).
- **What it is:** An open-source, 3D-printed, variable-stiffness arm driven by antagonistic composite rubber/nylon tendons — the open-source arm with the most variable-stiffness joints.
- **Hard numbers:** **7+1 DOF**, **5 variable-stiffness antagonist joints**, scaled to 50th-percentile female anthropometry (upper arm ~300 mm, forearm+hand ~400 mm); **reach 0.7 m**; **total arm mass 3 kg** (v1.0 PLA), 1.1 kg below the shoulder; **payload 0.75 kg at rest pose, 0.45 kg at full extension**; tendons = Filaflex elastomer core twined with nylon thread, stiffness rising exponentially with elongation (R² = 0.94); stiffness modulated 0–100% co-contraction in real time; **material cost < $6,000** (~90% servos), structural plastic ~$20; ~77 h print time on a hobby FFF printer. Certification: none. Human testing: public exhibit 2016–2017 with **hundreds of handshakes per day** from random members of the public; 2 kg mass dropped from 1.3 m caused no structural damage; across five arms over six years **no Dynamixel servo was replaced due to mechanical failure**.
- **Design relevance:** The single most useful number in this document for scoping: a well-engineered compliant anthropomorphic arm of realistic human scale carries **0.45 kg at full extension**. That is **~4.4 N**. Any assistive-force claim the team makes has to live between that and RIBA's ~600 N, and the team should decide now which end they are on. Copy: composite viscoelastic tendons (damping without metal springs, repairable in minutes), the co-contraction stiffness knob, and the "hundreds of uncontrolled handshakes" as a cheap, publishable safety-exposure protocol.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Where it fell short:** Authors are candid: payload "significantly lower than industrial manipulators" and insufficient for heavy manipulation; kinematic model degrades under load because the plastic flexes, so high gripper accuracy needs visual servoing; Dynamixel load sensing is slow and inaccurate; **composite tendons fail at their attachment points** and elastomer creep is expected with no long-term wear analysis done.

### PR2 as a robotic body surrogate — Grice & Kemp, 2019 (Georgia Tech)

- **Citation:** Grice, P. M., & Kemp, C. C. (2019). In-home and remote use of robotic body surrogates by people with profound motor deficits. *PLOS ONE*, 14(3), e0212904.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1371/journal.pone.0212904 — **fetched** https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0212904
- **Open access:** yes.
- **What it is:** A web-based interface letting people with profound motor deficits operate a PR2 to perform self-care (feeding, face wiping, shaving, applying lotion) and household tasks, including a week-long in-home deployment.
- **Hard numbers:** PR2 — omnidirectional base, vertically translating torso, **two 7-DOF arms** with grippers, pan/tilt head; **20 controllable DOF** through the interface (9 DOF during ARAT testing). Patient testing: **n = 15** participants with profound motor deficits (ARAT ≤ 9 both limbs; SMA n=6, MD n=3, SCI n=3, ALS n=1, arthrogryposis n=1, Dejerine-Sottas n=1), mean age 36.9 ± 8.7, operating remotely from across the US; plus **n = 1** expert user (Henry Evans, quadriplegia from brain-stem stroke) for a **7-day in-home deployment**, 22 h 30 min 50 s of use. 80% success on the simulated self-care task; mean 4:29 ± 1:55 per ARAT item vs ~5 s for able-bodied. Safety: dense foam padding around grippers and base, **fabric-based tactile sensing skin on arms and base**, adjusted gains for back-drivable arms, low-velocity motions, emergency run-stops with trained caregivers. Assistive force: **not published** (task is manipulation, not load bearing). Certification: **not published**.
- **Design relevance:** The gold standard for how to run patient testing on a contact robot, and the source of a safety recipe the team can copy wholesale: foam + tactile skin + back-drivable low-gain arms + low velocity + a caregiver-held run-stop. Also the honest recruitment arithmetic: 37 prescreened → 15 completed.
- **Evidence grade:** **A**.
- **Where it fell short:** The authors' own verdict: "The specific robotic hardware we used in our study is not sufficiently robust for longer-term use, nor is it cost-effective for consumers." The PR2 line is dead; Willow Garage closed in 2014; Kemp's response was to co-found Hello Robot around a far simpler, cheaper single-arm platform. Depth perception through the interface remained a hard problem, and participants could not grasp a 1 cm marble.

### HOBBIT PT2 — Bajones, Fischinger, Weiss, Vincze et al., 2018/2019 (TU Wien et al.)

- **Citation:** Bajones, M., Fischinger, D., Weiss, A., Wolf, D., Vincze, M., de la Puente, P., Körtner, T., Weninger, M., Papoutsakis, K., Michel, D., Qammaz, A., Panteleris, P., Foukarakis, M., & Adami, I. (2018). Hobbit: Providing Fall Detection and Prevention for the Elderly in the Real World. *Journal of Robotics*, 2018, 1754657. Companion field-trial paper: Bajones et al. (2019), *ACM Transactions on Human-Robot Interaction*, 9(2), 1–27, doi:10.1145/3368554. Earlier prototype: Fischinger, D., et al. (2016), *Robotics and Autonomous Systems*, 75, 60–78, doi:10.1016/j.robot.2014.09.029.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1155/2018/1754657 (Crossref-verified). Hardware and trial numbers **fetched** from the open companion chapter: https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/56864 (Vincze et al., doi:10.5772/intechopen.70453). Wiley and ACM HTML both returned 403.
- **Open access:** partial (Journal of Robotics is OA but blocked automated fetch; the IntechOpen chapter is OA and fetchable).
- **What it is:** A deliberately **low-cost** mobile service robot with a single arm for elderly fall prevention — picking objects off the floor so the user does not have to bend down — plus fall detection and emergency call. Multi-week autonomous deployment in real private homes.
- **Hard numbers:** **Single arm** (users asked for two; one was built for cost reasons); **height 125 cm**, max width **56 cm at the shoulder**; RGB-D head camera (ASUS Xtion) plus a floor-parallel depth camera, pan/tilt active head; **safety edges/bumpers at the base and tablet mount**; **hardware BOM ≈ €16,000**, described as "more than a magnitude cheaper than other similar robots" and still unaffordable for the target users. Arm DOF, payload, mass: **not published in the fetched source**. Certification: **not published**. Patient testing: **18 primary users aged 75+** across Austria (7), Greece (4), Sweden (7), **3 weeks per household**, **372 days** total autonomous deployment; 16 secondary users.
- **Design relevance:** The most brutally useful datum in the pass: **object pickup worked reliably on only 18% of the 372 deployment days**, while navigation ran at >98%. Manipulation in an unstructured home is where these systems die. If this project's assistive function depends on repeatable physical contact with a person in an uncontrolled environment, HOBBIT is the reliability baseline to beat, and 18% is the number to put in the design review.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (multi-week deployment with real elderly users in their own homes).
- **Where it fell short:** Reliability, not acceptance. Speech and gesture control underperformed; object pickup was unusable most of the time; the €16k BOM was still too expensive for the users it targeted; no commercial product resulted and the authors concede "considerable work ahead."

### Care-O-bot 3 / Care-O-bot 4 — Reiser et al., 2009 / Fraunhofer IPA — *industry + research*

- **Citation:** Reiser, U., Connette, C., Fischer, J., Kubacki, J., Bubeck, A., Weisshardt, F., Jacobs, T., Parlitz, C., Hägele, M., & Verl, A. (2009). Care-O-bot® 3 — creating a product vision for service robot applications by integrating design and technology. In *2009 IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS)*, 1992–1998.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/IROS.2009.5354526 (Crossref-verified). Care-O-bot 4 specs **fetched** from the official technical-data page: https://www.care-o-bot.de/en/care-o-bot-4/technical-data.html
- **Open access:** no (primary paper); vendor spec page open.
- **What it is:** Fraunhofer IPA's flagship service/care robot line. Care-O-bot 4 is a modular mobile humanoid (base, torso, arms, sensor ring, head) with patented spherical neck and hip joints, buildable with zero, one, or two arms.
- **Hard numbers (Care-O-bot 4, from the vendor technical data page):** **29 DOF**; arm **7 DOF**, reach **≈ 90 cm**, **payload 5 kg**, built from **Schunk PowerBall modules** (PRL100, ERB145, ERB115); total mass **140 kg**; footprint 72 × 72 × 158 cm; omnidirectional base max **1.1 m/s**; joint max velocity 200°/s; **3 safety laser scanners with a safety PLC**, **2-channel Safe-Torque-Off**, **2-channel wireless emergency stop**; 48 V Li-ion. Torque sensing and explicit compliance control: **not stated on the technical data page**. Certification: no certification number claimed on that page (safety *architecture* is described, not a certificate). Patient testing: **not published / not verified in this pass**.
- **Design relevance:** This is what a professionally engineered, functionally safe care robot's safety architecture looks like, and it is entirely **extrinsic** — scanners, a safety PLC, dual-channel STO, wireless e-stop. Copy the STO + dual-channel e-stop as the baseline the project must at minimum match, because "it's soft" is not a substitute for a functional-safety chain. Note the payload: 5 kg per 7-DOF arm on a 140 kg robot.
- **Evidence grade:** B, **I**.
- **Where it fell short:** Twenty-plus years and four generations without a care-market product. 140 kg of robot for a 5 kg arm payload. It remains a research/demonstration platform sold in small batches (the design is explicitly optimised for "economical small-batch production" in folded sheet metal), not a deployed assistive device.

### Baxter — Rethink Robotics, 2012 — *industry*

- **DOI/URL:** no primary paper cited here. Specs **fetched** from https://robotsguide.com/robots/baxter and https://www.generationrobots.com/en/401514-baxter-robot-research-prototype.html (reseller).
- **Open access:** n/a (industry sources).
- **What it is:** The first mass-market series-elastic-actuator dual-arm cobot designed to work next to people without a cage. The de facto reference design for "intrinsic compliance as the safety mechanism," and the platform HuggieBot's peers were built around.
- **Hard numbers:** **7 DOF per arm, 14 arm DOF, 16 total** (2 in the head); **series elastic actuators with brushless DC motors, metal and plastic gearboxes and a custom spring element**; force sensing derived from the SEAs; **payload 2.2 kg per arm**; **max reach 1210 mm**; mass **75 kg** (excl. pedestal), 139 kg with the optional mobile platform; height 94 cm waist-to-head; five cameras; head sonar array for human detection; IP50; price **$22,000** new (reseller listing €10,000 refurbished, from €44,928 list). Certification: "does not need to be isolated in a cage" is a vendor claim; **no certification number verified**. Patient testing: none.
- **Design relevance:** The benchmark for the compliance-vs-payload trade at human scale: 2.2 kg (~22 N) per arm from a 75 kg machine with purpose-built SEAs. If a soft arm is going to beat that ratio it needs an argument, not an aspiration. Also note the well-documented mode split — 2.3 kg in safety mode vs 25 kg with safety disabled — which is the clearest illustration available that "assistive force" and "safe force" are two different numbers on the same hardware.
- **Evidence grade:** B, **I**.
- **Where it fell short:** **Rethink Robotics shut down in 2018**; patents and trademarks went to Germany's Hahn Group; Baxter is discontinued. The company that best executed intrinsic compliance as a product still failed commercially — the cited reason across the industry was that SEA compliance cost accuracy and speed that industrial buyers would not give up.

### DLR LWR III → KUKA LBR iiwa — Albu-Schäffer, Haddadin, Ott, Stemmer, Wimböck & Hirzinger, 2007 — *research → industry*

- **Citation:** Albu-Schäffer, A., Haddadin, S., Ott, C., Stemmer, A., Wimböck, T., & Hirzinger, G. (2007). The DLR lightweight robot: design and control concepts for robots in human environments. *Industrial Robot: An International Journal*, 34(5), 376–385.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1108/01439910710774386 (Crossref-verified). Specs **fetched** from https://www.dlr.de/en/rm/research/robotic-systems/arms/lwr-iii and https://www.kuka.com/en-de/products/robot-systems/industrial-robots/lbr-iiwa
- **Open access:** no.
- **What it is:** The torque-sensing, impedance-controlled lightweight arm that defined physical human–robot interaction, and its commercial descendant, the first series-produced HRC-capable industrial robot.
- **Hard numbers (LWR III, DLR page):** **7 DOF** (R-P-R-P-R-P-P); **mass 14 kg**, **payload 14 kg** (1:1 ratio); **max reach 936 mm**; **completely torque controlled**, joint control at 3 kHz; position / torque / impedance control modes; joint torque sensors on the Harmonic Drive flex spline. **(LBR iiwa, KUKA page):** two variants, **7 kg payload (R800)** and **14 kg payload (R820)**, reach **800–820 mm**, 7 axes, **joint torque sensors in all seven axes**, axis-specific torque accuracy **±2% of maximum torque**, IP54, described by KUKA as "the world's first series-produced sensitive, and therefore HRC-compatible, robot." Certification: **no ISO 10218 / ISO/TS 15066 number appears on the fetched page** — the HRC claim is made in prose. Patient testing: none.
- **Design relevance:** The reference point for *active* compliance done properly: measure joint torque, close an impedance loop, get compliance you can retune in software. The key comparison for the team is that a rigid 14 kg arm with torque sensors carries 14 kg, while a 3 kg compliant tendon arm carries 0.45 kg. Intrinsic softness is not free — it costs roughly two orders of magnitude of payload-to-mass ratio, and the design review needs to state what is bought with that.
- **Evidence grade:** B, **I** (iiwa).
- **Where it fell short:** For this project's purposes: no soft surface at all, no patient application, and the price/complexity of a torque-sensed harmonic-drive joint is out of reach for a senior design build. It also never entered care robotics — its market was industrial HRC.

### COMAN and WALK-MAN — Tsagarakis et al., 2013 / 2017 (IIT)

- **Citation:** Tsagarakis, N. G., Morfey, S., Medrano Cerda, G., Zhibin, L., & Caldwell, D. G. (2013). COMpliant huMANoid COMAN: Optimal joint stiffness tuning for modal frequency control. In *2013 IEEE ICRA*, 673–678. — and — Tsagarakis, N. G., Caldwell, D. G., Negrello, F., Choi, W., Baccelliere, L., Loc, V. G., Noorden, J., Muratore, L., Margan, A., Cardellino, A., Natale, L., & Mingo Hoffman, E. (2017). WALK-MAN: A High-Performance Humanoid Platform for Realistic Environments. *Journal of Field Robotics*, 34(7), 1225–1259.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/ICRA.2013.6630645 and 10.1002/rob.21702 (both Crossref-verified). COMAN specs **fetched** from the IIT lecture slides https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/Humanoid_Soccer_School/slides/HSS_13_Lecture_Tsagarakis.pdf (Tsagarakis, Humanoid Soccer School 2013, text-extracted). WALK-MAN specs **fetched** from https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/798389 (IIT press release for the 2017 revision). Wiley returned 403.
- **Open access:** no (both papers); slides and press release open.
- **What it is:** IIT's two generations of intrinsically compliant, series-elastic full humanoids — COMAN as the passive-compliance research platform, WALK-MAN as the disaster-response humanoid that operated in a post-earthquake scenario.
- **Hard numbers — COMAN (from the IIT slides):** **25 major DOF** (arms, legs, torso; excluding hands and neck/head); **intrinsic passive compliance** plus joint torque sensing and active compliance; SEA at the sagittal leg joints, torso pitch and yaw, and **shoulder flex/ext, shoulder abd/add and elbow flex/ext**; **arm = 3-DOF serial shoulder + 1-DOF elbow**; **3-DOF serial torso mechanism**; no cable transmissions; 2 × 6-DOF F/T sensors, IMU, battery, full body covers with internal wiring. COMAN height and total mass: **widely quoted as 0.95 m / 31 kg but I could not confirm either from a source I fetched — treat as unverified.** **WALK-MAN (2017 revision, IIT release):** height **1.85 m**, mass **102 kg** (down from 133 kg), shoulder width 62 cm, torso depth 31 cm; **32 motors and control boards**; **elastic joints**; **payload 10 kg per arm**, sustained for 10+ minutes (up from 7 kg per arm); 4 F/T sensors at hands and feet, 2 accelerometers; 1 kWh battery ≈ 2 h; Ergal 60% / magnesium alloy 25% / titanium, steel, plastics. The JFR paper's original WALK-MAN is 1.915 m / 132 kg. Certification: none. Patient testing: **none**.
- **Design relevance:** COMAN's arm topology (3-DOF serial shoulder + 1-DOF elbow, with SEA at shoulder flex/ext, abd/add and elbow) is a proven minimal compliant-arm kinematic that a student team can actually build, and IIT's own slides admit the honest part: **"the tuning of the series passive elasticity still remains an experimental trial and error process and very little information on the methodologies used is available."** That is permission to tune empirically and a warning not to promise an optimal stiffness derivation. WALK-MAN's 10 kg/arm at 102 kg total is the current best-in-class payload-per-mass for an intrinsically compliant humanoid.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Where it fell short:** Disaster response, not care. No human-contact application, no patient testing, no certification, and both are unique research machines with no cost figure and no path to a care market. WALK-MAN's mass reduction from 133 → 102 kg to get from 7 → 10 kg per arm shows how expensive incremental force is on a compliant humanoid.

### Harvard soft inflatable shoulder exosuit — Zhou, Hohimer, Young et al., 2024 (Harvard Biodesign) — *competing approach*

## Where the actual gap is

**A freestanding soft humanoid upper body is not unoccupied ground. It has been built, at least three times, by people with more resources than this team.**

The nearest neighbour is **HuggieBot 2.0/3.0** (Block & Kuchenbecker). It is freestanding, human-sized, has a torso *and two arms*, its torso is a custom inflated soft chamber that doubles as a contact sensor, its arms are foam-padded and torque-limited, and it has absorbed uncontrolled full-body physical contact from 48 human subjects. That is the proposed architecture, already built and published in a top HRI venue. The second neighbour is **King Louie** — a 14-DOF *fully* inflatable fabric humanoid with two arms and a torso joint, i.e. softer than anything this project is likely to build, published at Humanoids in 2015. The third is **Niiyama's** blower-powered inflatable hugging robot. Any claim of the form "the first soft/compliant freestanding humanoid upper body for human contact" is false and a reviewer will find all three in one search.

What is genuinely absent is narrower and less flattering: **nobody has published a freestanding compliant upper body that delivers a *quantified, clinically framed assistive force* to elderly or rehabilitation patients.** Split the table and the reason is visible:

- Systems that deliver real assistive force to people — RIBA (61 kg), RIBA-II (80 kg), RoNA (claimed 227 kg) — are **rigid, 140–230 kg, extrinsically padded, and every one of them is dead.**
- Systems that are genuinely soft or intrinsically compliant — King Louie, GummiArm, Niiyama, HuggieBot, Disney — report **no assistive force at all**, and where a payload is published it is **0.45 kg (≈4.4 N)**.
- Systems that deliver measured assistance to actual patients are **worn, not freestanding**: 6.6 N·m and 150 g per limb, with clinical outcome measures, in *Science Robotics* and *Science Translational Medicine*.

So the gap is real but it is a **hole shaped like a physics problem, not an oversight**. It is empty because a load path that goes through a compliant structure to the floor costs one to two orders of magnitude of force capability, and the field's response was to move the load path onto the patient's own skeleton (exosuits) or to give up on softness (RIBA). If the team enters that gap, the novelty claim must be about **the quantification and the force envelope**, not about being first to think of a soft torso.

The defensible claim, roughly: *a freestanding compliant dual-arm upper body with a characterised and instrumented contact-force envelope for a specified low-force assistive task* — steadying, guiding, positioning, postural support — *with the force measured rather than asserted.* Everything in this table either asserts force or never measures it. Measuring it honestly, with a protocol, is the contribution that is actually available.

## Verdict for the design team

1. Do not claim a freestanding soft humanoid upper body as novel — HuggieBot, King Louie and Niiyama's hugging robot each occupy that ground, and the first has 48 human subjects behind it.
2. Pick your force number before you pick your actuator, because the table is bimodal: soft arms sit at ~4 N and patient-lifting arms sit at ~600–785 N, and nothing at all lives in between.
3. If the target task is patient transfer or sit-to-stand, the soft approach is already refuted by every data point here; choose steadying, guidance, positioning or postural support, and say so in the problem statement.
4. Answer the exosuit question in the first five minutes of the design review — 6.6 N·m at 150 g per limb with clinical outcomes is the competing solution to your stated problem, and "a freestanding torso is better because…" needs a real sentence, not a gesture at generality.
5. Copy the proven safety recipe instead of inventing one: foam plus tactile skin plus back-drivable low-gain arms plus low velocity plus a caregiver-held run-stop (Grice & Kemp), and add dual-channel STO and a wireless e-stop (Care-O-bot 4) because softness is not a functional-safety argument.
6. Budget for the failure modes the literature already names: inflatable seams and valves leaking (HuggieBot), bladder pinching that eats a third of your pressure range (King Louie), tendons failing at their attachment points and elastomer creep (GummiArm), and porous elastomer that will not hold pressure over time (Disney).
7. Treat HOBBIT's 18% as the reliability bar — object manipulation worked on 18% of 372 deployment days while navigation ran above 98% — and design your demonstration so it does not depend on the thing that historically fails.
8. Every commercial system in this table that tried this market is discontinued: Rethink 2018, Pepper 2021, RIKEN-SRK 2015, RoNA never shipped, HOBBIT never shipped, Willow Garage 2014. That is not a reason to stop, but any slide claiming a market opportunity has to explain why those six died.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **Robot-assisted dressing, feeding and bedside care** (Kapusta, Erickson, Clever, Bhattacharjee, Kemp): a whole literature on *low-force* physical assistance with real users, e.g. Kapusta et al. 2019 *PLOS ONE* 14(10):e0221854 (robotic bed + mobile manipulator) and 2019 *Autonomous Robots* 43(8):2183–2207 (robot-assisted dressing). This is probably a closer task match for an achievable senior-design assistive function than patient transfer, and it is not in my assignment.
- **Tactile skins for whole-arm contact during assistance** — Grice et al., "Whole-arm tactile sensing for beneficial and acceptable contact during robotic assistance," ICORR 2013, doi:10.1109/ICORR.2013.6650464. Directly relevant to sensing a soft torso, belongs to a sensing topic rather than prior art.
- **Affective touch and hugging as a care function** — Block & Kuchenbecker 2019 *Int. J. - **Safety standards** — ISO 13482 (personal care robots), ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 (collaborative industrial robots) are the relevant certification frameworks, but **none of the vendor or paper pages I fetched cited a certification number against any of them**, including Care-O-bot 4 and KUKA iiwa. - **Soft actuator and continuum-manipulator reviews** that set force/pressure design envelopes: Xavier et al. 2022 *IEEE Access* (soft pneumatic actuators, doi:10.1109/ACCESS.2022.3179589); Thuruthel et al. 2018 *Soft Robotics* 5(2):149–163 (control of soft manipulators); da Veiga et al. 2020 *Prog. - **Newer patient-transfer work** — a dual-arm repositioning-care system using deep predictive learning (arXiv 2407.13376) and CART-MPC for coordinating assistive transfer devices (arXiv 2501.11149) both surfaced; the transfer problem is being attacked again in 2024–25 with learning-based control rather than new mechanics.
