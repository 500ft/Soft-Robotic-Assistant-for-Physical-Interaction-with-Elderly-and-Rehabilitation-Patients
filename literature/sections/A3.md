# A3 — Pneumatic artificial muscles (PAMs) and fluidic actuators at human scale

**Decision this informs:** Are McKibben-type PAMs a viable arm actuator for a freestanding compliant soft-robotic upper body, and what is the real cost in bandwidth, hysteresis, controllability, compressor noise, and plumbing?

**Method note:** 13 distinct web searches (Google Scholar/arXiv/IEEE/Soft Robotics/JNER/PMC/MDPI/Frontiers routes). Every entry below was verified by fetching either the Crossref DOI record (`api.crossref.org/works/<doi>`), the Semantic Scholar record, Europe PMC full text, the publisher page, or the arXiv PDF. Where a number could not be pulled from a source I actually opened, it is labelled as such rather than asserted. Several publishers (MDPI, ScienceDirect, Springer, science.org, DTIC) returned 403 to automated fetches; for those I verified bibliographic identity via Crossref + Semantic Scholar abstract and say so explicitly.

Ranked most → least useful for this project.

### McKibben design review — Kortman, Jovanova, Nabae, Endo, Suzumori & Sakes, 2026
- **Citation:** V. G. Kortman, J. Jovanova, H. Nabae, G. Endo, K. Suzumori, A. Sakes, "Designing McKibben muscles: a critical review for practical implementation," *Bioinspiration & Biomimetics*, vol. 21, no. 3, art. 031002, 2026.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-3190/ae66c0 — **fetched:** Crossref record + IOPscience article page (full text readable).
- **Open access:** yes (CC BY).
- **What it shows:** Systematic review of 146 Scopus-indexed McKibben papers, organised as a *design* reference: bladder/sleeve choices, bundling topologies (serial, parallel, braided, convergent, pennate), and the performance envelope you actually get.
- **Hard numbers (from the article page I fetched):** axial strain 20–35% typical, 30–35% for best designs; braided configurations reach contraction ratio 0.34–0.41; smallest unit cited 1.5 mm dia. at 15% strain and 350 mm/s; largest hydraulic unit 40 mm dia. at up to 28 kN. **Typical driving frequency in the literature clusters around 0.3 Hz; above ~1 Hz the muscle cannot fully inflate/deflate.** Bandwidth improvable by up to 400% by cutting internal dead volume. Tension intensity 88–144 N/cm² (biological muscle 35 N/cm²); energy efficiency 0.32–0.49 (muscle 0.2–0.25); work density 0.13–0.20 J/cm³; peak power density 2.65 W/cm³ (muscle 0.70 W/cm³); one optimised inelastic-bladder design ~80% efficiency. Hysteresis grows with driving frequency from bladder viscoelasticity. Latex bladder fatigue life ~24× silicone at large strain; leakage is the common failure mode. Review states no comprehensive untethered-supply solution is established.
- **Design relevance:** This is the single most useful document for A3. It sets the honest bandwidth ceiling (~0.3–1 Hz for a normal build), gives the force-density argument *for* PAMs, and names the failure mode (leaks) and the unsolved problem (untethered supply). Use its strain/pressure envelope directly in the actuator trade table.
- **Evidence grade:** D (review), but the highest-quality D available.
- **Limitations:** A review of unit actuators, not of loaded joints. Nothing here tells you what happens when a 70 kg person leans on the arm and the muscle is back-driven; the quoted efficiency numbers are actuator-level, not system-level including compressor.

### Foundational McKibben model — Chou & Hannaford, 1996
- **Citation:** C.-P. Chou and B. Hannaford, "Measurement and modeling of McKibben pneumatic artificial muscles," *IEEE Transactions on Robotics and Automation*, vol. 12, no. 1, pp. 90–102, 1996.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/70.481753 — **fetched:** Crossref record (title, authors, venue, vol/iss/pages, year all confirmed) and the Semantic Scholar record (1554 citations).
- **Open access:** no. (A DTIC technical-report version exists at apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA299458.pdf but returned HTTP 403 to my fetch — I could not read it, so I am not quoting numbers from it.)
- **What it shows:** The static geometric force model F = f(P, contraction) for braided-sleeve muscles plus static and dynamic length–tension measurement, and the explicit comparison against human skeletal muscle.
- **Hard numbers:** **Not verified first-hand** — the paywall and the DTIC 403 blocked me. The numbers routinely attributed to this paper (max contraction stress ~0.3 MPa, contraction ratio 25–30%) appear in secondary sources I did not treat as authoritative. The *verified* equivalent figures are the tension-intensity/efficiency/work-density ranges in Kortman et al. 2026 above, which cite this model. Note the paper's well-known qualitative result: PAM force does **not** fall with shortening velocity the way muscle force does.
- **Design relevance:** It is the equation you will actually sizing-calculate with. Cite it for the model; cite Kortman for the numbers.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware characterisation, no humans).
- **Limitations:** Ideal-cylinder assumption, ignores bladder wall thickness and friction — i.e. it deliberately omits the hysteresis that will dominate your control problem. 1996 materials.

### X-PAM — Feng, Yang, Ren, Wei & Gu, 2023
- **Citation:** M. Feng, D. Yang, L. Ren, G. Wei, G. Gu, "X-crossing pneumatic artificial muscles," *Science Advances*, vol. 9, no. 38, 2023.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adi7133 — **fetched:** Crossref record + Semantic Scholar abstract (science.org itself 403'd).
- **Open access:** yes (Science Advances is OA).
- **What it shows:** A non-braided positive-pressure muscle using an X-crossing linkage that breaks the PAM trade-off between contraction ratio, force, and speed.
- **Hard numbers:** contraction ratio 92.9%; output force 207.9 N·kg⁻¹·kPa⁻¹; strain rate 1603.0 %/s; specific power 5.7 kW/kg; work density 842.9 kJ/m³. Demonstrated on a robotic elbow, a jumping robot and a gripper. Explicitly framed as overcoming "the slow actuation of conventional PAMs."
- **Design relevance:** Two uses. (1) If the team wants a fluidic arm that is actually fast, the braided McKibben is not the state of the art. (2) More usefully as a rhetorical baseline in the design review: a *Science Advances* paper exists whose entire premise is that conventional PAMs are too slow. That is the argument against McKibben, from the pro-fluidic camp.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Limitations:** Bench actuators and small demos, not a loaded human-scale arm. The X-crossing mechanism has rigid linkages — it is less intrinsically safe on contact than a soft braided muscle, which cuts against this project's whole premise. No fatigue/durability data at the scale a torso needs.

### Pneumatic upper-limb rehab review — Zhao, Zhang, Cai & Wei, 2026
- **Citation:** Y. Zhao, Y.-M. Zhang, M.-L. Cai, F. Wei, "Advances in Pneumatic Upper-Limb Rehabilitation Robots: A Critical Review of Structural Design, Human–Robot Interaction, and Clinical Translation," *Robotics*, vol. 15, no. 8, art. 159, 2026.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/robotics15080159 — **fetched:** Crossref record + Semantic Scholar abstract (mdpi.com 403'd my fetch, but the DOI and full abstract are confirmed and it is CC BY).
- **Open access:** yes (CC BY).
- **What it shows:** Current-decade survey of pneumatically actuated upper-limb rehab robots, comparing rigid exoskeletons vs. soft wearables vs. rigid–soft hybrids on output capability, motion accuracy, comfort and clinical applicability.
- **Hard numbers:** none extractable without the full text (MDPI blocked). The verified abstract-level findings are qualitative but load-bearing: clinical translation "remains hindered by challenges including actuator hysteresis, nonlinear dynamics, limited accuracy in intention recognition, and inconsistent clinical evaluation metrics"; existing trials are "constrained by small sample sizes, short interventions, and heterogeneous protocols"; the field's conclusion is that **rigid–soft hybrid** architectures are the developmental trend, because rigid gives precision and soft gives safety.
- **Design relevance:** Direct support for a hybrid architecture rather than a purist "all soft" torso — rigid skeleton, compliant actuation. Also the citation to use when a reviewer asks "why isn't everyone doing PAMs already?"
- **Evidence grade:** D.
- **Limitations:** 2026 review of a field where the underlying trials are weak; it inherits their weakness. Wearable exoskeletons, not freestanding torsos — the load path is completely different (their reaction force goes into the user's own body; yours goes into a base).

### PASE soft elbow exosuit, 19 subjects — Sridhar, Erel, Nasirian, Wijesundara & Rahman, 2025
- **Citation:** E. P. Sridhar, V. Erel, A. Nasirian, M. B. J. Wijesundara, M. Rahman, "Design, development, and evaluation of a pneumatically actuated soft wearable robotic elbow exoskeleton for reducing muscle activity and perceived workload," *Journal of Rehabilitation and Assistive Technologies Engineering*, vol. 12, art. 20556683251347517, 2025.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/20556683251347517 — **fetched:** PubMed esummary (PMID 40463354) and PMC full text (PMC12130663).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC).
- **What it shows:** A fabric/silicone pneumatic elbow exosuit measured on humans for EMG reduction and subjective workload across three realistic tasks.
- **Hard numbers:** n = 19 (14 M, 5 F, 18–45). Operating pressure 18 psi (124 kPa) nominal, tested 15–25 psi, max 21 psi (145 kPa). **Max torque 4.39 N·m at 21 psi, 30° elbow angle**; at 18 psi: 3.15 N·m (30°), 1.90 N·m (60°), 1.45 N·m (90°). EMG reduction: biceps 22.4% / triceps 18.2% (static hold), down to 10.2% / 5.2% on the power-tool task; variable-weight test biceps 24.2%. NASA-TLX reduced by 8.9–9.6 points. Device mass 638 g. **Inflation was manual during trials** — no compressor in the loop.
- **Design relevance:** This is the most honest "what does a soft pneumatic joint actually deliver to a human" datapoint in the set. Note the torque: **~1.5–4.4 N·m at the elbow, and it falls by ~55% as the joint extends from 30° to 90°.** If your spec needs to support a leaning adult, a single fluidic actuator of this class is roughly an order of magnitude short, and its torque is strongly configuration-dependent. Also note the authors dodged the supply problem entirely by hand-pumping.
- **Evidence grade:** A (hardware + 19 human subjects, standardised NASA-TLX + EMG).
- **Limitations:** Wearable, so the suit borrows the human skeleton as its structure; a freestanding torso has no such luxury. Healthy young adults, not elderly or impaired. Torque falls off steeply with angle — a leaning patient will load the arm at exactly the extended angles where it is weakest. And the manual inflation means this paper contains *no* evidence about continuous pneumatic supply.

### PAM assist suits in real nursing facilities — Kato et al., 2021
- **Citation:** K. Kato, T. Yoshimi, S. Tsuchimoto, N. Mizuguchi, K. Aimoto, N. Itoh, I. Kondo, "Identification of care tasks for the use of wearable transfer support robots – an observational study at nursing facilities using robots on a daily basis," *BMC Health Services Research*, vol. 21, art. 665, 2021.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-021-06639-2 — **fetched:** Semantic Scholar record (abstract, PMC8256590, PMID 34225718).
- **Open access:** yes (BMC, PMC8256590).
- **What it shows:** Time-motion observation of caregivers at two Japanese nursing facilities that have used PAM-driven Muscle Suit (INNOPHYS) and HAL Lumbar for >24 months as routine equipment.
- **Hard numbers:** two facilities; 1-minute observational time-motion sampling; robots used for direct care in **>70% of transits**, concentrated in transfer assistance and toileting; heaviest use in the morning wake-up block. UMIN000039204. No force/pressure/bandwidth numbers — it is a services-research paper.
- **Design relevance:** The only evidence I found that PAM actuation has *survived contact with an actual care setting over years*. Critically, the Muscle Suit is compressor-free in service (hand pump / gas cartridge). That is the deployment pattern that worked. It is an argument that PAMs are acceptable in elder care **when the air supply is not a running compressor**.
- **Evidence grade:** A (real-world deployment with human data), but soft outcomes.
- **Limitations:** Observational, no control condition, no injury or force data, two sites. It tells you about a worn lumbar assist device, not a freestanding robot that contacts a frail patient. Transfers nothing about actuator dynamics.

### EMG-controlled pneumatic rubber muscle assist suit — Ogawa, Ono, Fukuda, Tsuneyasu & Kurita, 2018
- **Citation:** K. Ogawa, A. Ono, Y. Fukuda, K. Tsuneyasu, Y. Kurita, "Development of a Lightweight Flexible Construction Work Assist Suit Using Pneumatic Rubber Artificial Muscles," in *Proc. 40th Annual International Conference of the IEEE Engineering in Medicine and Biology Society (EMBC)*, 2018.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/EMBC.2018.8513402 — **fetched:** Semantic Scholar record (abstract, PMID 30441325).
- **Open access:** no.
- **What it shows:** A lumbar assist suit with lightweight pneumatic rubber muscles whose assist force is PWM-modulated from spinal-erector EMG and an estimated hip torque; experimentally reduced measured muscle activity during bend/stretch. Also proposes a temple-mounted EMG for intent detection.

### RUPERT + FES with iterative learning control — Tu et al., 2017
- **Citation:** X. Tu, H. Han, J. Huang, J. Li, C. Su, X. Jiang, J. He, "Upper Limb Rehabilitation Robot Powered by PAMs Cooperates with FES Arrays to Realize Reach-to-Grasp Trainings," *Journal of Healthcare Engineering*, vol. 2017, art. 1282934, pp. 1–15, 2017.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/1282934 — **fetched:** Crossref record and Europe PMC full-text XML (PMC5494786).
- **Open access:** yes (CC BY, PMC5494786).
- **What it shows:** RUPERT — a lightweight portable exoskeleton whose joints are **unidirectionally** actuated by PAMs — paired with FES arrays to get the missing return direction, controlled by iterative learning control because neither the PAM nor the stimulated muscle is tractable with a fixed model.
- **Hard numbers:** 3 healthy subjects (ages 32, 34, 29), ethics-approved. Target angles [60°, 45°], 10 s task, RMS tracking error < 2°. The PAM model used is an explicitly **asymmetric three-element model with separate inflation and deflation damping coefficients (B₀ⁱ/B₁ⁱ vs B₀ᵈ/B₁ᵈ)** — i.e. the hysteresis is baked into the plant model as two different systems. EMG leads muscle force by 20–30 ms (their justification for bioelectric intent sensing). No force, pressure or mass figures given.
- **Design relevance:** Two hard lessons. (1) A real PAM arm went **unidirectional** and needed an entirely separate actuation modality (FES) to get the other direction — antagonistic pairs are not free. (2) The team resorted to iterative learning control, which works only for repeated trajectories. A robot a patient leans on unpredictably cannot use ILC. That is a direct warning about PAM controllability under unscripted contact.
- **Evidence grade:** A (human subjects) — but n = 3, healthy, no patients. Treat as A-with-an-asterisk.
- **Limitations:** n = 3 healthy adults, scripted trajectories, treadmill/bench context. Nothing about contact forces or disturbance rejection. Being honest: this is weak evidence dressed as a clinical paper.

### Thin McKibben muscle and multifilament bundles — Kurumaya, Nabae, Endo & Suzumori, 2017
- **Citation:** S. Kurumaya, H. Nabae, G. Endo, K. Suzumori, "Design of thin McKibben muscle and multifilament structure," *Sensors and Actuators A: Physical*, vol. 261, pp. 66–74, 2017.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sna.2017.04.047 — **fetched:** Crossref record (title/authors/venue/vol/pages/year confirmed) + Semantic Scholar record (136 citations).
- **Open access:** no (ScienceDirect 403'd).
- **What it shows:** Sub-2 mm-diameter McKibben muscles that are flexible enough to be woven, and the demonstration that bundling them into "multifilament" muscles gives force that **scales linearly with filament count** while keeping the bundle routable like a tendon.
- **Hard numbers (from sources I fetched, not from the paywalled PDF):** filament diameter ~1.8 mm (2–3 mm variants), durable to 0.5 MPa; a 60-filament demonstration bundle behaved as a linear actuator; output force proportional to filament count. Companion OA paper (Kurumaya, Suzumori, Nabae & Wakimoto, *ROBOMECH Journal* 3:25, 2016, DOI 10.1186/s40648-016-0061-3, Crossref-verified) applies the bundles to a musculoskeletal lower-limb robot — but Springer blocked my full-text fetch so I have no numbers from it.
- **Design relevance:** The most practical route to a *human-scale* PAM arm on a student budget. You size force by adding filaments rather than by making one fat muscle, and the bundle can be routed around a compliant torso structure the way a tendon is. Also directly informs B1: thin muscles can be embedded in fabric/structure rather than bolted on.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Limitations:** Force scales linearly with filaments; so does **air volume, and therefore compressor duty and response time**. 60 filaments is 60 potential leak points and 60 terminations to crimp. No human contact, no safety data. A senior design team will underestimate the fabrication labour here badly.

### Recurrent braiding to raise contraction ratio — Koizumi, Kurumaya, Nabae, Endo & Suzumori, 2020
- **Citation:** S. Koizumi, S. Kurumaya, H. Nabae, G. Endo, K. Suzumori, "Recurrent Braiding of Thin McKibben Muscles to Overcome Their Limitation of Contraction," *Soft Robotics*, vol. 7, no. 2, pp. 251–258, 2020.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1089/soro.2019.0022 — **fetched:** Crossref record + Semantic Scholar full abstract.
- **Open access:** no (abstract verified).
- **What it shows:** Braiding thin McKibben muscles out of thin McKibben muscles, recursively, raises stroke beyond the geometric limit of a single muscle.
- **Hard numbers:** single thin McKibben contraction ratio **28%**; braided once **37%**; braided twice **41%**. Theory and experiment agree; ratio keeps rising with more braiding levels.
- **Design relevance:** This is the number to quote when someone claims PAMs "only give 25%." A shoulder needing large joint excursion can get ~40% stroke from a bundled architecture. Conversely it confirms that a plain McKibben tops out near 28% — which sets your moment-arm and muscle-length geometry, and a long muscle needs a long torso to live in.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Limitations:** Contraction ratio is bought with force and with more air volume per unit stroke; the abstract does not report the force penalty, and I could not read the full text to check. Bench actuators only. Recursive braiding is a serious fabrication skill, not a weekend job.

### Silent electrically driven thin McKibben muscles — Cacucciolo, Nabae, Suzumori & Shea, 2020
- **Citation:** V. Cacucciolo, H. Nabae, K. Suzumori, H. Shea, "Electrically-Driven Soft Fluidic Actuators Combining Stretchable Pumps With Thin McKibben Muscles," *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, vol. 6, art. 146, 2020.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2019.00146 — **fetched:** Crossref record + the Frontiers full-text page.
- **Open access:** yes (CC BY).
- **What it shows:** Pairing a 1 g solid-state electrohydrodynamic stretchable pump with a low-pressure thin McKibben muscle to get a **completely silent, compressor-free, tube-free** fluidic actuator — and quantifies the price.
- **Hard numbers:** muscle blocked force 0.84 N; stroke 4 mm on 180 mm length = **2.2% contraction**; operating pressure 10–20 kPa (vs. standard 100–200 kPa); inner tube ~1 mm dia., 0.15 mm wall; 0.0424 N/kPa. Pump 1 g, 1.5 × 10 mm cross-section, 5–8 kV, ~100 µL/s, ~19 kPa at 7.2 kV. Combined mass 2 g. **Response 10–90%: rise ~14 s, fall ~6.6 s.** Pump efficiency 0.65%; full system efficiency **0.0052%**. Silent operation explicitly claimed.
- **Design relevance:** This is the honest answer to "can we get rid of the compressor?" for the noise constraint. Yes — and the actuator then produces 0.84 N with a 14-second rise time at 0.005% efficiency. The silent-supply route is nowhere near human-scale assistive force. Use this to close off a tempting-but-dead design branch in one slide.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Limitations:** Milli-newton scale, kilovolt drive (a new safety problem next to a patient), 14 s response. Zero transfer to a torso a person leans on, which is precisely why it is worth citing.

### Electro-pneumatic pumps — Diteesawat, Helps, Taghavi & Rossiter, 2021
- **Citation:** R. S. Diteesawat, T. Helps, M. Taghavi, J. Rossiter, "Electro-pneumatic pumps for soft robotics," *Science Robotics*, vol. 6, no. 51, eabc3721, 2021.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/scirobotics.abc3721 — **fetched:** Crossref record + Semantic Scholar abstract; OA author manuscript at research-information.bris.ac.uk.
- **Open access:** partial (accepted author manuscript OA via Bristol; publisher version paywalled).
- **What it shows:** A flexible dielectric-fluid electrostatic-zipping pump, 1.1 mm thick and 5.3 g, that silently supplies pressure locally — framed explicitly against "large, heavy pressure vessels or **noisy, inefficient air pumps**."
- **Hard numbers:** pressure up to 2.34 kPa; volumetric flow up to 161 mL/s; under 0.5 W; thickness 1.1 mm; mass 5.3 g. Drove a soft actuator to 32.40% contraction change at 54.43 %/s. Demonstrated in an antagonistic mechanism, an arm-flexing wearable, and a continuous-pumping system.

- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Limitations:** Pressure two orders of magnitude short of what a load-bearing McKibben needs. High-voltage drive. No human-contact data.

### phloSAR portable supply — Ahlquist, Jitosho, Bao & Okamura, 2024
- **Citation:** M. Ahlquist, R. Jitosho, J. Bao, A. M. Okamura, "phloSAR: a Portable, High-Flow Pressure Supply and Regulator Enabling Untethered Operation of Large Pneumatic Soft Robots," arXiv:2403.01086, 2024.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.01086 — **fetched:** arXiv abstract page and the full PDF (text extracted locally).
- **Open access:** yes (arXiv).
- **What it shows:** A 0.84 kg portable supply built from high-flow proportional valves, a 2 L PET reservoir and a Venturi vacuum generator, quantifying exactly what it costs to cut the air tether on a large soft robot.
- **Hard numbers (read from the PDF):** phloSAR mass 0.84 kg; total with soft robot 1.4 kg. Reservoir: 2 L PET bottle, operating reservoir pressure 690 kPa, burst >900 kPa. Valve flow: EVP 23.5 SLPM, DVP 67.0 SLPM; valve inlet limit 690 kPa. Control error cutoff 1 kPa. Key comparative figure: **micropumps for untethered soft robots cap out around 10 SLPM** and their flow drops as outlet pressure builds; single-use CO₂ cartridges hold ~6000 kPa but freeze the regulator on sustained draw. A reservoir has inherently finite operable duration; refilling it onboard means adding a compressor, which "increases system weight, size, and cost."
- **Design relevance:** This is the plumbing-cost paper. It says in print: for a *large-volume* pneumatic soft robot you either accept a tether to a compressor, or accept a finite-duration reservoir, or carry a compressor. For a freestanding torso in a care room, the tether is the least-bad option and the compressor belongs in the base or in another room. Use the 10 SLPM micropump ceiling to kill the "just use a small quiet pump" suggestion early.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware validated, models verified on hardware).
- **Limitations:** arXiv preprint, no journal peer review confirmed. Built for a 1.5 kg drone payload budget and a vine robot; a torso with several multifilament muscles has far larger volume and duty cycle. No noise measurement, no runtime figure in what I could read.

### Hydraulic vs. pneumatic working fluid — Chambers & Wereley, 2021
- **Citation:** J. M. Chambers and N. M. Wereley, "Influence of hydraulic versus pneumatic working fluids on quasi-static force response of fluidic artificial muscles," *Journal of Intelligent Material Systems and Structures*, vol. 32, no. 3, pp. 385–396, 2021.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1045389X21991927 — **fetched:** Crossref record + Semantic Scholar abstract (marked OA).
- **Open access:** yes per Semantic Scholar (SAGE CHORUS licence).
- **What it shows:** Directly tests whether the McKibben force model is fluid-dependent. Using air or water at precisely matched isobaric conditions, on both large and small actuators, the force–contraction curves were **nearly identical**.
- **Hard numbers:** none beyond "nearly identical isobaric force–contraction response curves" in the verified abstract; the specific force/pressure values are in the full text I did not read. Says explicitly the result extends existing pneumatic models to any practical liquid or gas.
- **Design relevance:** Settles the hydraulic-alternative question cleanly for *sizing*: switching to water buys you **no extra static force** at the same pressure. What hydraulics buys is stiffness, position accuracy and efficiency (secondary sources put hydraulic ~60% vs pneumatic ~25–30%, which I did not verify to a primary source and therefore flag as unconfirmed). What it costs is leaks onto a patient, mass, and a pump that is not obviously quieter. For a one-year senior design project: do not go hydraulic.
- **Evidence grade:** B.
- **Limitations:** Quasi-static only — the whole hydraulic advantage is dynamic, and this paper deliberately does not test it. Bench actuators. Says nothing about safety of a water-filled arm in a care setting.

### McKibben modelling review — Tondu, 2012
- **Citation:** B. Tondu, "Modelling of the McKibben artificial muscle: A review," *Journal of Intelligent Material Systems and Structures*, vol. 23, no. 3, pp. 225–253, 2012.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1045389X11435435 — **fetched:** Crossref record (title/venue/vol/iss/pages/year confirmed); Semantic Scholar shows 438 citations.
- **Open access:** no (SAGE paywall; no abstract exposed by Semantic Scholar either).
- **What it shows:** The standard survey of static and dynamic McKibben models up to 2012, including the widely repeated attribution of PAM hysteresis to **strand-on-strand friction inside the braided weave** (as distinct from bladder viscoelasticity).
- **Hard numbers:** none verified — I could not read past the paywall, and I am not quoting figures from search snippets.
- **Design relevance:** Cite for the *mechanism* of hysteresis when justifying a control approach: friction-dominated hysteresis is rate-independent-ish and amenable to Prandtl–Ishlinskii-style inversion, whereas viscoelastic hysteresis is not. That distinction decides whether feedforward compensation will work on your arm.
- **Evidence grade:** D.
- **Limitations:** 2012, pre-dates thin-muscle and bundled architectures entirely. Review only.

## Verdict for the design team

McKibben PAMs give you the force density and the intrinsic compliance this project is built around — 88–144 N/cm² tension intensity and ~2.65 W/cm³ peak power density beat skeletal muscle, and a bundled multifilament muscle lets you buy force linearly by adding filaments — so on paper they are the right actuator for an arm a person is allowed to lean on. The killer is bandwidth: the reviewed literature clusters around **0.3 Hz driving frequency**, and above roughly 1 Hz a McKibben cannot fully inflate or deflate, which means your arm cannot react to an unexpected contact faster than a person can push it, and your "compliant safety" will come from the rubber, not from the controller. Controllability is worse than the papers make it sound — the one PAM arm here that actually touched humans (RUPERT) went *unidirectional* and imported functional electrical stimulation to get the return stroke, then needed iterative learning control, which only works on repeated trajectories and is therefore useless for a patient leaning at a moment of their own choosing. The force you should plan for is sobering: the best human-subject soft pneumatic elbow in this set delivers 1.45–4.39 N·m and loses over half of it going from 30° to 90°, i.e. it is weakest exactly where a leaning person loads it. Hydraulics is a dead end for you — Chambers & Wereley show the static force is identical for the same pressure, so you would take on leaks, mass and a pump for a dynamic benefit you have no time to exploit. On noise and tethering, be blunt in the design review: silent supplies exist and they are not remotely adequate (2.34 kPa from the Science Robotics electro-pneumatic pump, 0.84 N and a 14-second rise time from the silent thin-McKibben system at 0.005% system efficiency), micropumps cap near 10 SLPM, reservoirs have finite duration, and the only deployment in this set that genuinely survived years in nursing facilities — the Muscle Suit — ducked the problem by not running a compressor at all. So: **yes, PAMs are viable for this arm, on three conditions** — put the compressor outside the care room or in a heavy damped base and run a tether, adopt the rigid–soft hybrid architecture the 2026 rehab review converges on rather than an all-soft torso, and write a spec that never asks the arm to move faster than ~0.5 Hz or to hold torque at full extension. If the team cannot accept a tether, stop now and go to A2 (SEA/VSA), because every untethered fluidic supply in the verified literature is one to two orders of magnitude short of human-scale assistive force. One more warning: a 60-filament bundle is 60 crimped terminations and 60 leak paths, and leakage is the documented common failure mode — budget fabrication and leak-chasing time the way you would budget machining, or the one-year clock will be spent on plumbing rather than on the deflection tests that actually validate the design.
