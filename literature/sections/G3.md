# G3 — Standards and regulatory status: what is actually current, and which rules actually bind this project

**Scope:** verification pass over the standards and regulatory claims made in a new project document, plus the question the document only gestures at — which regulatory path actually applies to an unsold university prototype that physically supports elderly people.

**Verification rule used:** every stage code, date, page count and price below was read off a catalogue page fetched in this pass, and the fetched URL is named in each entry. Nothing was filled in from memory. Anything I could not fetch is marked **UNVERIFIED** rather than guessed.

**Fetch note — read this before trusting the URLs.** `www.iso.org` returns **HTTP 403** to every automated fetch (both the agent fetch tool and `curl` with a browser user-agent, on `/standard/53820.html` and `/standard/71370.html`). Two workarounds were used and are named per entry:
1. **Wayback replay** of the ISO page (`http://web.archive.org/web/<ts>/https://www.iso.org/...`) — this is ISO's own rendered catalogue page, archived. Snapshot timestamps are given.
2. **r.jina.ai text proxy** in front of the live ISO page — this returns the live page, so the content is current as of 2026-09-24.
`webstore.iec.ch` and `www.fda.gov` were fetched directly with no blocking. `www.ecfr.gov` **302-redirects automated fetches to `unblock.federalregister.gov`**, so all CFR text below was taken from Cornell LII (`law.cornell.edu/cfr/...`), which reproduces the eCFR text with its Federal Register amendment history. Also note: general web search was unavailable in this session (search budget exhausted; DuckDuckGo, Mojeek, Startpage, SearXNG and Ecosia all returned CAPTCHA or 403 to `curl`), so every page below was reached by direct URL or by walking ISO's own TC 299 catalogue listing.

### ISO 13482:2014 — ISO, 2014 (the current personal-care-robot standard)

- **Citation:** ISO 13482:2014, *Robots and robotic devices — Safety requirements for personal care robots*. International Organization for Standardization, Geneva. Edition 1, published 2014-02-03. 79 pages. ISO/TC 299, ICS 25.040.30.
- **DOI/URL:** `https://www.iso.org/standard/53820.html` — **403 to automated fetch**. Fetched instead via Wayback replay of ISO's own page: `http://web.archive.org/web/20260915113859/https://www.iso.org/standard/53820.html` (snapshot 2026-09-15).
- **Open access:** no. **CHF 204** for the English PDF+ePub or paper copy, direct from ISO. USD price: **UNVERIFIED** (ANSI Webstore page `https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/iso/iso134822014` was reachable through the text proxy but the price element did not render; the raw ANSI page 403s). University library route: see **Access routes** at the end — NYU's Mechanical Engineering standards guide lists Knovel and ASTM Compass only, so this is probably a purchase, not a library pull.
- **What it shows:** The only published International Standard aimed squarely at robots that physically assist people outside industry. Scope, verbatim from ISO's abstract: *"specifies requirements and guidelines for the inherently safe design, protective measures, and information for use of personal care robots, in particular the following three types of personal care robots: mobile servant robot; physical assistant robot; person carrier robot."* It *"covers human-robot physical contact applications."*
- **Hard numbers:**
  - Stage code **90.92 — "International Standard to be revised"**. **VERIFIED.** Life-cycle record: 60.60 published 2014-02-03; 90.20 systematic review 2019-01-15; **90.93 confirmed 2020-08-21**; **90.92 set 2021-07-23**.
  - Edition 1, publication date 2014-02. **VERIFIED.**
  - 79 pages. **VERIFIED.**
  - CHF 204. **VERIFIED.**
  - Contact force / pressure limits published: **none. VERIFIED**, and ISO says so itself in the abstract: *"Attention is drawn to the fact that for hazards related to impact (e.g. due to a collision) no exhaustive and internationally recognized data (e.g. pain or injury limits) exist at the time of publication of ISO 13482:2014."* This is the cleanest single sentence the project has for the "no published limits" finding — it is ISO's own catalogue text, not commentary.
  - Exclusions, verbatim: does not apply to *"robots travelling faster than 20 km/h"*, *"robot toys"*, *"water-borne robots and flying robots"*, *"industrial robots, which are covered in ISO 10218"*, **"robots as medical devices"**, *"military or public force application robots."* **VERIFIED.**
  - Revision underway: **yes.** ISO's page carries the banner *"Expected to be replaced by ISO/FDIS 13482 within the coming months."* **VERIFIED** (own entry below).
- **Design relevance:** This is the governing document for the build as currently framed — a freestanding robot that physically supports a person is a **physical assistant robot** in ISO 13482's taxonomy, and possibly a mobile servant robot depending on the base. It requires the risk to be eliminated or reduced first by inherently safe design and then by protective measures (the ISO 12100 hierarchy), and it gives the project no numbers to hit. The "robots as medical devices" carve-out is the hinge for item 7 below: what the project *calls* the machine decides which standard claims it.
- **Evidence grade:** A — ISO's own catalogue page, including abstract, stage code and full life-cycle table, read through a Wayback replay of that page.
- **Limitations:** Publishes no biomechanical limits, no per-body-region thresholds, no test methods (those are in ISO/TR 23482-1). It is also, as of 2021-07-23, formally flagged for replacement, and the replacement changes both the title and the scope (below). Citing ISO 13482:2014 as "the current standard" is correct today but has a visible expiry.

  - Edition 2. **VERIFIED.**

Second, **the medical-application exclusion survives the revision** — edition 2 still says it is "not intended to cover robots used in industrial applications and medical applications." The gap the project is arguing around does not close with this revision; it just moves.
- **Evidence grade:** A — ISO's own project page with the full stage history.
- **Limitations:** It is a draft. The FDIS text is not public, so whether edition 2 finally publishes contact limits is **UNVERIFIED** — the abstract does not say. That is worth watching, because if it does, the project's ISO 14971 import argument gets weaker or stronger overnight.

### ISO/TR 23482-1:2020 — ISO, 2020 (test methods)

- **What it shows:** The test-method companion to ISO 13482. Scope, verbatim: *"This document describes methods that can be used to test personal care robots in terms of safety requirements defined in ISO 13482. The target robots of this document are identical to those of ISO 13482."*
- **Hard numbers:**
  - **The title in the new document is wrong.** It is *not* "Safety-related test methods for personal care robots." The registered title is **"Robotics — Application of ISO 13482 — Part 1: Safety-related test methods."** **PARTIAL** — the subtitle is right, the framing is not; this is a Part 1 of an *Application of ISO 13482* series, not a standalone test-methods standard.
  - Stage code **60.60 — "International Standard published"**. **VERIFIED.** No revision project registered; 90.20 / 90.92 / 90.93 rows are all empty.
  - Edition 1, 2020-02-28, 79 pages, CHF 204. **VERIFIED.**
  - Claim "testing parameters follow the robot-specific risk assessment": **VERIFIED**, verbatim — *"The manufacturer determines the required tests and appropriate testing parameters based on a risk assessment of the robot's design and usage. This risk assessment can determine that tests and test parameters other than those contained in this document are acceptable."*
  - Additional, not in the new document: *"Not all test methods are applicable to all robot types. Test methods labelled 'universal' are applicable to all personal care robots. For other tests, the heading states for which robot types the test can be applied (e.g. 'for wearable robot' or 'for mobile robot')."* **VERIFIED.**
- **Design relevance:** This is the document that tells the project *how* to test, and it explicitly hands the choice of test and test parameter back to the manufacturer's own risk assessment. That is a direct structural match to the ISO 14971 import argument the repo already makes: the import is not a workaround, it is the mechanism ISO/TR 23482-1 itself nominates. It also means the project's test plan must be traceable to its risk assessment or it has no standing at all.
- **Evidence grade:** A — ISO's own catalogue page, abstract and life-cycle table, via text proxy over the live page.
- **Limitations:** A **Technical Report**, not a normative standard — it describes methods that "can be used," it does not require them. It publishes no pass/fail thresholds, so it cannot supply the limits ISO 13482 lacks. Its target robots are identical to ISO 13482's, so it inherits the same medical-device exclusion.

## The regulatory path

**Short answer: as an unsold university prototype, this device is almost certainly outside FDA device regulation, and the binding review is the NYU IRB, not FDA — but that holds only until someone runs it on a human subject to determine safety or effectiveness, and it collapses entirely on commercialization.** Four questions, in order.

**1. Is it a "device" at all?** FDA's definition is intent-based, not hardware-based. Per FD&C Act §201(h)(1), as reproduced on FDA's own page (`https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/classify-your-medical-device/how-determine-if-your-product-medical-device`, content current as of 09/29/2022), a device is an article *"intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions, or in the cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease"* or intended to affect body structure or function without chemical action. FDA emphasizes that **intended use and indications for use** are what determine classification. So: a machine described as *"an upper-body assistive robot for elderly and rehabilitation patients"* is stating a therapeutic and structure-or-function intent and **meets the definition of a device**. A machine described as a compliant-manipulator research platform that happens to be tested with human participants does not, on its face. The design report's own words are the determining fact — this is not a technicality, it is the mechanism.

For what it is worth, FDA does treat this class of machine as a device when it is marketed: **21 CFR 890.3480, "Powered lower extremity exoskeleton"** (`https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/890.3480`), classifies an *"external, powered, motorized orthosis that is placed over a person's paralyzed or weakened limbs for medical purposes"* as **Class II with special controls**, and the special controls list is a near-perfect specification of the validation this project would eventually owe: biocompatibility of patient-contacting materials; EMC/EMI, electrical, thermal, mechanical, battery and wireless testing; software verification, validation and hazard analysis; mechanical bench and durability testing; **cyclic-loading simulated-use testing to demonstrate performance of device commands and safeguards under worst-case conditions and after durability testing**; validation of manual override controls; clinical testing; and a training program. There is no upper-body equivalent regulation that I could locate this pass (see unknowns), but 890.3480 is the closest published analogue and is the right thing to design *toward*.

**2. Does FDA regulation attach to an unsold prototype?** Two separate exemptions do the work, and both are conditional.

*Registration and listing:* **21 CFR 807.65(f)** (`https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/807.65`) exempts from establishment registration *"Persons who manufacture, prepare, propagate, compound, or process devices solely for use in research, teaching, or analysis and do not introduce such devices into commercial distribution."* Verbatim. A senior design team building one unit in a university lab and never distributing it fits this squarely. Note the two conjunctive conditions — **solely** for research/teaching, **and** no commercial distribution. Selling one, or handing units to a care facility for routine use, breaks it.

*Clinical investigation:* **21 CFR 812.2(a)** (`https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/812.2`) — *"This part applies to all clinical investigations of devices to determine safety and effectiveness, except as provided in paragraph (c)."* The trigger is **"to determine safety and effectiveness."** Bench testing, dummy-loading, instrumented mannequins and force measurement on a load cell are not clinical investigations and Part 812 never engages. Putting a real elderly person in the machine to find out whether it is safe and whether it works **is** a clinical investigation and Part 812 does engage. None of the 812.2(c) exemptions rescue that: (c)(1) and (c)(2) need pre-1976 or substantially-equivalent commercial distribution; (c)(3) is diagnostic devices; (c)(4) is consumer-preference or modification testing *"if the testing is not for the purpose of determining safety or effectiveness and does not put subjects at risk"* — a machine that physically supports a person's body weight puts subjects at risk on its face; (c)(5) is veterinary; (c)(6) is lab animals; (c)(7) is custom devices.

**3. What the IRB actually does, and why it is the real gate.** Under **21 CFR 812.2(b)**, an investigation of a **nonsignificant risk (NSR)** device is treated as having an approved IDE — no FDA submission — provided the sponsor labels per 812.5, **obtains IRB approval after presenting the reviewing IRB with a brief explanation of why the device is not a significant risk device**, obtains informed consent per 21 CFR 50, monitors per 812.46, and keeps the specified records and reports. If the device is a **significant risk (SR)** device, a full IDE application to FDA is required before any human use.

The SR/NSR line is drawn by **21 CFR 812.3(m)** (`https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/812.3`): a significant risk device is one that is an implant presenting potential for serious risk; is for supporting or sustaining human life and presents potential for serious risk; *"is for a use of substantial importance in diagnosing, curing, mitigating, or treating disease, or otherwise preventing impairment of human health and presents a potential for serious risk to the health, safety, or welfare of a subject"*; or *"otherwise presents a potential for serious risk to the health, safety, or welfare of a subject."* That last catch-all is where a student-built robot supporting a frail elderly person's torso lands if the fall/crush/pinch hazards are not convincingly controlled. **The IRB, not the team, makes the SR/NSR call in the first instance** — FDA's information sheet guidance *Significant Risk and Nonsignificant Risk Medical Device Studies* (January 2006, `https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/significant-risk-and-nonsignificant-risk-medical-device-studies`) exists specifically to explain *"the IRB's responsibilities when making the risk determination for investigational medical devices."* If the IRB calls it SR, the study cannot start without an FDA-approved IDE.

Separately and **regardless of whether FDA is involved at all**, human-subjects review applies. **45 CFR 46.102(e)(1)** (`https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/46.102`) defines a human subject as a living individual about whom an investigator **"(whether professional or student)"** conducting research obtains information through intervention or interaction — and "intervention" expressly *"includes both physical procedures by which information or biospecimens are gathered ... and manipulations of the subject or the subject's environment that are performed for research purposes."* Strapping a participant into a robot and recording what happens is an intervention, the student status of the investigator is explicitly no defense, and NYU's Human Research Protection Program (IRB Registration No. 00000310, FWA #00006386, `https://www.nyu.edu/research/resources-and-support-offices/getting-started-withyourresearch/human-subjects-research.html`) operates under the Common Rule. **Practical consequence: submit to the NYU IRB before the first human trial, not after, and expect the IRB to demand the risk analysis, the hazard controls and an explicit NSR justification as part of the package.** That timeline should be in the project schedule now; IRB review is measured in weeks to months, and it is the single most likely thing to slip the demo date.

**4. What changes on commercialization.** Everything. 807.65(f) evaporates the moment the device enters commercial distribution, so establishment registration and device listing attach. The device needs a classification and a marketing pathway: with no obvious predicate for a freestanding upper-body assistive robot, the realistic route is **De Novo classification**, which is how the powered lower-extremity exoskeleton class at 890.3480 came into existence in the first place — and the special controls in that regulation become the template for what the submission has to demonstrate. A full quality system, design history file, labeling, and post-market surveillance follow. Any pivotal clinical study to support the submission is squarely a Part 812 investigation. And the standards above stop being optional reading: ISO 14971 risk management becomes an expectation, and depending on the intended-use wording, either ISO 13482 (soon ISO/FDIS 13482) or the whole IEC 60601-1 stack plus IEC 80601-2-78 becomes the applicable safety basis.

**One thing the project should do now and probably will not:** FDA's Robotic Medical Devices page states, verbatim, *"The FDA encourages early engagement from device developers working on novel robotic medical devices,"* and points at the **Q-Submission Program** (guidance *Requests for Feedback and Meetings for Medical Device Submissions: The Q-Submission Program*, final, **May 2025**, docket FDA-2018-D-1774). A Pre-Sub costs nothing but preparation time and produces written FDA feedback on intended use, classification and testing plan. Whether academic/non-commercial sponsors are eligible and whether there is any fee: **UNVERIFIED** — the guidance landing page does not say and the full PDF was not fetched this pass.

## Corrections to the claims above

1. **ISO/TR 23482-1:2020 title is wrong.** Claimed *"Safety-related test methods for personal care robots."* Actual registered title: **"Robotics — Application of ISO 13482 — Part 1: Safety-related test methods."** It is a part of an *Application of ISO 13482* series, not a standalone test-methods document. Fix the citation.
2. **ISO/TR 23482-2:2019 title is wrong.** Claimed *"Application guidelines for ISO 13482."* Actual: **"Robotics — Application of ISO 13482 — Part 2: Application guidelines."** Same error pattern. Fix the citation.
3. **"A revision is currently progressing through ISO" is true but far too vague to be useful, and understates how far along it is.** ISO 13482:2014 has carried stage **90.92 ("to be revised") since 2021-07-23**, and the revision is **ISO/FDIS 13482, edition 2, at stage 50.20 with the FDIS ballot initiated 2026-09-15**. That is the last approval stage before publication, not an early-stage effort. Say the stage code.
4. **The new document does not mention that the revision renames and re-scopes the standard.** Edition 2 is titled **"Robotics — Safety requirements for service robots"** — the phrase "personal care robots" disappears from the title, and the document grows from 79 to 160 pages. Any report written in 2026–2027 should name both designations.
5. **The new document does not mention that edition 2 keeps the medical exclusion.** ISO/FDIS 13482's scope states it *"is not intended to cover robots used in industrial applications and medical applications."* The gap the project is arguing around survives the revision. This should be in the limitations section, not omitted.
6. **"IEC 80601-2-78 second edition is under development" is correct, but the stage cannot be stated cleanly.** IEC's own pages say **Stage code CD** (base page) and **ACD** (amendment page), forecast publication **2027-07-26**; ISO's mirror record says **IEC/DIS 80601-2-78**, "within the coming months." CD/ACD and DIS are different stages. Report the conflict, do not pick one.
7. **Page-count conflict on the amendment.** IEC's product page says **35 pages**; ISO's mirror record says **29**. Both primary. Prefer IEC's number and note the discrepancy.
8. **All three FDA claims are VERIFIED verbatim** from `https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/products-and-medical-procedures/robotic-medical-devices` (content current as of **09/24/2026**): *"A robotic medical device is any machine or electrical system that uses robotic technology to improve, assist, or perform tasks in health care, such as surgery, diagnosis, or **rehabilitation**."* / *"The development of devices that are capable of operating at a distance (remote teleoperation) or performing certain tasks autonomously have the potential to further transform patient care."* / *"The FDA encourages early engagement from device developers working on novel robotic medical devices."* No correction needed.
9. **ISO 14971:2019 is current with no amendment** — stage **90.93**, confirmed in **2025**. VERIFIED, no correction needed. Add ISO/TR 24971:2020 (the guidance companion) to the reading list; ISO bundles the two with ISO 10993-1:2025 at CHF 557.
10. **The new document "only gestures at" the regulatory path, and the gesture is the dangerous part.** The operative facts are: the device is exempt from FDA registration under **21 CFR 807.65(f)** *only* while it is built solely for research or teaching and never commercially distributed; **21 CFR 812** engages the moment a human is used to determine safety or effectiveness; and **the NYU IRB decides SR vs NSR**, which decides whether an FDA IDE is needed before anyone gets in the machine. A report that says "not FDA regulated because it isn't sold" without those three conditions is stating a conclusion that is true today and false the first time a participant is strapped in outside IRB approval.
