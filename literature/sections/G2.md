# G2 — Verification pass on a 21-item LLM-generated citation list

**Scope:** every entry below was supplied by an LLM as prior art for a *freestanding compliant soft-robotic upper body (torso + two arms)* for elderly and rehabilitation users. This file records what each citation actually is.

**Verification rule used:** no entry is graded VERIFIED from a search snippet. Each was resolved through Crossref, OpenAlex, Unpaywall, Europe PMC, the arXiv API or the publisher's own metadata, and — where a numeric claim was attached — a full text was fetched and the number read off the page. The fetched source is named in each entry. Numbers I could not read are written **UNVERIFIED**, not repeated.

**Result:** 21/21 papers exist. 0 fabricated. 9 carry metadata errors a reader would otherwise propagate (see *Corrections that matter*). All four flagged numeric claims (1,981/87; n = 12; 112 N vs 18 N·m; 223 ms) were checked against full text; three are correct as stated, one (223 ms) is correct but mis-described.

Verification date: 2026-09-24.

### Baloo — Johnson, Clawson & Killpack, 2024 (preprint)

- **Citation:** Johnson, C. C., Clawson, A., & Killpack, M. D. (2024). *Baloo: A Large-Scale Hybrid Soft Robotic Torso for Whole-Arm Manipulation*. arXiv:2409.08420 (v1 12 Sep 2024; v2 13 Mar 2025). Comments field states "Submitted to IEEE Transactions on Mechatronics for review."
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08420 — **fetched** the v2 PDF (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.08420v2) and read the full text. **No journal or conference version exists as of 2026-09-24**: Crossref and OpenAlex return only the arXiv preprint. Related BYU output: Johnson, C. C. (2025), *Modeling, Control, and Learning for Whole-Body Manipulation With Large-Scale Soft Robots* (BYU ScholarsArchive thesis).
- **Open access:** yes (arXiv).
- **What it shows:** A hybrid rigid-soft robotic **torso** with two 1 m pneumatic arms on a rigid torso and vertical elevator, built to exploit passive structural compliance for whole-arm, contact-rich grasping of large objects.
- **Hard numbers:** end-effector payload **up to 19 kg**; joint actuation chambers run at a regulated **410 kPa** maximum against a **615 kPa** theoretical burst (safety factor 1.5); grasping pressure trajectories capped at **300 kPa** to avoid crushing objects; three joint sizes, masses **1.65 / 2.03 / 4.63 kg** (small/medium/large), each 220 mm high; **24 independently regulated pressures** (12 per arm) commanded at 20 Hz; **30 whole-body grasping trials** (6 objects × 5 trials) at **100% success**; free-response damping ratio of an uncontrolled small joint **ζ ≈ 0.0015**, oscillation period ≈ 0.12 s; comparison claim that Baloo exceeds existing hybrid designs' payload by **≥3.8×**. Object masses spanned ~6.75–21.9 kg. Significant hysteresis in the differential-pressure-to-angle map is reported, and the hysteresis loop shifts after long deformations.
- **Design relevance:** The single closest published system to this project's architecture, and the source for its hardest design numbers: pressure ceiling with an explicit 1.5 safety factor, per-joint mass budget, and the admission that a fully passive soft joint is essentially undamped (ζ = 0.0015) and hysteretic. If the project's torso is to carry a leaning person, Baloo says the achievable class is tens of kilograms per arm at ~4 bar, with open-loop pressure control good enough for grasping but not for position.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware-validated, quantitative, no human subjects — and not peer reviewed).
- **Limitations:** Freestanding but **not assistive**: every experiment grasps inanimate objects, nothing touches a person, and no safety standard is invoked. Payload is measured at the end effector, not as a distributed leaning load on the torso. Treat as an actuator/structure existence proof only, and cite it as a preprint.

### RIBA — Mukai et al., 2010–2011

- **Citation (primary):** Mukai, T., Hirano, S., Nakashima, H., Kato, Y., Sakaida, Y., Guo, S., & Hosoe, S. (2010). Development of a nursing-care assistant robot RIBA that can lift a human in its arms. In *2010 IEEE/RSJ IROS*, 5996–6001.
- **Citation (tactile motion adjustment):** Mukai, T., Hirano, S., Yoshida, M., Nakashima, H., Guo, S., & Hayakawa, Y. (2011). Tactile-based motion adjustment for the nursing-care assistant robot RIBA. In *2011 IEEE ICRA*, 5435–5441.
- **Citation (whole-body contact):** Mukai, T., Hirano, S., Yoshida, M., Nakashima, H., Guo, S., & Hayakawa, Y. (2011). Whole-body contact manipulation using tactile information for the nursing-care assistant robot RIBA. In *2011 IEEE/RSJ IROS*, 2445–2451.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/IROS.2010.5651735 ; 10.1109/ICRA.2011.5979559 ; 10.1109/IROS.2011.6094403 — all three Crossref-verified; abstracts **fetched** from OpenAlex records for each DOI. (A duplicate Crossref stub exists for the 2011 IROS paper under 10.1109/IROS.2011.6048049 with no pages — use 6094403.)
- **Open access:** no (IEEE paywalled); abstracts open.
- **What it shows:** A human-type-armed wheeled care robot that performs **bed↔wheelchair patient transfer**, instructed by a caregiver through tactile "tactile guidance," with distributed whole-arm/torso tactile sensing used both as the command channel and as the contact-regulation feedback.
- **Hard numbers:** 2010 abstract states RIBA "succeeded in transferring a human from a bed to a wheelchair and back" and reports "experiments including the transfer of humans" — **actual human transfer CONFIRMED.** The 2011 ICRA motion-adjustment experiments used a **lifesize dummy**, not a human. Lift capacity in kilograms is **not stated in these three abstracts** (the widely quoted 61 kg for RIBA and 80 kg for RIBA-II come from RIKEN press releases, already recorded in F1 — do not attribute them to these papers).
- **Design relevance:** The force target and the contact philosophy in one system. Two ideas transfer directly: flat, finite-area contact surfaces chosen deliberately to hold contact pressure down, and preserving each contact region's force/pressure pattern through tactile feedback so the arms do not graze the person's skin. Both are requirements for a torso a person leans against.
- **Evidence grade:** A for the 2010 paper (human transfer demonstrated); B for the two 2011 papers (dummy/manipulation method).
- **Limitations:** Rigid arms with urethane-foam covering, 230 kg class machine, never deployed in a care facility (see F1). Transfer risk to a soft torso: RIBA's safety story is sensing plus mass, not intrinsic compliance.

### Whole-arm tactile sensing on PR2 — Grice et al., 2013 *(cited as "Jain et al." — wrong first author)*

- **Citation:** Grice, P. M., Killpack, M. D., Jain, A., Vaish, S., Hawke, J., & Kemp, C. C. (2013). Whole-arm tactile sensing for beneficial and acceptable contact during robotic assistance. In *2013 IEEE 13th International Conference on Rehabilitation Robotics (ICORR)*, 1–8.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/ICORR.2013.6650464 — Crossref-verified; **fetched** the Georgia Tech repository PDF via the DSpace API (https://repository.gatech.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e44f2b90-85ed-475e-a359-e8180f87c3cb/content) and read the full text.
- **Open access:** yes (institutional repository).
- **What it shows:** Deliberately allowing arm-body contact vastly enlarges the set of reachable end-effector poses near a seated person; a PR2 with a whole-arm tactile skin and a contact-regulating controller reaches those poses while keeping contact forces low, and users find it acceptable.
- **Hard numbers:** **two users with motor impairments** (one with quadriplegia, Henry Evans, who used the system in his own bed to grasp and pull a blanket over himself and to grab a cloth and wipe his face) plus a lab study with **eight able-bodied participants** (mean age with SD 8.6; 4 White, 2 Asian, 1 Hispanic, 1 African American; none with prior robot experience; <15 min training). Link that first violates the safety margin: **forearm 35.74%, gripper palm 46.32%, upper arm 7.07%, shoulder pan 4.95%, wrist flex 5.19%, elbow flex 0.73%, shoulder lift 0%.** Acceptability assessed with 5 Likert items, Wilcoxon signed-rank, α = 0.05. **Contact force in newtons: not reported, and cannot be** — the paper states its taxel output "is a non-trivial function of both the applied force and the contact area, which neither allows for direct interpretation as newtons of total force nor pascals of pressure."
- **Design relevance:** The evidentiary basis for "contact is a feature, not a failure" — the argument this whole project rests on — plus a caution: the link that hits the person first is the forearm, so that is where skin and compliance must go. It is also a warning about tactile-skin specification: a resistive taxel array that cannot be read in newtons cannot be used as a safety-limit sensor against ISO/TS 15066 pressure limits.
- **Evidence grade:** A (hardware, human subjects including users with motor impairments, in-home).
- **Limitations:** Rigid PR2 arms with a compliant skin and low-stiffness torque control — compliance again at the surface plus the controller. Freestanding mobile manipulator, not a torso; nobody leans on it, and all loads are light.

### PrioriTouch — Madan et al., CoRL 2025

- **Citation:** Madan, R., Lin, J., Goel, M., Li, A., Xie, A., Liang, X., Lee, M., Guo, J., Thakkar, P. N., Banerjee, R., Barreiros, J., Tsui, K., Silver, T., & Bhattacharjee, T. (2025). PrioriTouch: Adapting to User Contact Preferences for Whole-Arm Physical Human-Robot Interaction. *Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL) 2025*. arXiv:2509.18447.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18447 — **fetched** the PDF and read the full text; the arXiv comments field gives the venue as CoRL. Cornell University + Toyota Research Institute.
- **Open access:** yes (arXiv).
- **What it shows:** A hierarchical operational-space controller whose *priority ordering* over per-contact force objectives is learned online (LinUCB-Rank) from sparse user feedback, with candidate priority updates first validated in a digital twin before being run on the real robot.
- **Hard numbers:** population preference survey **n = 98, ages 32–77**, over body regions × three touch categories (functional assistance, emergency, sympathetic); real-robot bed-bathing evaluation with **n = 8 participants without visible mobility limitations**, of whom **7/8 preferred PrioriTouch over the heuristic-priority baseline**; platform is a **Kinova Gen3 with distributed tactile sensing**, simulation in RCareWorld; digital twin built from a RealSense D455 plus OpenPose.
- **Design relevance:** The clearest statement in this list that whole-arm assistive contact is a *multi-objective* problem with incompatible per-body-part force limits, and that a single global force cap is the wrong controller. If the project's two arms will contact several body regions at once, this is the control formulation to adopt. The simulation-in-the-loop exploration is also the right pattern for tuning contact force on a real person safely.
- **Evidence grade:** A (real robot, human subjects; no patients).
- **Limitations:** Rigid Kinova arm; all compliance is in the controller and the skin. Wheelchair/bed-mounted single arm, not a bilateral freestanding torso. Preferences were elicited from people without visible mobility limitations, so comfort thresholds may not hold for the target population.

### VTTB — Gu & Demiris, IEEE RA-L 2024

- **Citation:** Gu, Y., & Demiris, Y. (2024). VTTB: A Visuo-Tactile Learning Approach for Robot-Assisted Bed Bathing. *IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters*, 9(6), 5751–5758.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/LRA.2024.3396108 — Crossref-verified; **fetched** the accepted manuscript from Imperial College Spiral (handle 10044/1/112337, bitstream 83002b40-f4b2-4059-95c8-21a6f3445371) and read the full text.
- **Open access:** partial (IEEE paywalled; accepted manuscript free in Spiral).
- **What it shows:** A Transformer-based imitation-learning policy that fuses a visual 3D body contour with local tactile contact detail, letting a Baxter follow body curvature during wiping and generalise to unseen geometries.

- **Evidence grade:** A (hardware, two human subjects; mostly manikin).
- **Limitations:** Rigid Baxter arm; wiping, not weight bearing. 10 N is a *tool-on-skin* bound and says nothing about the load a torso must carry when leaned on — do not reuse it as a structural limit.

### Bimanual bathing — Gu & Demiris, IROS 2024

- **Citation:** Gu, Y., & Demiris, Y. (2024). Learning Bimanual Manipulation Policies for Bathing Bed-bound People. In *2024 IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS)*, 8936–8943.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/IROS58592.2024.10801478 — Crossref-verified; **fetched** the accepted manuscript from Imperial Spiral (handle 10044/1/118859, bitstream 20caaab2-2ded-4434-a7f4-3abfade70f3c) and read the full text.
- **Open access:** partial (accepted manuscript free in Spiral).
- **What it shows:** Multi-agent RL with a variable-impedance action space in which one arm lifts a limb while the other wipes underneath it — the bimanual "support-and-clean" pattern human carers use — trained in simulation and transferred to a physical Baxter.
- **Hard numbers:** reward-shaped **safe contact-force range [5 N, 10 N]**, described as "commonly adopted in relevant literature"; limb-support strengths **capped at 10 N** on the right arm for safety; tool-to-limb proximity threshold 10 cm; real-world evaluation restricted to **forearm and upper arm only, because of Baxter's payload limit**; target = medical-grade manikin. **No human subjects.**
- **Design relevance:** The only entry here that is genuinely *bilateral*, and the one that names the real constraint on two-arm assistance: payload. The authors could not lift a manikin's leg with a Baxter. A bilateral assistive torso lives or dies on per-arm lift capacity, and this is the cautionary data point.
- **Evidence grade:** B (sim-trained, hardware transfer to a manikin, no humans).
- **Limitations:** Rigid arms, simulation-dominant, manikin only. The 5–10 N band is a *simulation reward bound*, not a measured human tolerance.

### Soft exosuit after brachial plexus injury — Ferroni et al., 2026 *(authors were missing from the list)*

- **Citation:** Ferroni, R., Aglietta, V., Mauceri, D. F., Carpaneto, J., Micera, S., Titolo, P., & Proietti, T. (2026). Supporting upper limb movements with a soft robot after a brachial plexus injury. *Nature Communications*, 17(1), 10104. Published online 24 Aug 2026.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1038/s41467-026-76824-6 — Crossref-verified; **fetched** the publisher HTML at https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-76824-6 and read the abstract, figure captions and discussion, plus the `citation_author` metadata for the exact author order.
- **Open access:** yes (Nature Communications).
- **What it shows:** A soft wearable exosuit with **pneumatic shoulder and elbow modules on a textile harness, controlled from three IMUs** for kinematics-based volitional intent detection, providing **active gravity compensation at the shoulder** and triggered passive assistance at the elbow, tested single-session in people with chronic traumatic brachial plexus injury.

- **Limitations:** **Wearable exosuit, worn on the body**, reacting against the wearer's own torso — the exact opposite reaction path to a freestanding robot. Single-session, cross-sectional, no carry-over or long-term data, and the authors say so. The clinical outcome set transfers; the mechanics do not.

### SPA fascicles — Robertson, Sadeghi, Florez & Paik, 2017

- **Citation:** Robertson, M. A., Sadeghi, H., Florez, J. M., & Paik, J. (2017). Soft Pneumatic Actuator Fascicles for High Force and Reliability. *Soft Robotics*, 4(1), 23–32. (Published online 11 Oct 2016; issue dated March 2017. Robertson and Sadeghi contributed equally.) A 2019 **Correction** exists: *Soft Robotics*, 6(5), 685, DOI 10.1089/soro.2016.0029.correx.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1089/soro.2016.0029 — Crossref-verified; **fetched** the full text from PMC (PMC5346960) after the publisher PDF (liebertpub → journals.sagepub) was blocked by Cloudflare. Correction **fetched** from https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6793432/.
- **Open access:** yes (CC-BY; PMC5346960).
- **What it shows:** Bundling several fiber-reinforced soft pneumatic actuators in a soft interstitial constraint ("SPA pack") beats a volumetrically equivalent single actuator on force, and degrades gracefully when one unit in the pack fails.
- **Hard numbers — THE 112 N vs 18 N·m RECONCILIATION.** **Both figures are correct and they describe different objects.** From the paper's own Table 2 ("Summary of Modular SPA Characteristics"), at an operating pressure of **200 kPa (2 bar)**:
  - **Single unit SPA:** max load force **26 N**; 17 × 175 mm; **28 g**; bandwidth 1.958 Hz.
  - **SPA 4Pack** (a fascicle of **four** actuators): max load force **112 N**; 84 × 19 × 175 mm; **185 g**; bandwidth 2.028 Hz. Model predicted 122 N; the measured 112 N is **8.2% lower** than predicted. Quoted from the abstract: "Experimental measurements show an SPA pack to generate over 112 N linear force."
  - **Multi-DoF platform** (built from four SPA packs): cumulative vertical force **468 N** (4.1% below the 488 N predicted), average **117 N per pack**; **maximum moment capacity 18.00 N·m**; angular range **±5°**; bandwidth 1.327 Hz; 290 × 290 × 175 mm; **945 g**.
  - So: **112 N is the blocked linear force of one four-actuator pack. 18 N·m is the blocked pitch moment of the four-pack platform.** Neither figure contradicts the other, and "18 N·m at 2 bar" is only correct if you say it is the *platform*, not the fascicle.
  - Modelled advantage of a four-actuator fascicle over a volumetrically equivalent single SPA: **+23%** force, predicted and validated, with up to **+50%** achievable at higher actuator counts. Reliability claim is qualitative — on the failure of one unit the pack "is still capable of functioning at an only slightly reduced capacity" — **no cycle-life number is reported.**

- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware, bench-characterised, no humans).
- **Limitations:** Bench actuators, no robot, no person. Blocked-force figures are static and at a single pressure; there is no fatigue or cycle-life data, so "reliability" in the title means redundancy, not endurance. A torso built from these must supply its own life testing.

### Collision detection survey — Haddadin, De Luca & Albu-Schäffer, 2017

- **Citation:** Haddadin, S., De Luca, A., & Albu-Schäffer, A. (2017). Robot Collisions: A Survey on Detection, Isolation, and Identification. *IEEE Transactions on Robotics*, 33(6), 1292–1312.

## Verdict

Against a base that already holds Baloo, RIBA, ROBEAR, Robertson 2017, SEA/VSA and ISO/TS 15066, most of this list is redundant or off-target, and the honest count of genuinely new, load-bearing evidence is about six papers. The real additions are: Grice 2013 (the empirical case that deliberate arm-body contact is acceptable to users with motor impairments, plus the finding that the **forearm** is the link that touches first), Lakshmipathy 2026 (the first dataset of what caregivers actually do on a body, which is the only way to specify contact behaviour from evidence rather than from guidelines), Ferroni 2026 (14 patients, the strongest clinical result that **parallel, gravity-compensating** assistance works and that trajectory-imposing assistance is the wrong philosophy), PrioriTouch (the formal argument that per-body-part force limits conflict and a single global force cap is the wrong controller), Zhu 2026 (a hard latency number for an LLM-in-the-loop safety layer — ~240 ms, i.e. supervisory only — and an 18.5% NMAE reality check on model-based torque observers), and Wang & Xu 2021 (parallel kinematics as the way to buy back the stiffness that softness costs). Everything else is either a wearable glove or exosuit whose reaction path is the user's own body, which is the opposite of a freestanding torso, or a review that adds framing and no numbers. Six of the twenty-one are outright wearables and cannot inform structural sizing at all; three more are bed-bathing systems whose entire force envelope is ≤10 N, three orders of magnitude below what a leaning adult applies. The Robertson reconciliation is the most useful single result in this pass: at 2 bar you get 26 N per actuator, 112 N per four-pack, 468 N and 18 N·m from a four-pack platform with **±5°** of travel — which says plainly that SPA fascicles can reach RIBA-class force only as a large parallel array with almost no joint range, and that the project's hard problem is range and stiffness under load, not peak force. The list's worst habit is the one worth fixing repo-wide: it attaches real numbers to real papers that describe a different device than the reader will assume (112 N vs 18 N·m, 223 ms, a finger robot presented as a rehabilitation robot, a doll presented as a human), which is more dangerous than a fabricated citation because every metadata check passes.
