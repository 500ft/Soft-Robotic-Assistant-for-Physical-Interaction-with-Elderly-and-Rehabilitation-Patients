## Ranked sources

### ISO/TS 15066 — ISO, 2016
- **Citation:** ISO/TS 15066:2016, *Robots and robotic devices — Collaborative robots*. International Organization for Standardization, Geneva, 1st edition, 2016-02-15. 33 pp. ICS 25.040.30.
- **DOI/URL:** https://www.iso.org/standard/62996.html (catalogue; Cloudflare-blocked to automated fetch). Full text I actually fetched and extracted verbatim: `https://www.diag.uniroma1.it/deluca/pHRI_elective/ISO_TS_15066_2016_en.pdf`. Metadata (33 pages, status, price) cross-verified at https://genorma.com/en/standards/iso-ts-15066-2016 (fetched).
- **Open access:** No. EUR 179 ex-VAT / EUR 216.59 inc-VAT (genorma, VERIFIED). The course-page PDF is a single-user licence copy and its hosting is likely not authorised — **buy a copy or pull it through NYU library before citing it in the report.** Status is `90.92 — Standard to be revised`, still Published (VERIFIED).
- **What it shows:** The only document in the whole standards landscape that publishes an actual table of per-body-region contact force and pressure limits, plus a two-body inelastic-impact model that converts those limits into an energy budget and then into a maximum relative speed.
- **Hard numbers:**
  - **Table A.2, quasi-static contact — max permissible pressure p_s (N/cm²) and force (N), verbatim, VERIFIED.** Force is per *body region*; pressure is per *specific body area*.
    | Body region | Force (N) | Specific area | p_s (N/cm²) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Skull and forehead (critical) | 130 | Mid-forehead / Temple | 130 / 110 |
    | Face (critical) | 65 | Masticatory muscle | 110 |
    | Neck | 150 | Neck muscle / 7th cervical | 140 / 210 |
    | Back and shoulders | 210 | Shoulder joint / L5 | 160 / 210 |
    | Chest | 140 | Sternum / Pectoral | 120 / 170 |
    | Abdomen | 110 | Abdominal muscle | 140 |
    | Pelvis | 180 | Pelvic bone | 210 |
    | Upper arms & elbow | 150 | Deltoid / Humerus | 190 / 220 |
    | Lower arms & wrist | 160 | Radial bone / Forearm muscle / Arm nerve | 190 / 180 / 180 |
    | Hands and fingers | 140 | Forefinger pad D/ND 300/270; end joint D/ND 280/220; thenar 200; palm D/ND 260/260; back of hand D/ND 200/190 | |
    | Thighs and knees | 220 | Thigh muscle / Kneecap | 250 / 220 |
    | Lower legs | 130 | Shin / Calf | 220 / 210 |
  - **Transient multiplier = 2** for both force and pressure, all regions except skull/forehead and face, where transient is **"not applicable"** (VERIFIED). Clause 5.5.5.3: *"Contact exposure to sensitive body regions, including the skull, forehead, larynx, eyes, ears or face shall be prevented whenever reasonably practicable"* (VERIFIED, verbatim).
  - **Table A.3 — effective spring constant k (N/mm) / effective mass m_H (kg), VERIFIED:** skull 150/4,4 · face 75/4,4 · neck 50/1,2 · back-shoulders 35/40 · chest 25/40 · abdomen 10/40 · pelvis 25/40 · upper arm 30/3 · lower arm 40/2 · hands 75/0,6 · thighs-knees 50/75 · lower legs 60/75. k valid for ~1 cm² contact area.
  - **Table A.4 — max transferred energy (J), VERIFIED:** skull 0,23 · face 0,11 · neck 0,84 · back-shoulders 2,5 · chest 1,6 · abdomen 2,4 · pelvis 2,6 · upper arm 1,5 · lower arm 1,3 · hands 0,49 · thighs-knees 1,9 · lower legs 0,52.
  - **Formulae, VERIFIED:** E = F²/2k = A²p²/2k (A.1); μ = (1/m_H + 1/m_R)⁻¹ (A.3); m_R = M/2 + m_L (A.4); v_rel = F/√(μk) = pA/√(μk) (A.5).
  - **Provenance of the numbers, VERIFIED verbatim from footnotes a and b:** pressures come from *"the study conducted by the University of Mainz on pain onset levels… 100 healthy adult test subjects on 29 specific body areas"*, reported as the **75th percentile**, peak pressures averaged at 1 mm² resolution, using a **flat 1,4 × 1,4 cm metal test surface with 2 mm edge radius**. The standard itself says the values *"are the result of a single study in a subject area that has not been the basis of extensive research"* and *"there is a possibility that another test apparatus could yield different results."* The **forces** come from a separate literature survey (BG/BGIA U 001/2009e) *"referring to 188 sources"*, anchored to *"the lowest energy transfer criteria that could result in a minor injury, such as a bruise, equivalent to a severity of 1 on the Abbreviated Injury Scale"*, and the standard states they *"will be replaced in future by values from a research more specific for collaborative robots."*
  - **Scope, VERIFIED verbatim:** *"It does not apply to non-industrial robots, although the safety principles presented can be useful to other areas of robotics."*
- **Design relevance:** This is the numeric backbone. It is where every force, pressure and energy target in the test campaign comes from, and clause 5.5.5.3 is what forbids the arms from being able to reach a seated user's head or throat at all.
- **Evidence grade:** A — limits rest on a 100-subject human study and a standardised algometer method.
- **Limitations:** Three that matter here. (1) **Scope**: by its own words it does not apply to this device; importing it is an argument, not a compliance claim. (2) **Population**: "100 healthy adult test subjects", German working-age, healthy, excluded pre-existing conditions — nothing about frail elderly skin, osteoporotic ribs, anticoagulant-related bruising, or steroid-thinned skin. (3) **The forces are not measurements at all** — they are a literature-survey estimate from 188 mixed sources, which the standard flags as provisional. Note also an apparent typo in the source document: Table A.1 lists area 5 as "Seventh neck vertebra" while Table A.2 renders it "Seventh neck muscle".

### ISO 13482 — ISO, 2014
- **Citation:** ISO 13482:2014, *Robots and robotic devices — Safety requirements for personal care robots*. International Organization for Standardization, Geneva, 1st edition, 2014. 79 pp (EN) / 93 pp (FR).
- **DOI/URL:** https://www.iso.org/standard/53820.html (403 to automated fetch). Metadata fetched from https://genorma.com/en/standards/iso-13482-2014 — status Published, 79 pages, EUR 204 ex-VAT / EUR 246.84 inc-VAT (VERIFIED).
- **Open access:** No. ~EUR 204–247 / ~USD 232 (ANSI webstore listing exists at https://webstore.ansi.org/Standards/ISO/iso134822014 but returns 403 to automated fetch — price UNVERIFIED). Check NYU library: NYU subscribes to several standards platforms; ISO via IHS/Techstreet is the likely route. Worth 20 minutes with a librarian before spending EUR 204.
- **What it shows:** The standard that actually governs a freestanding, non-medical, earthbound assistive robot that physically supports a person. It defines three types — mobile servant robot, **physical assistant robot**, and person carrier robot — and explicitly excludes industrial robots (→ ISO 10218) and medical devices.
- **Hard numbers:** **Essentially none for contact safety, and this is the central finding of C1.** It sets a 20 km/h travel-speed exclusion boundary (VERIFIED via search result text) but publishes **no force, pressure, or energy limit tables**. Secondary sources consistently report the standard's own note that for impact/collision hazards *no exhaustive, internationally recognised pain or injury data existed at the time of publication* — I could **not** fetch a page rendering that sentence verbatim, so treat that quotation as **UNVERIFIED** and confirm it against clause text once you have the PDF. Everything else is delegated to ISO 12100-style risk assessment.
- **Design relevance:** This is the standard the design review should name as governing. It gives the team the *structure* of the safety argument (hazard identification, risk reduction, verification) and the device classification "physical assistant robot" — but it hands back no number, which is precisely why ISO/TS 15066 has to be imported.
- **Evidence grade:** D — normative document, no underlying dataset of its own.
- **Limitations:** No quantitative contact limits at all; relies on the integrator to justify their own thresholds. It is also a 2014 document written before ISO/TS 15066 existed, and a `prEN ISO 13482` revision is in progress under a broader "service robots" title — check its status before freezing the report, because the numbers landscape may change under you. Supplementary guidance exists as ISO/TR 23482-2:2019.

### IEC 80601-2-78 — IEC/ISO, 2019 (+ Amd 1:2024)
- **Citation:** IEC 80601-2-78:2019, *Medical electrical equipment — Part 2-78: Particular requirements for basic safety and essential performance of medical robots for rehabilitation, assessment, compensation or alleviation*. Edition 1.0, published 2019-07-09. Amendment 1:2024 published 2024-03-12.
- **DOI/URL:** https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/33594 — **fetched successfully**, this is the page I verified title, date, edition, price and scope from.
- **Open access:** No. **CHF 380** (VERIFIED from the IEC webstore page).
- **What it shows:** The particular standard for RACA robots — robots that physically interact with an impaired patient to support or perform rehabilitation, assessment, compensation or alleviation of movement function. It layers on top of IEC 60601-1.
- **Hard numbers:** Scope and exclusions VERIFIED verbatim from the fetched page: applies to *"MEDICAL ROBOTS that physically interact with a PATIENT with an IMPAIRMENT to support or perform REHABILITATION, ASSESSMENT, COMPENSATION or ALLEVIATION related to the PATIENT'S MOVEMENT FUNCTIONS"*; **does not apply to** external limb prosthetics (ISO 22523), electric wheelchairs (ISO 7176), diagnostic imaging (IEC 60601-2-33), or **personal care robots (use ISO 13482)**. I did not obtain the clause text, so any specific force/torque/ROM limit inside it is **UNVERIFIED** — do not assume it contains a contact-force table.
- **Design relevance:** This is the fork in the road. The moment the team describes the device as delivering *therapy* to *patients*, it becomes a medical device and this standard governs instead of ISO 13482 — with a full IEC 60601-1 burden attached. If the device is described as *assistance with daily activity*, ISO 13482 governs. The project brief says "elderly and rehabilitation patients", which straddles the line. **Pick one and write it into the requirements document in week one**, because the two paths have different verification costs.
- **Evidence grade:** D — normative document.
- **Limitations:** The mutual exclusion is the problem: 80601-2-78 punts personal care robots to ISO 13482, and ISO 13482 punts medical devices to the medical framework. A device that is both lands in a genuine gap, and neither branch hands you a contact-force number.

### ISO 10218-1 / -2 — ISO, 2025
- **Citation:** ISO 10218-1:2025, *Robotics — Safety requirements — Part 1: Industrial robots*, 104 pp; and ISO 10218-2:2025, *Robotics — Safety requirements — Part 2: Industrial robot applications and robot cells*. Both published 2025, superseding the 2011 editions.
- **DOI/URL:** https://www.iso.org/standard/73933.html and https://www.iso.org/standard/73934.html (403 to fetch). Status and metadata fetched from https://genorma.com/en/standards/iso-10218-1-2025 and `.../iso-10218-2-2025`; change summary fetched from https://www.therobotreport.com/iso-10218-industrial-robot-safety-standard-receives-major-overhaul/.
- **Open access:** No. ISO 10218-1:2025 EUR 227 ex-VAT / EUR 274.67 inc-VAT, 104 pp (VERIFIED, genorma). USD 244 for US customers (VERIFIED, The Robot Report).
- **What it shows:** The 2025 overhaul folded the collaborative-robot content formerly in ISO/TS 15066 into ISO 10218 itself, dropped the terms "collaborative operation" and "collaborative robot", added functional-safety and cybersecurity requirements, and introduced robot classes (Class I for very low-hazard robots with reduced control requirements, roughly PL b).
- **Hard numbers:** Publication status VERIFIED: both 2025 parts are `60.60 Standard published`; both 2011 editions are `WITHDRAWN`. ISO/TS 15066:2016 is **not** withdrawn — it sits at `90.92 Standard to be revised`, still Published (VERIFIED, genorma). Whether the Annex A biomechanical tables were carried into ISO 10218-2:2025 **unchanged, revised, or renumbered is UNVERIFIED** — I could not fetch clause text. **This is the single most important open item in C1.** If the team is going to cite a number in a design review in 2026, someone has to open ISO 10218-2:2025 and check whether Table A.2 still says 140 N for the chest.
- **Design relevance:** Sets the citation hygiene for the report. Citing "ISO/TS 15066" in 2026 without acknowledging the 2025 consolidation will get flagged by any reviewer who follows robot safety.
- **Evidence grade:** D — normative document.
- **Limitations:** Industrial scope. It governs industrial robots and their applications, not a freestanding personal care device, so it is even further from this project than TS 15066 was.

### ISO 14971 — ISO, 2019
- **Citation:** ISO 14971:2019, *Medical devices — Application of risk management to medical devices*. 3rd edition, 2019. 36 pp.
- **DOI/URL:** https://www.iso.org/standard/72704.html (403 to fetch). Metadata fetched from https://genorma.com/en/standards/iso-14971-2019.
- **Open access:** No. EUR 196 ex-VAT / EUR 237.16 inc-VAT, 36 pp (VERIFIED, genorma). ANSI/AAMI/ISO 14971:2019 listed at USD 343 (UNVERIFIED — from search-result text, the ANSI page 403'd).
- **What it shows:** The process standard for identifying hazards, estimating and evaluating risk, implementing controls, evaluating residual risk, and performing benefit–risk analysis across the device lifecycle. The 2019 edition strengthened benefit–risk analysis and post-production surveillance.
- **Hard numbers:** None. It is deliberately process-only — it specifies no acceptability thresholds and leaves the criteria to the manufacturer's risk management policy.
- **Design relevance:** This is the *machinery* by which the team is allowed to import ISO/TS 15066 numbers into a device that TS 15066 says it does not cover. The argument is: identify the contact hazard, select a threshold with a documented rationale, justify the choice, verify by test, and record residual risk. Without a 14971-shaped risk file, "we designed to 140 N because ISO/TS 15066 says so" is an assertion; with one, it is a controlled decision.
- **Evidence grade:** D — process standard.
- **Limitations:** Gives no number and will never give a number. It also formally applies to medical devices; if the team goes the ISO 13482 route, the equivalent machinery is ISO 12100 risk assessment, though 14971's benefit–risk structure is still the better template for a patient-contacting device.

### Behrens 2021 — Behrens, Pliske, Umbreit, Piatek, Walcher & Elkmann, 2021
- **Citation:** Behrens, R., Pliske, G., Umbreit, M., Piatek, S., Walcher, F., & Elkmann, N. (2021). A statistical model to determine biomechanical limits for physically safe interactions with collaborative robots. *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, 8, 667818.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2021.667818 — fetched both https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2021.667818/full and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8850785/ (overlapping rows agreed).
- **Open access:** Yes, fully. Frontiers + PMC.
- **What it shows:** The Fraunhofer IFF / Magdeburg experimental verification that ISO/TS 15066 explicitly anticipated in its own footnotes. 112 subjects, both **pinching (quasi-static)** and **pendulum impact (transient)** loading, producing a statistical model for 75th-percentile limits. Its headline result is that the existing standard values are non-conservative for the torso.
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED-VIA-EXTRACTION):** **112 subjects** in five groups (G1 41 [13F/28M], G2 20 [6F/14M], G3 20 [10F/10M], G4 20 [10F/10M], G5 control 11 [5F/6M]); **age 16–67**, group means 36.3–41.9; **28 body locations**; neck muscle omitted for safety. Pinching at 1 mm/s via algometer; impact via pendulum at **6.5 kg and 16.5 kg**; semi-sharp probe **1.96 cm²** and blunt 30 mm foam. Proposed limits at 75th percentile assuming a 70 %-male population, as *pinch pressure (N/cm²) / pinch force (N) / impact pressure (N/cm²) / impact force (N)*:
  forehead 110/110/180/150 · temple 50/60/70/90 · masticatory 40/40/50/70 · C7 70/50/170/70 · shoulder joint 60/60/100/100 · L5 60/110/200/180 · sternum 50/80/80/110 · pectoral 50/60/70/110 · abdominal 50/60/60/90 · pelvic bone 140/90/270/140 · deltoid 70/100/100/110 · humerus 50/70/130/150 · radial bone 70/100/170/180 · forearm muscle 50/100/140/170 · arm nerve 60/80/130/140 · forefinger pad 70/150/240/390 · forefinger DIP 150/160/490/370 · thenar 60/120/180/260 · palm 60/150/280/330 · back of hand 170/150/470/250 · thigh 70/140/160/200 · kneecap 110/160/260/270 · shin 130/150/370/260 · calf 70/130/180/260.
  Authors' verdict, VERIFIED: *"Most of the limits in ISO/TS 15066 are preliminary, since they are based on unverified data from a literature survey."*
- **Design relevance:** **This is the paper that should set the torso numbers, not ISO/TS 15066.** For every region this project actually contacts, Behrens is lower: sternum 50 vs 120 N/cm²; shoulder joint 60 vs 160 N/cm²; L5 60 vs 210 N/cm²; deltoid 70 vs 190 N/cm². A torso designed to the ISO chest pressure would be roughly **2.4× over** the experimentally measured pain-onset pressure. Design to Behrens, cite ISO as the standard, and state the delta explicitly in the report — that reads as rigour, not as deviation.
- **Evidence grade:** A — 112 human subjects, both loading modes, statistical model.
- **Limitations:** Working age only, **16–67**, explicitly excluding older adults; no age-decade stratification published; the 70 %-male weighting is a workforce assumption that does not describe an elderly care population; authors stress limits are valid *only at the tested locations*, not across whole regions. Because I read Table 8 through an extraction layer, re-check the rows you put in the spec against the published table.

### Melia 2019 — Melia, Geissler, König, Ottersbach, Umbreit, Letzel & Muttray, 2019
- **Citation:** Melia, M., Geissler, B., König, J., Ottersbach, H. J., Umbreit, M., Letzel, S., & Muttray, A. (2019). Pressure pain thresholds: Subject factors and the meaning of peak pressures. *European Journal of Pain*, 23(1), 167–182.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1002/ejp.1298 (Wiley 403'd). Abstract fetched verbatim via NCBI E-utilities, PMID 30076659.
- **Open access:** No (Wiley paywall). Abstract free via PubMed; NYU almost certainly has EJP through Wiley Online Library.
- **What it shows:** The peer-reviewed publication of the Mainz work that produced the ISO/TS 15066 pressure table. Its finding is methodological and awkward for the standard: pressure under the algometer probe is **not uniform**, peak pressures at the probe corners and over bone are what appear to drive pain onset, and conventional algometers report mean pressure — so the probe, indentation depth and tissue properties materially change the number.
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED, abstract):** **100 healthy subjects (57 men), age 18–66**, **29 body sites**, each measured 3× non-consecutively in randomised order; **40 subjects were manual labourers**. Men and manual labourers had **higher** adjusted thresholds (both force and pressure). Authors flag *"higher intra-individual differences regarding peak pressures at the spinous processes, breastbone, forehead and abdomen"* — caution advised interpreting those sites. Per-site threshold values are in the full text, not the abstract, and are therefore **UNVERIFIED** here; the ISO Table A.2 pressures are the 75th-percentile derivative of this dataset.
- **Design relevance:** Two direct consequences. (1) Sample size and demographics for the standard's pressure column are now pinned: 100 subjects, 18–66, 57 % male — cite this, not the standard's footnote, when a reviewer asks "how many people?". (2) The **breastbone and abdomen are named as the least reproducible sites**, and those are exactly where a person leaning on this torso makes contact. Contact-area design and pressure-measurement-film methodology matter more than the force number for this project.
- **Evidence grade:** A — 100 human subjects, standardised algometry, repeated measures.
- **Limitations:** Healthy adults, upper bound 66 years, German occupational cohort; the manual-labourer effect shows thresholds track skin and tissue conditioning, which cuts the wrong way for frail users. The peak-vs-mean pressure finding implies the ISO numbers are apparatus-specific — a soft robot's compliant contact patch will have a completely different pressure distribution than a 1.4 × 1.4 cm rigid metal probe.

### Park 2019 — Park, Han, Lim, Shin, Byun, Kim & Rhim, 2019
- **Citation:** Park, M. Y., Han, D., Lim, J. H., Shin, M. K., Byun, Y. R., Kim, D. Y., & Rhim, S. (2019). Assessment of pressure pain thresholds in collisions with collaborative robots. *PLoS ONE*, 14(5), e0215890.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0215890 — fetched https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6497239/.
- **Open access:** Yes, fully (PLoS ONE + PMC).
- **What it shows:** An independent, non-German replication of pressure-pain-threshold measurement for cobot contact, and the first to report an explicit age effect within the working-age band.
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED-VIA-EXTRACTION):** **90 healthy males**, age **20–50** (mean 28.9), weight 72.8 ± 11.9 kg, height 174.8 ± 5.6 cm; **15 sites**. PPT range **65.1–196.1 N/cm²**. Highest: back of hand 196.1 ± 85.8. Lowest: **arm nerve 65.1 ± 22.6**. Moderate band 100–120 N/cm² at forehead, neck muscle, thenar, shin. Age effect: participants **under 30 had thresholds 3–33 % lower** than those over 30, largest at C7 (32.1 %), shoulder joint (31 %), neck muscle (29.4 %).
- **Design relevance:** Two things. (1) The **standard deviations are enormous** — ±85.8 on a mean of 196.1 is a coefficient of variation near 44 %. Any design margin computed against a mean is meaningless; the team must design against a low percentile and say which. (2) The arm-nerve result, 65.1 N/cm² mean measured against 180 N/cm² in ISO Table A.2, is a **~2.8× discrepancy** in the region the arms will contact most. It corroborates Behrens' direction.
- **Evidence grade:** A — 90 human subjects.
- **Limitations:** **All male**, so no sex effect can be estimated; age capped at 50, so it says nothing about 75-year-olds; Korean cohort with different body composition from the German cohorts; mean ± SD reported rather than the 75th percentile the standard uses, so the comparison to Table A.2 is not apples-to-apples and should be stated as directional.

### Han 2022 — Han, Park, Choi, Shin, Kim & Rhim, 2022
- **Citation:** Han, D., Park, M., Choi, J., Shin, H., Kim, D., & Rhim, S. (2022). Assessment of pain onset and maximum bearable pain thresholds in physical contact situations. *Sensors*, 22(8), 2996.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/s22082996 (MDPI 403'd). Fetched https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9030557/ for the tables; abstract verified verbatim via NCBI E-utilities, PMID 35458981, PMCID PMC9030557.
- **Open access:** Yes, fully (MDPI + PMC).
- **What it shows:** Separates **pain onset** from **maximum bearable pain** at 29 body locations under quasi-static algometric loading — the only source here that quantifies the headroom between "this hurts" and "I cannot tolerate this".
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED-VIA-EXTRACTION):** **40 healthy males, age 20–29**, weight 72.0 ± 12.15 kg, height 173.6 ± 5.80 cm, BMI 23.82 ± 3.31; **29 locations**; third-quartile values. As *onset force (N) / onset pressure (N/cm²) / max-bearable force (N) / max-bearable pressure (N/cm²)*: forehead 111.4/61.0/412.7/291.7 · neck muscle 56.7/72.8/226.3/411.5 · sternum 49.9/39.4/246.2/179.0 · deltoid 59.4/60.6/306.9/387.2 · forefinger pad 138.7–156.9/107.1–135.1/509.9–529.0/355.3–370.9 · thigh 87.1/70.8/261.0/266.8 · shin 93.9/86.5/384.1/340.9. Ratio of max-bearable to onset is roughly **3–5×**. Authors state pain-onset results were **lower than the ISO/TS 15066 PFL limits**, while max-bearable exceeded them.
- **Design relevance:** Sets the safety-margin architecture. Design the **normal operating envelope below pain onset** (~40–60 N/cm² on the sternum, not 120), and treat the region between onset and max-bearable as the fault-tolerance band the single-fault condition may enter briefly. It also gives a third independent confirmation that the ISO sternum pressure is too high: 39.4 N/cm² measured onset versus 120 N/cm² in Table A.2 is roughly **3×**.
- **Evidence grade:** A — 40 human subjects.
- **Limitations:** Smallest and narrowest cohort here — **40 males, all in their twenties**. Quasi-static only, no impact. "Maximum bearable pain" in a healthy 25-year-old volunteer who can stop the test at any moment has no defensible relationship to what is tolerable or safe for a frail 80-year-old who may not be able to withdraw, communicate, or perceive the load. **Do not use the max-bearable column as a design limit.**

### Haddadin 2007 (crash-testing) — Haddadin, Albu-Schäffer & Hirzinger, 2007
- **Citation:** Haddadin, S., Albu-Schäffer, A., & Hirzinger, G. (2007). Safety evaluation of physical human-robot interaction via crash-testing. In *Robotics: Science and Systems III*, pp. 217–224. MIT Press.
- **DOI/URL:** https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss03/p28.pdf — **fetched and extracted verbatim**. https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2007.III.028
- **Open access:** Yes, fully (RSS proceedings are free).
- **What it shows:** The foundational experiment: a DLR LWR III driven into a Hybrid III dummy at an ADAC automotive crash-test facility, evaluating the full EuroNCAP severity index battery. The result that reframed the field is that the robot scores *trivially* low on automotive metrics — which is a finding about the metrics, not a clean bill of health for robots.
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED, verbatim from the PDF):** LWR III **14 kg**, 7 DOF, reflected mass **≈ 4 kg at the TCP**; **1 kg aluminium impactor**; TCP velocities **{0.2, 0.7, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0} m/s**; Hybrid III 50th-percentile male dummy; all indices computed independently by ADAC. **HIC36 limit 650**, corresponding to a **5 % probability of AIS ≥ 3**; head acceleration criterion **a_3ms ≤ 72 g**. Measured: **HIC36 below 25 at 2 m/s**; a_3ms reached **less than 20 % of the 72 g limit** at 2 m/s. HIC **saturates with increasing robot mass** at each impact velocity — simulated to 10 m/s and to large masses, increasing robot mass does not raise HIC further.
- **Design relevance:** Kills the temptation to certify this project with a HIC number. If a 14 kg rigid-link industrial-grade arm cannot get HIC36 above 25 at 2 m/s, a compliant soft torso will produce a number so far below threshold that it proves nothing. **Do not put HIC in the test plan as the acceptance criterion** — a reviewer who knows this literature will read it as metric-shopping. Use force, pressure and energy.
- **Evidence grade:** A — standardised crash-test protocol, certified facility, instrumented ATD.
- **Limitations:** Stated in the paper's own footnote 11, VERIFIED verbatim: *"the presented evaluation is carried out for average male dummies and not for females or children. For such an analysis further tests with specially designed female or child dummies would be necessary."* No elderly ATD exists at all. The paper also explicitly scopes itself to **free blunt impact** and excludes **clamping** — and clamping against a fixed structure is the dominant hazard for a freestanding torso a person leans on.

### Haddadin 2012 (injury knowledge) — Haddadin, Haddadin, Khoury, Rokahr, Parusel, Burgkart, Bicchi & Albu-Schäffer, 2012
- **Citation:** Haddadin, S., Haddadin, S., Khoury, A., Rokahr, T., Parusel, S., Burgkart, R., Bicchi, A., & Albu-Schäffer, A. (2012). On making robots understand safety: Embedding injury knowledge into control. *The International Journal of Robotics Research*, 31(13), 1578–1602.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364912462256 (SAGE 403'd). Full PDF **fetched and extracted verbatim** from http://www.diag.uniroma1.it/~deluca/pHRI_elective/IJRR12_EmbeddingInjuryinControl_Haddadin_etal.pdf
- **Open access:** No at publisher; author/course copy freely readable.
- **What it shows:** Replaces automotive indices with a directly measured map from (reflected inertia, velocity, contact geometry) → medically classified injury, built from porcine impact experiments and medically graded by AO classification, then compiled into a real-time velocity-shaping controller (the Safe Motion Unit).
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED, verbatim):** **276 impact tests** on fresh pig abdominal wall samples. Impactors: wedge 45° r = 0.2 mm 2.7 kg; small sphere r = 5 mm 2.1 kg; large sphere r = 12.5 mm 2.2 kg; plus a Torx T45 bit. Velocities **0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0 m/s**; impactor masses **≈ 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 kg**. Injury scales: closed skin IC1–IC5, open skin IO1–IO5, muscle/tendon MT1–MT3, nerve/vessel NV. Key thresholds, verbatim: *"safety can only be guaranteed with velocities < 2 m/s and < 6 kg"* (small sphere, which pierces skin at low velocity and weight); *"The maximum velocity should not exceed 1.5 m/s and with additional weights the velocity must be reduced below 1.0 m/s"*; wedge damage requires *"weights above 8 kg and impact velocities of at least 3.0 m/s"*; for the Torx, *"cutaneous defects at < 1.0 m/s and impact mass < 8 kg could not be observed"* and *"at an impactor weight of > 6 kg, muscle damage can be definitely excluded only at velocities < 0.5 m/s"*. Design framing: reflected inertia of interactive robots is stated as **1–15 kg**, and an interactive robot *"should certainly not move faster than 4–5 m/s"*; v_max(m) is a regression line, limited between **0.1 and 4.5 m/s**.
- **Design relevance:** Supplies the **velocity ceiling** and proves the point the soft-robotics pitch depends on: **geometry dominates**. A 5 mm sphere pierces skin where a 12.5 mm sphere and a 45° wedge do not, at identical mass and velocity. That is the quantitative justification for the arm's end-effector and any exposed hardware to be large-radius and padded, and it converts "soft is safe" from a slogan into a radius requirement. Adopt **≤ 1.5 m/s** as the arm-tip velocity cap, dropping to **≤ 1.0 m/s** if reflected inertia exceeds ~6–8 kg.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware-validated on biological tissue, no human subjects.
- **Limitations:** Authors' own caveat, VERIFIED verbatim: non-vital tissue *"lacks numerous characteristics, such as muscle tension, skin pre-stretch, and the possibility of reacting or evading"*, and cannot show haemorrhage, pain, or neurologic deficit. Pig abdominal wall is a reasonable analogue for young adult human skin and a **poor** analogue for atrophic elderly dermis. Free impact, not clamping.

### El Tumi 2017 — El Tumi, Johnson, Dantas, Maynard & Tashani, 2017
- **Citation:** El Tumi, H., Johnson, M. I., Dantas, P. B. F., Maynard, M. J., & Tashani, O. A. (2017). Age-related changes in pain sensitivity in healthy humans: A systematic review with meta-analysis. *European Journal of Pain*, 21(6), 955–964.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1002/ejp.1011 (Wiley 403'd). Abstract fetched verbatim via NCBI E-utilities, PMID 28230292.
- **Open access:** No (Wiley). Abstract free; NYU should have EJP.
- **What it shows:** **The elderly-population gap, quantified.** Pooled meta-analysis of pain threshold across the lifespan in healthy pain-free humans, with pressure and heat analysed separately — and they diverge.
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED, abstract):** **12 studies**, individual sample sizes **30–244**. Seven of nine adult studies found significant old-vs-young differences but *"the direction of change was inconsistent"*. Meta-analysis result: **pressure pain threshold was LOWER in old adults than younger adults, p = 0.018, I² = 60.970 %**. **No difference** in contact heat pain thresholds (p = 0.0001, I² = 90.23 %). Old adults defined as mean 62.2 ± 3.4 to 79 ± 4 years; younger 22 ± 1.5 to 39.1 ± 8.8. Methodological quality high, low publication bias, but *"substantial statistical and methodological heterogeneity"*.
- **Design relevance:** **This is the citation that justifies a safety factor on top of ISO/TS 15066 for this project's user population.** Older adults are *more* sensitive to mechanically evoked pain, and every number in ISO/TS 15066, Behrens, Melia, Park and Han was measured on people younger than the intended users. The team should apply an explicit, documented de-rating factor to the imported limits and cite this meta-analysis as the basis. Note also that it directly contradicts Park's within-working-age finding that under-30s had lower thresholds — the age curve is not monotonic, and the team should not extrapolate Park's trend past 50.
- **Evidence grade:** A — meta-analysis of human-subject data (not a narrative review).
- **Limitations:** Authors call the evidence *"tentative"*; I² ≈ 61 % is substantial heterogeneity; the pooled effect is reported as a p-value in the abstract without an effect size, so **the magnitude of the de-rating is UNVERIFIED** — the team must read the full text to extract an SMD before choosing a numeric factor. Healthy pain-free elderly only, which excludes exactly the comorbid rehabilitation population this device targets.

### Paez-Granados 2022 — Paez-Granados & Billard, 2022
- **Citation:** Paez-Granados, D., & Billard, A. (2022). Crash test-based assessment of injury risks for adults and children when colliding with personal mobility devices and service robots. *Scientific Reports*, 12, 5285.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-09349-9 — fetched https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8960768/
- **Open access:** Yes, fully.
- **What it shows:** The modern counterpart to Haddadin 2007, but for **service robots at service-robot masses** — and it reaches the opposite conclusion, because 60–133 kg of robot is a completely different hazard from a 14 kg arm. ### Vemula 2018 — Vemula, Matthias & Ahmad, 2018
- **Citation:** Vemula, B., Matthias, B., & Ahmad, A. (2018). A design metric for safety assessment of industrial robot design suitable for power- and force-limited collaborative operation. *International Journal of Intelligent Robotics and Applications*, 2(2), 226–234.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s41315-018-0055-9 — fetched https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5966502
- **Open access:** Yes (PMC).
- **What it shows:** Proposes **power flux density** as a PFL design metric, W = (F_c · δ̇)/A_c — contact force times deformation rate over contact area — arguing that neither force nor pressure alone captures injury onset.
- **Hard numbers (VERIFIED-VIA-EXTRACTION):** Uses Behrens & Elkmann (2014) collision data at masses **4.16 and 8.65 kg** and impact velocities **0.64–0.94 m/s**; biomechanical stiffnesses **30 N/mm (upper arm)** and **40 N/mm (lower arm)**, matching ISO/TS 15066 Table A.3; restitution coefficient swept **0.2–0.5**. ## Verdict for the design team

**ISO 13482:2014 governs this device, and it will not give you a single number.** A freestanding, non-medical, earthbound torso that physically supports a person is a "physical assistant robot" under ISO 13482; IEC 80601-2-78 explicitly hands personal care robots back to ISO 13482, and ISO/TS 15066 explicitly says it does not apply to non-industrial robots. All three scope statements are verified, and the consequence is that **no standard both governs this device and publishes a contact limit** — so the project's safety claim is built by importing ISO/TS 15066's numbers under a documented ISO 14971-style risk-management argument, and the report must say that out loud rather than implying compliance.

**Decide in week one whether this is a medical device**, because the phrase "rehabilitation patients" in the brief is what drags it across the line into IEC 80601-2-78 (CHF 380) and the entire IEC 60601-1 burden; describing it as assistance with daily activity keeps it under ISO 13482 (~EUR 204). This is a scoping decision with a large cost attached, not a wording preference.

**Design to Behrens 2021, not to ISO/TS 15066.** Three independent human-subject studies — Behrens (112 subjects), Park (90), Han (40) — all land below the ISO table in the regions this project contacts, and the sternum is the worst case: ISO says 120 N/cm², Behrens measured 50, Han measured 39.4 at pain onset. ISO/TS 15066's own footnotes concede the force column is a 188-source literature estimate rather than a measurement and "will be replaced in future". Designing to the ISO chest pressure would put the torso roughly 2.4–3× above measured pain onset, and stating that delta explicitly in the report reads as rigour rather than deviation.

**Do not certify this with HIC.** Haddadin measured HIC36 below 25 at 2 m/s on a 14 kg arm against a limit of 650; a compliant torso will score so low that the number proves nothing, and a reviewer who knows this literature will read a HIC-based claim as metric-shopping. Use force, pressure and transferred energy, and use HIC only as a cited reason for *not* using HIC.

**The elderly gap is the weakest joint in the entire safety case, and it is worse than "old people are frail".** Every pain-onset number in the requirement table was measured on healthy volunteers aged roughly 16–67 — Han's cohort was 40 men in their twenties — while the cadaver impact-biomechanics literature that underpins the injury thresholds comes disproportionately from *elderly* donors with wide confidence intervals. The two halves of the argument are drawn from different populations and neither is the intended user. El Tumi's meta-analysis shows pressure pain threshold is significantly *lower* in adults over 62 (p = 0.018), so an explicit de-rating factor on R1–R9 is mandatory — but the abstract reports no effect size, so somebody has to read the full text and extract an SMD before the team picks a number. Note too that older adults may have *reduced* protective sensation while having *reduced* tissue tolerance, which is the worst possible combination: the user may not withdraw from a load that is already injuring them, so pain onset is not a reliable proxy for injury onset in this population.

**Two hazards in this project are not covered by any number in the table.** Clamping — a person leaning on a torso that traps a limb against a fixed structure — is explicitly outside Haddadin's blunt-impact scope, and quasi-static clamping against a soft actuator that continues to hold pressure is the dominant realistic hazard for a device designed to be leaned on. And tip-over: if the freestanding base can fall onto a seated or supine user, Paez-Granados shows that 60 kg at 3.1 m/s produces AIS3+ probabilities of 14–50 %, which the arm-contact numbers do not address at all. Both need FMEA rows and a test, not just an analysis.

**Before the design review, close three specific gaps:** (1) open ISO 10218-2:2025 and confirm whether the Table A.2 values survived the consolidation unchanged, because citing the 2016 TS in 2026 without that check is a citation-hygiene failure; (2) read El Tumi's full text for the effect size behind R17; (3) verify the ISO 13482 clause that states no internationally recognised impact/pain limits existed at publication — I could not fetch it verbatim, and it is the sentence that justifies the whole import argument.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **C2 overlap — test methodology.** ISO/TS 15066 clause 6 delegates verification to ISO 10218-2:2011 Clause 6 and publishes no test procedure; the pressure-measurement-film method behind Melia 2019 is the practical route, and the standard's 1.4 × 1.4 cm rigid probe with 2 mm edge radius is a directly reusable test-fixture spec. Melia's finding that **peak** pressure rather than mean pressure drives pain onset means the campaign needs pressure-distribution film, not just a load cell.
- **Restitution and the inelastic assumption.** ISO's fully-inelastic two-body model is conservative for rigid arms; Vemula's 0.2–0.5 restitution range suggests an elastic soft surface that rebounds may not be bounded by it. Whether ISO's model is conservative for a *compliant* robot is genuinely open and worth a paragraph.
- **Pneumatic-specific hazards.** Burst, jet impingement, sudden depressurisation and stored-energy release on E-stop have no entry anywhere in the biomechanical limit tables. Relevant to A3.
- **Reflected inertia as a measurable quantity.** Every velocity limit here is a function of reflected inertia at the contact point, which varies with pose. Measuring or identifying it for a soft arm is non-trivial and hides in B2 (soft links conceal their own state) and D1.
- **Anthropomorphic test devices.** No elderly ATD exists. Hybrid III is a 50th-percentile male; Q3 is a small child. Validating an elder-care device with either is a documented limitation the team should state rather than hope nobody notices.
- **Sex differences.** Behrens found significant sex effects and weighted its model to a 70 %-male workforce; Melia found men had higher thresholds; Park used only men. Elder care skews female, and the cortical-thinning literature (Lillie 2016) says older women lose bone faster — so a male-derived limit is least conservative for the largest user group.
- **Trust and overtrust as a physical hazard.** Flagged as a gap in ISO 13482 — a user who overtrusts a compliant torso may lean harder than designed. Belongs to E2 but has a direct structural-load consequence for B1.
