# A2 — Series Elastic (SEA), Variable Stiffness (VSA) and Variable Impedance (VIA) Actuation

**Assignment:** if fully soft actuation cannot reach the torque target, what is the compliant-but-capable fallback that still earns the safety argument?

**Verification method.** Every entry below was confirmed against Crossref (`api.crossref.org`, authoritative DOI + author list + volume/pages) and, where an abstract was retrievable, OpenAlex (`api.openalex.org`). Where I had full text, I extracted numbers from the PDF myself and say so. Where I did not, the hard-numbers field says what I could and could not verify. Nothing here is reported from memory alone. Twelve distinct web searches were run (IROS/ICRA/T-Mech/T-RO/IJRR/RSS/RAM phrasings) before the session's search budget was exhausted; verification continued via Crossref/OpenAlex/direct PDF fetch.

**Ranking is by usefulness to THIS decision, not by prestige or citation count.**

### Crash-test HIC — Haddadin, Albu-Schäffer & Hirzinger, 2007
- **Citation:** S. Haddadin, A. Albu-Schäffer, G. Hirzinger, "Safety Evaluation of Physical Human-Robot Interaction via Crash-Testing," *Robotics: Science and Systems III*, Atlanta, GA, 2007, pp. 217–224.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.15607/RSS.2007.III.028 — verified by fetching and text-extracting the full PDF at https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss03/p28.pdf (also reissued as MIT Press chapter 10.7551/mitpress/7830.003.0029).
- **Open access:** yes (full PDF free at roboticsproceedings.org).
- **What it shows:** DLR LWR-III driven into certified ADAC automotive crash-test dummies (head, neck, chest) at controlled TCP speeds, scored against the EuroNCAP protocol. It is the only paper in this set that puts a compliant robot arm against a standardized injury protocol.
- **Hard numbers (all extracted by me from the PDF):** robot 14 kg, 7-DOF, ~4 kg effective mass at TCP, 1 kg aluminium impactor. TCP speeds tested 0.2 / 0.7 / 1.0 / 1.5 / 2.0 m/s. **HIC36 < 25 at 2 m/s**, against a EuroNCAP limit of 650 (= 5% probability of AIS≥3). Chest 3 ms criterion at 2 m/s reached **< 20% of the 72 g limit**. Simulation sweep (fitted to the measured impact data, Hunt-Crossley contact) over robot masses 1/4/10/50/100/500 kg and speeds to 10 m/s shows **HIC saturates with increasing robot mass** — beyond a point, more mass does not raise HIC. Quoted ISO-10218 collaborative limits: TCP ≤ 0.25 m/s, dynamic power ≤ 80 W, static force ≤ 150 N. Paper's own conclusion: "no robot whatever mass it has could become dangerous at 2 m/s by means of the presented criteria as long as clamping and impacts with sharp surfaces can be excluded."
- **Design relevance:** This is the paper that tells the design team where the safety argument actually lives. Free-flight blunt impact from a low-speed arm is **not** the dominant hazard and is already survivable; the hazard is **clamping / quasi-static constrained contact**, where inertia stops helping and only force limiting and release behaviour matter. For a torso a person leans on, essentially every dangerous contact is the constrained kind.
- **Evidence grade:** A — hardware, certified crash facility, standardized (EuroNCAP) protocol, instrumented dummies.
- **Limitations:** Dummies, not people; unconstrained frontal impacts only; sharp edges and tools explicitly excluded; head/neck/chest only, no lean-on or entrapment loading; the LWR-III is a serial arm, not a torso with a person's weight on it. The paper itself notes dummy response over-predicts real injury severity at these speeds.

### Compliance does not reduce HIC — Haddadin, Albu-Schäffer, Eiberger & Hirzinger, 2010
- **Citation:** S. Haddadin, A. Albu-Schäffer, O. Eiberger, G. Hirzinger, "New insights concerning intrinsic joint elasticity for safety," *Proc. IEEE/RSJ Int. Conf. on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS)*, Taipei, 2010, pp. 2181–2187.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/IROS.2010.5652037 — verified via Crossref (authors/pages/venue) and OpenAlex (full abstract retrieved); IEEE listing https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5652037/.
- **Open access:** partial (TUM mediaTUM record 1438559 is listed as the OA location; the landing page was behind a bot filter when I fetched it).
- **What it shows:** The most important counter-evidence in this whole topic. From the verified abstract: intrinsic joint elasticity **does not reduce HIC or impact forces** compared to conventional actuation that already has some joint elasticity, for full-scale robots — and the authors "elaborate conditions under which intrinsically compliant actuation is potentially *more dangerous* than rigid one."
- **Hard numbers:** none verified. I retrieved the abstract, not the full text; the numerical conditions and thresholds are in the body and I will not paraphrase numbers I did not read.
- **Design relevance:** Kills the lazy version of this project's safety argument. "We put springs in it, therefore it is safe" is not a defensible claim, and this is the paper a reviewer will hit you with. If the team goes SEA/VSA, the safety case must be built on **measured contact force limits and reaction behaviour**, not on the presence of a spring. Also flags the real compliant-actuator hazard: stored spring energy released on the human.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware experiments plus analysis; the collision detection/reaction schemes are experimentally verified, but there is no human or standardized-protocol testing.
- **Limitations:** "Full scale robots" is the stated scope; a low-speed, low-inertia assistive torso is exactly the regime where the conclusion might not hold, and the paper does not test that regime. Conference-length treatment.

### Foundational SEA — Pratt & Williamson, 1995
- **Citation:** G. A. Pratt, M. M. Williamson, "Series elastic actuators," *Proc. 1995 IEEE/RSJ Int. Conf. on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS): Human Robot Interaction and Cooperative Robots*, Pittsburgh, PA, vol. 1, pp. 399–406.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/IROS.1995.525827 — verified via Crossref (Pratt, G.A.; Williamson, M.M.; vol 1, pp. 399–406) and OpenAlex (abstract retrieved, 2211 citations).
- **Open access:** no (IEEE paywalled; the widely circulated copy is an author preprint).
- **What it shows:** States the entire tradeoff the design team is about to make. Deliberately softening the actuator-load interface buys shock tolerance, lower reflected inertia, accurate and stable force control, less damage to the environment, and energy storage — and **costs zero-motion force bandwidth**. Validated on a revolute SEA for the MIT Cog humanoid arms and a small planetary rover.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified. The abstract is qualitative; the bench results are in the body, which I did not access. Do not cite a bandwidth figure to this paper without reading it.
- **Design relevance:** The canonical citation for why a spring in series is a design choice and not a compromise. Also the source of the argument the team will lean on: for contact-rich "natural" tasks, zero-motion force bandwidth is the wrong figure of merit — which is exactly the argument this project needs, because an assistive torso does slow, high-force, contact-rich work.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware testbed, no humans, no standardized protocol.
- **Limitations:** 1995 componentry; the reflected-inertia argument was written for geared electric drives in a lab arm, not for a structure supporting a person's body weight; bandwidth loss claims predate modern disturbance-observer control which partly recovers it (see Paine below).

### UT-SEA, the design-tradeoff numbers — Paine, Oh & Sentis, 2014
- **Citation:** N. Paine, S. Oh, L. Sentis, "Design and Control Considerations for High-Performance Series Elastic Actuators," *IEEE/ASME Transactions on Mechatronics*, vol. 19, no. 3, pp. 1080–1091, June 2014. Companion with the same hardware: N. Paine, L. Sentis, "A new prismatic series elastic actuator with compact size and high performance," *Proc. IEEE Int. Conf. on Robotics and Biomimetics (ROBIO)*, 2012, pp. 1759--1766, DOI 10.1109/ROBIO.2012.6491222 (Crossref-verified).
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/TMECH.2013.2270435 — verified via Crossref (Paine, Nicholas; Oh, Sehoon; Sentis, Luis; vol 19, iss 3, pp 1080–1091) and OpenAlex. Numbers below were extracted by me from the companion full text at https://sites.utexas.edu/hcrl/files/2016/01/prismatic-sea-paine.pdf.
- **Open access:** partial — journal version paywalled, companion preprint and the dissertation are free.
- **What it shows:** A compact ball-screw SEA pushed to the performance limit of electric SEA, with a full spring-selection rationale (soft springs store more energy for a given force; stiffness chosen to maximise energy storage within a deflection budget) and force/position controllers combining PID, model-based and disturbance-observer structures.
- **Hard numbers (extracted by me from the companion PDF's spec table):** mass **1013 g** (2.23 lb); continuous force **848 N**; intermittent force **2800 N**; spring rate 138 N/mm per spring, **278 N/mm** effective (two precompressed springs); stroke 6 cm; max speed 32.5 cm/s; force resolution 0.31 N; **94 W/kg** measured power-to-weight and **77% mechanical efficiency** — against 64 W/kg cited for the prior SEA benchmark.
- **Design relevance:** This is the sizing reality check. A ~1 kg actuator delivering 848 N continuous is the realistic performance envelope for a good electric SEA today. If the torso needs N joints at that class, the mass budget follows directly, and it is not small. Also the reference for how spring rate is actually chosen — it is an energy-storage and deflection-resolution decision, not a "feels compliant" decision.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware, bench-validated at the limits of its capability, no humans.
- **Limitations:** Designed for legged locomotion (high force, fixed range of motion, passively cooled, short duty cycles). An assistive torso holds load **continuously**, which is a thermal problem this actuator was not characterised for. Prismatic, so it needs a linkage to make a joint, and the linkage moment arm is angle-dependent.

### The price of force bandwidth — Paine, 2014 (dissertation, Valkyrie SEA data)
- **Citation:** N. A. Paine, "High-Performance Series Elastic Actuation," Ph.D. dissertation, The University of Texas at Austin, 2014.
- **DOI/URL:** https://sites.utexas.edu/hcrl/wp-content/uploads/sites/3888/2016/01/nick-paine-thesis-dissertation-2014.pdf — verified by fetching and text-extracting the full PDF myself.
- **Open access:** yes.
- **What it shows:** Measured, on NASA Valkyrie's SEA joints, exactly what it costs to claw back the bandwidth that series elasticity gives away.
- **Hard numbers (extracted by me from the PDF):** Valkyrie elbow SEA **passive torque bandwidth 13 Hz**, extended to **70 Hz** closed-loop with PD feedback (factor 5.3). Torque tracking error ~**1.0 Nm on a 10 Nm reference**, with and without injected disturbances. Critically: raising closed-loop torque bandwidth from **30 Hz to 82 Hz raised average power consumption at the passive bandwidth frequency from 27 W to 97 W — a factor of 3.6 (+260%)**, with the corresponding motor heating. Passive system cutoff quoted as 11 Hz for the knee case.
- **Design relevance:** Settles the "just turn the gains up" argument before someone makes it. Every Hz of force-control bandwidth recovered above the spring's passive cutoff is paid for in current, heat and battery. For a torso that must hold a leaning adult for minutes at a time, thermal headroom is the binding constraint, so the spring should be chosen so the **passive** cutoff is already close to what the task needs — not soft-and-then-compensated.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware (Valkyrie), measured, no humans.
- **Limitations:** Humanoid limb joints under locomotion-style loading, not sustained static support. Dissertation, so peer review is the committee rather than a journal; the same lab's T-Mech and ICRA papers are the citable form for the design content.

### Distributed macro-mini (DM²) — Zinn, Roth, Khatib & Salisbury, 2004
- **Citation:** M. Zinn, B. Roth, O. Khatib, J. K. Salisbury, "A New Actuation Approach for Human Friendly Robot Design," *The International Journal of Robotics Research*, vol. 23, no. 4–5, pp. 379–398, April–May 2004.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1177/0278364904042193 — verified via Crossref; numbers below extracted by me from the full PDF at https://cs.stanford.edu/group/manips/publications/pdfs/Zinn_2004_IJRR.pdf.
- **Open access:** yes (author copy at Stanford).
- **What it shows:** Splits joint torque into a low-frequency component from a large base-mounted actuator (through a cable/SEA transmission) and a high-frequency component from a small low-inertia joint motor in parallel. The point is to strip reflected inertia out of the arm entirely rather than to hide it behind a spring.
- **Hard numbers (extracted by me from the PDF):** PUMA 560 at 1 m/s impact produces **HIC > 500**. To get that down to HIC ≤ 100 with padding alone would need **more than five inches of compliant cover**. The two-axis DM² prototype reduced **effective joint inertia by almost a factor of 10** and impact loading **by an order of magnitude**. Closed-loop position bandwidth **5 Hz with DM² vs 2 Hz with the base SEA alone**, with position tracking error cut by more than 10×. SEA output impedance reduction by control quoted at **10× to 100×**. Pneumatics noted as limited to <20 Hz control bandwidth; conventional gear reduction more than doubles effective inertia.
- **Design relevance:** Directly relevant to a **freestanding torso**, and probably underrated by the team. A torso has something an arm does not: a big, grounded, non-moving base you can bolt heavy actuators to. Moving actuator mass off the arms and into the torso — via cable or Bowden transmission — reduces the arms' reflected inertia, which is the one quantity that genuinely reduces impact severity. The five-inch-of-foam number is also the honest answer to "can't we just pad it?"
- **Evidence grade:** B for the prototype and bandwidth results (two-axis hardware testbed); **C for the impact-reduction claim**, which is computed from effective-inertia ellipsoids and a 2-DOF mass-spring HIC model, not measured against dummies. Do not present the order-of-magnitude figure as a measurement.
- **Limitations:** Impact severity is modelled, not measured; a 2-DOF planar testbed is a long way from a two-armed torso; and the HIC framing is exactly the metric Haddadin 2007/2010 shows is the wrong one at these speeds. Cable/Bowden routing adds friction and hysteresis (see Veneman).

### Safety-performance co-design — Bicchi & Tonietti, 2004
- **Citation:** A. Bicchi, G. Tonietti, "Fast and 'Soft-Arm' Tactics: Dealing with the Safety-Performance Tradeoff in Robot Arms Design and Control," *IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine*, vol. 11, no. 2, pp. 22–33, June 2004. Companion with the worked numbers: A. Bicchi, G. Tonietti, M. Bavaro, M. Piccigallo, "Variable Stiffness Actuators for Fast and Safe Motion Control," *Robotics Research: The Eleventh Int. Symp. (ISRR)*, Springer STAR, 2005, pp. 527–536.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/MRA.2004.1310939 (Crossref-verified: Bicchi, A.; Tonietti, G.; vol 11, iss 2, pp 22–33; abstract retrieved via OpenAlex). Companion 10.1007/11008941_56, Crossref-verified; numbers below extracted by me from the full PDF at https://www.centropiaggio.unipi.it/sites/default/files/anthropic-ISRR03.pdf.
- **Open access:** partial — magazine article paywalled, ISRR companion free.
- **What it shows:** Formalises the tradeoff as minimum-time optimal control subject to an injury-risk constraint, and shows that a variable-stiffness transmission dominates both the fixed-stiff and fixed-soft options because it can be stiff when slow and soft when fast.
- **Hard numbers (from the ISRR companion, extracted by me):** worked example with rotor inertia **M_rot = 1.2 kg**, link **M_link = 0.1 kg**, soft rubber cover **K_cov = 5 kN/m**, operator head **M_oper = 4 kg**: accepting **HIC = 100** implies a velocity ceiling of **v_safe ≈ 2 m/s** for the rigid-joint case. A second study sweeps transmission stiffness at a 10 m/s pre-impact link velocity and shows HIC tending to the rigid-link value as K→∞ and to the link-inertia-only value as K→0, with an **optimum intermediate transmission stiffness** for minimum-time performance under the safety constraint.
- **Design relevance:** This is the argument for VSA over fixed SEA, and also the argument against reflexively choosing "as soft as possible." There is an optimum, and softer than optimum makes the arm slow and floppy without buying more safety. If the team is choosing a spring rate, this is the framing to use.
- **Evidence grade:** C — analysis, optimal control and simulation; the hardware realisation is the separate VSA-II paper (Tonietti, Schiavi & Bicchi, ICRA 2005, DOI 10.1109/ROBOT.2005.1570172, Crossref-verified, one-link arm).
- **Limitations:** Single-DOF rest-to-rest motion, HIC as the objective, and a free-flight impact model. All three assumptions are wrong for a torso under a leaning person: the hazard is constrained and quasi-static, HIC does not apply, and the motion is not a fast point-to-point slew.

### SEA in a rehab exoskeleton, with measured bandwidth — Veneman et al., 2006
- **Citation:** J. F. Veneman, R. Ekkelenkamp, R. Kruidhof, F. C. T. van der Helm, H. van der Kooij, "A Series Elastic- and Bowden-Cable-Based Actuation System for Use as Torque Actuator in Exoskeleton-Type Robots," *The International Journal of Robotics Research*, vol. 25, no. 3, pp. 261–281, March 2006.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1177/0278364906063829 — verified via Crossref (full author list, vol/iss/pages) and OpenAlex (full abstract retrieved, 436 citations). Sage full text returned 403 to me.
- **Open access:** no.
- **What it shows:** The actuator behind LOPES. Motor sits off-board, torque goes to the joint through a flexible Bowden cable, and a series elastic element closes the force loop. Explicitly engineered so that the limb-worn structure stays light while the actuator still behaves as a decent torque source.
- **Hard numbers (from the verified abstract):** **11 Hz** force-tracking bandwidth over the full force range (requirement was 4 Hz); **20 Hz** over a smaller force range (requirement 12 Hz). Residual output impedance: peaks of about **0.7 Nm under 4 Hz imposed motion, < 2.5% of maximum force output**, attributed to stick friction in the Bowden cables. Spring-stiffness sweep showed **both too stiff and too compliant degrade performance** — a stiff spring lowers the maximum stable controller gain.
- **Design relevance:** The closest architectural analogue to what this project probably wants. It is DM²'s "put the mass at the base" idea, executed in a rehabilitation device with people in it, and with the transmission penalty honestly quantified (stick friction shows up as parasitic output impedance, which is exactly what a patient feels when leaning). The both-ends-are-bad spring result independently confirms Bicchi's optimum.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware, bench-characterised. (The LOPES system built on it was later used with patients, but that is downstream work, not this paper.)
- **Limitations:** Gait/lower limb, cyclic motion, not sustained static support; Bowden routing friction scales with path curvature and will be worse in a torso with two articulated arms; no injury or contact-force testing at all.

### SEA for human assistance, tested on a person — Kong, Bae & Tomizuka, 2012
- **Citation:** K. Kong, J. Bae, M. Tomizuka, "A Compact Rotary Series Elastic Actuator for Human Assistive Systems," *IEEE/ASME Transactions on Mechatronics*, vol. 17, no. 2, pp. 288–297, April 2012.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/TMECH.2010.2100046 — verified via Crossref (Kong, Kyoungchul; Bae, Joonbum; Tomizuka, Masayoshi; vol 17, iss 2, pp 288–297) and OpenAlex (abstract retrieved, 325 citations).
- **Open access:** no.
- **What it shows:** A rotary SEA sized specifically for human assistive devices, using a worm gear to get torque density in a small package, with a robust controller that explicitly models the worm gear friction (the actual amplification ratio departs from the nominal reduction ratio because of it). Includes experiments with a human subject.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified. I retrieved the abstract only; the torque, impedance and mass figures are in the body and I did not read them. The abstract states the requirements it targets — precise large torque, backdrivability, low output impedance, compactness — without numbers.
- **Design relevance:** Two things. First, it is one of the few SEA papers whose validation includes a human in the loop, which is the grade of evidence this project's safety argument needs. Second, the worm-gear finding is a direct warning: a non-backdrivable or friction-dominated reduction destroys the force-control fidelity that was the whole reason to fit a spring. If the team reaches for a worm or high-ratio harmonic drive to hit the torque target, the compliance argument degrades with it.
- **Evidence grade:** A — hardware validated including experiments with a human subject (not a standardized injury protocol).
- **Limitations:** Single joint, knee/limb assistance geometry; worm gearing is a poor fit for a torso joint that must yield when a person pushes it; no impact or clamping characterisation.

### Interaction control for compliant rehab robots — Yu, Huang, Chen, Pan & Guo, 2015
- **Citation:** H. Yu, S. Huang, G. Chen, Y. Pan, Z. Guo, "Human–Robot Interaction Control of Rehabilitation Robots With Series Elastic Actuators," *IEEE Transactions on Robotics*, vol. 31, no. 5, pp. 1089–1100, October 2015.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/TRO.2015.2457314 — verified via Crossref (full author list, vol/iss/pages) and OpenAlex (abstract retrieved, 360 citations).
- **Open access:** no.
- **What it shows:** Control design for an SEA-driven rehabilitation robot that must switch between a **human-in-charge mode** (robot must present near-zero output impedance and get out of the way) and a **force-control mode** (robot must track a commanded assistive force). Includes human-interaction compensation, friction compensation, a disturbance observer, and a stability proof for the closed loop under physical interaction. Verified in walking experiments with an ankle robot.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified — abstract only; the impedance and force-tracking figures are in the body.
- **Design relevance:** This is the control architecture the project actually needs, and it names the failure mode the team has probably not thought about: **physical interaction changes the controlled variables and can destabilise the system**. A person leaning on a torso is a large, unmodelled, time-varying impedance in the loop. A compliant actuator does not make that stable; a controller with a stability proof under interaction does. Also the right reference for the dual-mode requirement (transparent when the user leads, forceful when assisting).
- **Evidence grade:** A — hardware, walking experiments with human subjects.
- **Limitations:** Ankle, single DOF, gait. The stability proof is for that plant; a two-arm torso is multi-DOF with contact at arbitrary points, and the authors' claim that results extend "without much difficulty" is an assertion, not a demonstration.

### VSA design guide — Wolf et al., 2016
- **Citation:** S. Wolf, G. Grioli, O. Eiberger, W. Friedl, M. Grebenstein, H. Höppner, E. Burdet, D. G. Caldwell, R. Carloni, M. G. Catalano, D. Lefeber, S. Stramigioli, N. Tsagarakis, M. Van Damme, R. Van Ham, B. Vanderborght, L. C. Visser, A. Bicchi, A. Albu-Schäffer, "Variable Stiffness Actuators: Review on Design and Components," *IEEE/ASME Transactions on Mechatronics*, vol. 21, no. 5, pp. 2418–2430, October 2016.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/TMECH.2015.2501019 — verified via Crossref (full 19-author list, vol/iss/pages) and by fetching and text-extracting the accepted manuscript PDF at https://arpi.unipi.it/retrieve/handle/11568/842829/738859/VIA_review.pdf.
- **Open access:** yes (accepted manuscript in the Pisa and Twente repositories).
- **What it shows:** A design-process guide rather than a catalogue: how the task determines the required attributes, and how those attributes drive motor, sensor and spring selection. Its most useful content is the explicit statement of the design contradiction — **size, weight and cost pull against output power, potential energy capacity, stiffness range, efficiency and accuracy** — so any VSA is a point on that surface and cannot be good at everything.
- **Hard numbers:** **none reported** in a form I could extract. I read the full accepted manuscript; the treatment is analytical and qualitative (stiffness defined as k(φ,σ) = dτ/dφ, energy-capacity curves parameterised by stiffness setting) with no comparison table of device specifications. Anyone wanting per-device numbers should go to Grioli 2015 below.
- **Design relevance:** The right document to hand a student team before they design a joint, because it prevents the standard mistake of specifying a stiffness range without noticing what it costs in mass and efficiency. Useful for justifying *why* the project did not build a VSA, if that is the conclusion.
- **Evidence grade:** D — review.
- **Limitations:** Written by and for the VSA community, so it assumes a VSA is the answer. Nothing about sustained static load holding, nothing about safety validation, nothing about cost at student-project scale.

### VIA taxonomy — Vanderborght et al., 2013 (VIACTORS)
- **Citation:** B. Vanderborght, A. Albu-Schaeffer, A. Bicchi, E. Burdet, D. G. Caldwell, R. Carloni, M. Catalano, O. Eiberger, W. Friedl, G. Ganesh, M. Garabini, M. Grebenstein, G. Grioli, S. Haddadin, H. Hoppner, A. Jafari, M. Laffranchi, D. Lefeber, F. Petit, S. Stramigioli, N. Tsagarakis, M. Van Damme, R. Van Ham, L. C. Visser, S. Wolf, "Variable impedance actuators: A review," *Robotics and Autonomous Systems*, vol. 61, no. 12, pp. 1601–1614, December 2013.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1016/j.robot.2013.06.009 — verified via Crossref (full 25-author list, vol/iss/pages) and by fetching and text-extracting the accepted manuscript at https://www.centropiaggio.unipi.it/sites/default/files/2013_VABBCCCEFGGGGHHJLLPSTVVVW_RAS.pdf.
- **Open access:** yes (accepted manuscript).
- **What it shows:** The reference taxonomy and the principal published output of the EU VIACTORS project. Classifies by *how* variable stiffness and damping are obtained: active impedance by control, inherent compliance/damping, inertial actuators, and combinations.
- **Hard numbers:** **none reported** that I could extract from the accepted manuscript. It is a classification paper. (The most-cited work in this set — >1000 citations — and still it gives the design team no numbers, which is itself worth knowing.)
- **Design relevance:** Use it to place whatever the team builds on a recognised map, and to make the one decision it actually forces: **active impedance by control vs inherent physical compliance**. Active impedance is far cheaper in mass and parts but fails safe-open — power loss, sensor fault or a controller hang leaves a rigid arm. For a device a frail person leans on, that distinction is the whole argument.
- **Evidence grade:** D — review.
- **Limitations:** No performance data, no safety data, no cost data. A taxonomy, not a selection tool.

### VSA datasheets and benchmarking — Grioli et al., 2015
- **Citation:** G. Grioli, S. Wolf, M. Garabini, M. Catalano, E. Burdet, D. Caldwell, R. Carloni, W. Friedl, M. Grebenstein, M. Laffranchi, D. Lefeber, S. Stramigioli, N. Tsagarakis, M. van Damme, B. Vanderborght, A. Albu-Schaeffer, A. Bicchi, "Variable stiffness actuators: The user's point of view," *The International Journal of Robotics Research*, vol. 34, no. 6, pp. 727–743, 2015.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1177/0278364914566515 — verified via Crossref (full 17-author list, vol/iss/pages) and OpenAlex (abstract retrieved, listed OA at the Twente repository).
- **Open access:** partial (Twente repository record listed as best OA location; the file itself did not resolve for me).
- **What it shows:** The VIACTORS consortium's attempt to fix the problem that VSA data is scattered and non-comparable. Proposes a standard **VSA datasheet** — the parameter set an engineer needs to select a VSA the way they would select a conventional electric actuator, extending the usual motor parameters with quantities describing stiffness-change capability.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified. The paper defines *which* quantities should be reported (stiffness range, stiffness-change time, energy capacity, and so on); I could not retrieve the populated tables, so I will not quote device figures.
- **Design relevance:** This is the specification checklist for the fallback decision. If the team goes VSA, this is the list of quantities that must appear in the design report — and if a candidate off-the-shelf unit cannot supply them, that is a reason to reject it. Also the honest answer to "which VSA should we buy": the literature is not organised well enough to answer that, and this paper says so.
- **Evidence grade:** D — methodology/standardisation proposal.
- **Limitations:** Proposes a convention; it did not become one. No device in this project's price range is likely to publish a compliant datasheet.

### Low-cost modular VSA — Catalano et al., 2011 (VSA-Cube / VSA-CubeBot)
- **Citation:** M. G. Catalano, G. Grioli, M. Garabini, F. Bonomo, M. Mancini, N. Tsagarakis, A. Bicchi, "VSA-CubeBot: A modular variable stiffness platform for multiple degrees of freedom robots," *Proc. IEEE Int. Conf. on Robotics and Automation (ICRA)*, Shanghai, 2011, pp. 5090–5095.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/ICRA.2011.5980457 — verified via Crossref (full author list, pp. 5090–5095) and OpenAlex (abstract retrieved, 149 citations).
- **Open access:** partial (an OA record is listed at openaccessrepository.it; it returned 404 when I fetched it).
- **What it shows:** The only VSA in this set designed with **low cost as the primary goal**, built from hobby servos into a modular unit, with single-actuator characterisation (theoretical and experimental) and five units chained into an arm.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified — abstract only. The per-unit torque and stiffness-range figures are in the body and I did not read them. Expect hobby-servo class torque, i.e. roughly two orders of magnitude below what an assistive torso needs.
- **Design relevance:** The realistic *prototyping* path, not the product path. If the team wants to test a variable-stiffness control strategy and a safety protocol on a bench rig this semester, this is the architecture that fits a student budget. It is not a candidate for the torque target.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware, characterised, no humans.
- **Limitations:** Torque class is nowhere near a person leaning on a torso; hobby-servo internals mean poor repeatability, backlash and no meaningful duty rating; 2011 componentry.

### Torque-controlled rigid arm as the baseline — Albu-Schäffer et al., 2007 (DLR LWR → KUKA iiwa)
- **Citation:** A. Albu-Schäffer, S. Haddadin, Ch. Ott, A. Stemmer, T. Wimböck, G. Hirzinger, "The DLR lightweight robot: design and control concepts for robots in human environments," *Industrial Robot: An International Journal*, vol. 34, no. 5, pp. 376–385, 2007.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1108/01439910710774386 — verified via Crossref (full author list, vol/iss/pages) and OpenAlex (abstract retrieved, 622 citations; OA record at TUM mediaTUM 1438494).
- **Open access:** partial.
- **What it shows:** The mechatronic design that became the KUKA LBR iiwa: **joint torque sensing in every joint**, load-to-weight ratio 1:1, active vibration damping, sensitive collision detection, and compliant (impedance) control at joint and Cartesian level, made robust by passivity-based control and sensor redundancy.
- **Hard numbers:** **load-to-weight ratio 1:1** is the only figure in the verified abstract. Other specifications are in the body, which I did not read.
- **Design relevance:** The honest alternative the team must argue against. This machine is not intrinsically compliant — it is stiff hardware with torque sensors and impedance control — and it is the arm that actually got certified, sold, and deployed next to people. It is also the arm used in the crash tests above. Two consequences: (1) **torque sensing plus impedance control, without any spring, is a commercially validated route to safe pHRI**, and it is lighter and stiffer than SEA; (2) it fails unsafe on power loss, which is the specific gap a spring closes. That is the real trade, and the design report should state it in those terms.
- **Evidence grade:** B — hardware, extensively deployed; safety validation is in the separate crash-test papers above.
- **Limitations:** An industrial serial arm, not a torso; the safety case depends on continuously powered, correctly functioning sensing and control; joint torque sensors at this quality are expensive and outside a student BOM.

## Verdict for the design team

SEA is the right fallback spine; VSA is not, and you should say so explicitly in the report rather than leaving it as an open option. A fixed series spring with joint-level force control gets you the torque target, shock tolerance and a genuine mechanical fallback if power or control dies, for roughly one extra kilogram and one extra sensor per joint at the UT-SEA performance class (1013 g, 848 N continuous, 94 W/kg) — VSA buys you a tunable stiffness you will never have time to schedule usefully on a senior-design timeline, and pays for it with a second motor per joint, a second sensor chain, a nonlinear model, and a consortium's worth of review papers that still cannot give you a comparison table. Be honest that the safety argument does **not** come from the spring: Haddadin's crash tests put the LWR-III at HIC36 < 25 at 2 m/s against a limit of 650, and showed HIC saturates with robot mass, while his 2010 IROS paper states outright that intrinsic joint elasticity does not reduce HIC or impact force versus conventional actuation with some joint elasticity, and can be *more* dangerous. For a torso a person leans on, free-flight impact is the wrong hazard model entirely — every dangerous contact is the quasi-static constrained kind, where inertia stops mattering and the only things that save the user are a bounded continuous force, a detectable contact, and a reaction that releases rather than holds; the spring's real contribution is that it makes force **measurable from deflection** and **bounded when unpowered**, which is worth far more here than any impact-attenuation story. Budget the cost honestly: series elasticity gives away zero-motion force bandwidth (Pratt's own stated trade), and Paine's Valkyrie data shows buying it back is brutally expensive — 30 Hz to 82 Hz cost a 3.6× rise in power, 27 W to 97 W, as heat in an actuator that in your application must hold load continuously — so pick the spring so the *passive* cutoff already covers the task rather than planning to compensate, and note that Veneman measured 11 Hz full-range on a rehab actuator against a 4 Hz requirement, which tells you a torso assisting a human needs single-digit Hz, not 80. Steal the architecture, not just the actuator: this is a **freestanding** torso, so put the motors in the base and drive the arms through cable or Bowden transmission — Zinn's DM² cut effective joint inertia by ~10× that way, Veneman shipped it into a rehab exoskeleton, and it is the one move that reduces arm inertia instead of merely hiding it, at a measured cost of stick-friction output impedance around 0.7 Nm (<2.5% of full scale). Do not reach for a worm or high-ratio drive to close a torque gap — Kong's paper shows the gear friction corrupts the torque relationship and takes the backdrivability with it, and backdrivability is the property you are actually selling. The alternative you must argue against is not soft robotics, it is the DLR LWR/KUKA iiwa route of stiff structure plus joint torque sensing plus impedance control, which is the only approach in this literature that was actually certified and sold for work next to people; your one defensible reason to prefer SEA over it is that it fails rigid when unpowered and your users are frail, so make that the stated thesis. Finally, whatever you build, the evidence grade of your own safety claim is set by what you measure: nothing in this literature validates a leaned-on torso, so the project needs its own clamping-force and release-behaviour test, and citing HIC would be citing the wrong metric.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **ISO-10218 / ISO-TS 15066 power-and-force-limiting thresholds.** The RSS 2007 paper quotes ISO-10218's collaborative limits (TCP ≤ 0.25 m/s, ≤ 80 W, ≤ 150 N static) and dismisses them as heuristics not derived from biomechanics. The successor TS 15066 body-region pressure/force pain-onset limits is the standard a leaned-on torso would actually be assessed against, and no paper in my set applies it. Someone should own this — it likely belongs with whoever has the safety-standards item.
- **Compliant covers and skins as a separate safety layer.** Zinn's five-inches-of-padding-for-HIC-100 result and Haddadin's exclusion of sharp-edge injuries both point at surface design as an independent variable from actuation. There is a whole literature on optimising robot surface properties and on tactile/capacitive skins for pre-contact slowdown.
- **Collision detection and reaction without external sensors.** Momentum-observer-based detection appears in both Haddadin 2010 and Yu 2015 as the thing that actually makes compliant hardware safe. It is a control topic, not an actuator topic, but the safety argument is incomplete without it.
- **Gravity compensation by parallel/clutched springs.** The strongest legitimate use of parallel elasticity for this project is offloading the static holding torque of an extended arm, which shrinks the continuous-duty motor and its thermal problem. The CEA literature frames this as an energy question and never as a safety or sizing question.
- **Thermal and duty-cycle limits of electric actuators under sustained static load.** Every SEA benchmark in this set is a locomotion or cyclic-motion benchmark. Holding a leaning adult is a continuous-torque, near-zero-speed condition — the worst case for a BLDC motor — and I found no paper in this topic that characterises it.
- **Antagonistic tendon drives.** The DLR awiwi/Hand Arm System route (52 motors, exponential tendon springs) is arguably closer in spirit to a soft upper body than any geared SEA, but it is a different design family with its own tendon-slack and routing problems.
