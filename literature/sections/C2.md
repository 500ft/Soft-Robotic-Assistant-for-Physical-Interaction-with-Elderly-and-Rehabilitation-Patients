# C2 — Impact and contact safety test methods, instrumentation, and benchmarks

**Design decision informed:** How is "safe under uncontrolled contact" actually measured in a lab, with what rig, and against what benchmark?

**Scope note / honesty up front:** there is a mature, standardized measurement apparatus for *industrial cobot* contact safety (ISO/PAS 5672 + biofidelic pressure-force measurement devices, PFMDs). There is **no** equivalent published, validated impact-test protocol for *soft* robots, and **no** publicly readable impact-test protocol specific to ISO 13482 personal-care robots. Everything below either (a) transfers the cobot method, or (b) is a one-off academic rig. Sources are ranked by how directly they let this team build a rig.

---

### ISO/PAS 5672 — ISO/TC 299 WG8, 2023
- **Citation:** ISO/PAS 5672:2023, *Robotics — Collaborative applications — Test methods for measuring forces and pressures in human-robot contacts*, 1st edition, International Organization for Standardization, Geneva, November 2023, 41 pp.
- **DOI/URL:** https://www.iso.org/standard/82488.html — **I fetched the free 12-page preview PDF** at https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/82488/e9f2f066763b4189b18e2b9d3e6dccec/ISO-PAS-5672-2023.pdf (iso.org itself 403s automated fetch)
- **Open access:** partial — front matter, Scope, Terms, and Clause 4 (Overview/contact types) are free in the preview; Clauses 5–7 (measuring instrument design parameters, calibration, sensor characteristics, measurement procedure) are paywalled.
- **What it shows:** The only standard that actually specifies *how* to measure forces and pressures in human-robot contact, and what a PFMD must be. Defines the test as: replicate the contact situation by letting the robot collide with the PFMD, analyze, compare to biomechanical limits, pass/fail, document.
- **Hard numbers:** Contact classified on two axes — **load profile** (quasi-static vs dynamic) × **spatial configuration** (constrained vs unconstrained), producing contact types Push / Pinch / Impact / Crush. **Unconstrained requires ≥500 mm free distance to the nearest fixed obstacle** (referencing ISO 13854). Defines *biomechanical response curve* as contact force vs tissue deformation. Three transient-contact measurement architectures are recognized (fixed device; device on a linear sledge; device on a pendulum). Clause 5.2 "Design parameters", 5.3 "Calibration", 5.4.2/5.4.3 force and pressure sensor characteristics exist but their numeric content is behind the paywall — **I did not read those numbers and will not invent them.**
- **Design relevance:** This is the skeleton of the whole campaign. Adopt its contact taxonomy verbatim: your torso will produce *push* (person leans, quasi-static, unconstrained), *impact* (arm swings into a standing person, dynamic, unconstrained), and *pinch/crush* (arm traps a forearm against the torso shell or a table — dynamic/quasi-static, constrained). Your test matrix should be exactly those four cells, not "we hit it a few times."
- **Evidence grade:** **I** (standards document, consensus, not peer-reviewed research). The free preview is verifiable; the numeric clauses are not.
- **Limitations:** Costs roughly CHF 150–200 for the full text. Explicitly **does not apply to non-professional or medical robots** (§1) — an assistive/rehab torso for elderly users is arguably outside scope, though the document says the methods "can be applied in these areas, if deemed appropriate." That sentence is your justification for transfer, and you should quote it in the design review rather than pretending the standard covers you.

### Zimmermann-biofidel — Zimmermann, Huelke & Clermont, 2022
- **Citation:** Zimmermann, J., Huelke, M., & Clermont, M. (2022). Experimental comparison of biofidel measuring devices used for the validation of collaborative robotics applications. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 19(20), 13657.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph192013657 — **I fetched the PMC full text** at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9602772/ (mdpi.com 403s automated fetch)
- **Open access:** yes (MDPI CC-BY, mirrored in PMC).
- **What it shows:** Three commercial biofidelic PFMDs were run head-to-head on a linear motor and a pendulum. Authors are from IFA (the German statutory accident-insurance research institute) — i.e. the people who define the German cobot test practice. The point: nominally-equivalent certified devices disagree.
- **Hard numbers:** Spring elements (CC2) tested at **40 N/mm and 75 N/mm**; damping/compression layer (CC1) materials **SH70A at 7 mm, SH30A at 14 mm, SH10A at 21 mm**. Force recording **≥1 kHz**, filtered with a **100 Hz Butterworth low-pass**. Pendulum impact energies **0.5 J and 1 J**. Within-device repeatability (SD): **Device 1 = 1.4 %, Device 2 = 0.4 %, Device 3 = 0.6 %**. Between-device **peak-force differences up to 10 %** at identical settings. Device 1 moving mass ≈ **1.5 kg**. Linear motor: positioning accuracy **0.04 mm**, max holding force **1 kN**. Manufacturers anonymized for compliance reasons.
- **Design relevance:** Two things. (1) It gives you the *actual recipe* for a tissue surrogate: a stiff spring in series with a shore-hardness-specified elastomer pad, with body-region-specific spring rate. You can build this. (2) It sets your uncertainty floor — if certified instruments differ by 10 %, a home-built rig cannot claim better than ~±15–20 %, and your pass/fail criterion must have that margin baked in.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (hardware-validated instrument metrology, no human subjects — by design).
- **Limitations:** Devices anonymized, so you cannot buy "Device 2." Energies tested (0.5–1 J) are low; a leaning torso stores much more. Says nothing about soft-covered impactors, which is exactly your case.

### Svarny-skins — Svarny, Rozlivek, Rustler, Hoffmann et al., 2022
- **Citation:** Svarny, P., Rozlivek, J., Rustler, L., Hoffmann, M., Nicolis, D., Rocco, P., & Zanchettin, A. M. (2022). Effect of active and passive protective soft skins on collision forces in human–robot collaboration. *Robotics and Computer-Integrated Manufacturing*, 78, 102363.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rcim.2022.102363 — **I fetched and text-extracted the author-accepted PDF** at https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.09872 (the DOI line is printed on every page of that PDF, which is how I confirmed the venue)
- **Open access:** partial — publisher version paywalled, accepted manuscript free on arXiv, **dataset publicly released**.
- **What it shows:** The single most directly transferable paper here. It quantifies *how much a soft cover actually buys you* in measured collision force, separating the passive cushioning effect from the active sensing/stopping effect. This is the experiment your project is a variant of.
- **Hard numbers:** **2250 collision measurements**, three robots (UR10e, KUKA LBR iiwa 7R800, KUKA Cybertech) with and without AIRSKIN. **Transient collisions: passive skin lowered peak impact force by ~40 %.** Measuring device **CBSF-75-Basic**, spring constant **75 000 N/m (75 N/mm)**, range **500 N**, certified error **≤3 N**, collection frequency **1000 Hz**. Robot-side logging 800 Hz (UR) / 1000 Hz (KUKA). AIRSKIN pad approximated as **k_s = 3000 N/m with compressible thickness d_s = 16 mm**. Skin raised the ISO/TS-15066-permissible velocity from **0.26 → 0.35 m/s** (UR10e) and **0.32 → 0.43 m/s** (iiwa) against the 280 N transient limit; against the 140 N post-0.5 s clamping limit, **0.13 → 0.26 m/s** and **0.16 → 0.32 m/s**. Extra skin mass 1.8 kg. They propose an extension of the TS 15066 force–velocity formula that adds k_s and d_s.
- **Design relevance:** This is your headline comparison and your model. Measure your torso/arm bare vs covered, at matched velocities, and report the percentage reduction — that is a defensible, publishable claim. Their extended formula (adding cover stiffness and compressible thickness in series with the body-part spring) is the analytical model you should fit your own data to. Critically, it also shows the **quasi-static/clamping case is where soft covers stop helping** — which is exactly the failure mode of a torso a person leans on.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (large, well-instrumented hardware study; no human subjects).
- **Limitations:** Rigid industrial arms with a soft *cover*, not a soft *structure* — an arm whose links deform is a different mechanical system, and their two-body spring model may not hold. AIRSKIN is a commercial product (~thousands of EUR). Their 40 % figure is for that specific pad on those specific robots.

### Haddadin-crashtest — Haddadin, Albu-Schäffer & Hirzinger, 2007
- **Citation:** Haddadin, S., Albu-Schäffer, A., & Hirzinger, G. (2007). Safety evaluation of physical human-robot interaction via crash-testing. In *Robotics: Science and Systems III*, Atlanta, GA, pp. 217–224.
- **DOI/URL:** https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss03/p28.pdf — **fetched and text-extracted this PDF directly**
- **Open access:** yes (RSS proceedings are free).
- **What it shows:** The founding paper of automotive-to-robotics crash-test transfer, and the paper that killed the naive "robots are lethal at 1 m/s" simulation consensus. DLR LWR-III driven into a Hybrid III dummy at a certified ADAC crash-test facility, scored against EuroNCAP.
- **Hard numbers:** TCP velocities **0.2, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0 m/s**. **Hybrid III dummy instrumented at 20 kHz sampling**, signals filtered per CAC (Crash Analysis Criteria v1.6.1). Criteria and limits applied: **HIC₃₆ ≤ 650** (5 % probability of AIS ≥ 3); **3 ms criterion ≤ 72 g**; chest **compression criterion ≤ 22 mm**; **viscous criterion ≤ 0.5 m/s**; neck limits **1.9/3.1 kN shear, 2.7/3.3 kN tension, 42/57 Nm extension** at 0 ms. Results: **HIC₃₆ < 25 at 2 m/s** (essentially no head-injury risk); 3 ms criterion reached **<20 % of the 72 g limit at 2 m/s**. Free-space head impact is a **6–10 ms** event; measured peak at the aluminium impactor at 2 m/s was **2 kN and 35 g**. Simulated robot masses **1, 4, 10, 50, 100, 500 kg** — **HIC saturates with increasing robot mass**, velocity dominates. Also shows that even instantaneous collision detection cannot reduce a stiff-contact peak, because the peak arrives before any actuator can respond. Prior ISO-10218 heuristics (0.25 m/s, 80 W, 150 N) are shown to be un-derived from biomechanics.
- **Design relevance:** (1) Gives you the *criteria list* — HIC, 3 ms, chest compression, viscous, neck loads — and the numeric limits your campaign is measured against, all from a free PDF. (2) Gives you the key physical argument for your design review: **for blunt unconstrained impact, velocity and contact stiffness dominate; mass saturates.** So the defensible safety claim for a compliant torso is about *stiffness and speed*, not "it's light." (3) The detection-is-too-late result tells you not to claim sensing as your primary impact mitigation — the compliance is the mitigation.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (certified standardized test facility, standardized dummy and protocol).
- **Limitations:** ADAC crash-test facility time is far outside a senior-design budget. Rigid-link robot. Explicitly excludes sharp edges, clamping against rigid obstacles (which they flag as the genuinely dangerous case), and the head-only free-space geometry. 2007 — predates ISO/TS 15066 entirely.

### Behrens-limits — Behrens, Pliske, Umbreit, Piatek, Walcher & Elkmann, 2022
- **Citation:** Behrens, R., Pliske, G., Umbreit, M., Piatek, S., Walcher, F., & Elkmann, N. (2022). A statistical model to determine biomechanical limits for physically safe interactions with collaborative robots. *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, 8, 667818.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2021.667818 — **fetched** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8850785/
- **Open access:** yes (CC-BY).
- **What it shows:** The largest pain-onset study behind modern cobot limits, and — for C2 purposes — a fully-described *test rig* for both the quasi-static and impact cases.
- **Hard numbers:** **112 human subjects**, five groups, **28 body locations**, **~29 000 observations**, four scenarios (pinch/impact × blunt/semi-sharp). **Algometer:** KISTLER 9311B single-axis load cell, **±500 N**, **2.11 % max error**, loading rate **1 mm/s**. **Pendulum:** four-bar linkage, adjustable mass **1.9–20 kg**, **max impact velocity 1.25 m/s**, KISTLER 9327C three-axis load cell **±1 kN**. Example 75th-percentile limits: sternum **80 N pinch / 110 N impact** (blunt); shoulder joint **60 N pinch / 100 N impact** (semi-sharp); kneecap **160 N / 270 N**; forefinger pad **150 N / 390 N**. Pressure thresholds span **23 N/cm² (masticatory muscle) to 170 N/cm² (back of hand)**. Log-logistic CDF with Weibull regression, gender as covariate, 95 % CIs throughout.
- **Design relevance:** This is the **benchmark your numbers get compared to**, and it is the *only* source here that gives you a defensible target for a chest/sternum contact — which is precisely the contact a freestanding torso makes. Note **sternum blunt impact ≈ 110 N** is far below the generic 280 N transient limit people quote; if your torso can push on a chest, 110 N is your number, not 280 N. Their two-rig split (slow algometer for quasi-static, pendulum for impact) is the right architecture for your campaign and is cheaply imitable in concept.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (human subjects, standardized apparatus, large n, statistical model with CIs).
- **Limitations:** Subject population is a working-age industrial cohort. **Your users are elderly and rehab patients — thinner skin, osteopenic ribs, anticoagulants, reduced pain reporting.** These pain-onset limits are *not* injury limits and are *not* validated for your population; you must say so and add margin rather than quietly reusing the table. Building a calibrated four-bar pendulum with a ±1 kN triaxial Kistler is not a senior-design purchase.

### Svarny-CFM — Svarny, Rozlivek, Rustler & Hoffmann, 2021
- **Citation:** Svarny, P., Rozlivek, J., Rustler, L., & Hoffmann, M. (2021). 3D collision-force-map for safe human-robot collaboration. In *2021 IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation (ICRA)*, pp. 3829–3835.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA48506.2021.9561845 — **fetched arXiv PDF** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.01036 and **confirmed venue/pages via the Crossref API**
- **Open access:** partial (arXiv preprint free; IEEE version paywalled).
- **What it shows:** Impact force at a fixed commanded velocity varies by **more than 100 %** across the workspace, because effective mass and structural stiffness change with configuration. The ISO/TS 15066 closed-form force–velocity relation therefore under- or over-predicts depending on where you are.
- **Hard numbers:** Two robots (UR10e, KUKA LBR iiwa 7R800), **>400 impacts per robot**, **CBSF-75-Basic** device (**75 000 N/m**, **500 N** range, **≤3 N** error, **1000 Hz**), damping material chosen per TS 15066 to mimic **back of the non-dominant hand**. Velocities **0.20, 0.25, 0.30, 0.35, 0.40 m/s** downward. Grid: UR distance 0.52–0.89 m in 0.09 m steps × 5 heights from 0.14 m in 0.08 m steps; KUKA 0.56–0.86 m in 0.075 m steps. **Training set only 27 states (81 measurements) per robot** suffices to fit the model. 117 extra measurements verified rotational symmetry about joint 1. Samples exceeding the 500 N device limit were discarded. Reference TS 15066 permissible velocities computed as 0.26 m/s (UR) / 0.32 m/s (iiwa) at 280 N, and 0.13 / 0.16 m/s at 140 N.
- **Design relevance:** Directly sets your **sampling plan**. You cannot characterize a two-arm torso by hitting the device once — force depends on arm configuration and height. Their result that **27 well-chosen states is enough to fit a predictive map** is the single most budget-relevant methodological finding in this whole file: it turns an impossible full-factorial sweep into about a day of testing. Also: place your measurements at the height a seated or standing elderly user's chest/head actually occupies, not at a convenient bench height.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (large hardware dataset, no human subjects).
- **Limitations:** Rigid arms, single contact geometry, single body-region surrogate (hand). Device saturates at 500 N. Their conclusion is about *configuration dependence*, which for a soft arm may be swamped by material nonlinearity and pressurization state — an untested extrapolation.

### Kirschner-effmass — Kirschner, Mansfeld, Gomez Peña, Abdolshah & Haddadin, 2021
- **Citation:** Kirschner, R. J., Mansfeld, N., Gomez Peña, G., Abdolshah, S., & Haddadin, S. (2021). Notion on the correct use of the robot effective mass in the safety context and comments on ISO/TS 15066. In *2021 IEEE International Conference on Intelligence and Safety for Robotics (ISR)*, pp. 6–9.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/ISR50024.2021.9419495 — **fetched the free PDF** at http://iliad-project.eu/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Notion-on-the-correct-use.pdf and **confirmed DOI/pages via the Crossref API**
- **Open access:** partial (free author PDF via the ILIAD project site; IEEE version paywalled).
- **What it shows:** The only source here that gives a **practical experimental method to measure reflected/effective mass**: swing a passive physical pendulum of known effective mass into the robot, measure the pendulum's post-impact velocity, and invert conservation of momentum. It also shows the ISO/TS 15066 simplified effective-mass formula is badly wrong.
- **Hard numbers:** Pendulum: length to contact **l = 636 mm**, computed **effective mass at the contact point m_p,eff = 3.663 kg**. Franka Emika Panda, configuration 1: **measured m_r,exp = 3.018 ± 0.184 kg**, Khatib reflected-mass model **2.800 ± 0.005 kg**, **ISO/TS 15066 simplified value 5.222 kg** — errors of **7.8 %** (model vs experiment) and **73.0 %** (ISO/TS vs experiment). Configuration 2: **m_r,exp = 2.765 ± 0.062 kg** vs model **2.797 ± 0.003 kg**, **1.1 %** error. ISO/TS simplified masses: **6.3 kg** for KUKA LWR IV+, **5.545 kg** for Panda.
- **Design relevance:** This is your **reflected-inertia measurement method**, and it is genuinely cheap — a rigid pendulum, a known geometry, and velocity from video or an encoder. For a compliant arm you cannot compute effective mass from a rigid-body model, so measuring it is the only honest route. Report effective mass at several arm poses; it is the number that, with velocity, sets your predicted impact force.
- **Evidence grade:** **B/C** — hardware experiment, but explicitly "preliminary," tiny number of configurations, 4-page conference paper.
- **Limitations:** Rigid serial arms with joint-torque sensing. **For a soft arm the impact is not an instantaneous rigid-body momentum exchange** — the structure deforms over the contact duration, so the single-parameter "effective mass" abstraction may not be meaningful at all. Treat this as a method to try and possibly report as inapplicable, which would itself be a finding.

### Alhaddad-headrig — Alhaddad, Cabibihan, Hayek & Bonarini, 2019
- **Citation:** Alhaddad, A. Y., Cabibihan, J.-J., Hayek, A., & Bonarini, A. (2019). Safety experiments for small robots investigating the potential of soft materials in mitigating the harm to the head due to impacts. *SN Applied Sciences*, 1(5), 476.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s42452-019-0467-7 — **fetched the full-text PDF** at https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.09621 and **confirmed journal/volume/article via the Crossref API**
- **Open access:** yes (SN Applied Sciences is OA; preprint also on arXiv).
- **What it shows:** A complete, **genuinely low-cost, university-buildable head-impact rig**, plus a soft-material study whose main honest result is negative.
- **Hard numbers:** **3D-printed dummy head suspended on nylon-coated wire ropes** in a frame, with an embedded accelerometer read through a DAQ card. Pendulum impactor: 3D-printed cylinder, **10 cm diameter × 10 cm**, clay-filled to **0.4 kg**, faced with **5 × 8 cm** elastomer samples. Materials Ecoflex 00-30 and Dragon Skin FX-Pro (Smooth-On); **storage moduli 0.2, 0.3, 1.7 MPa** measured at 1 Hz by DMA (RSA-G2, frequency sweep 0.1–100 Hz). Thicknesses **1, 3, 5 mm**. Impact velocity bands **1.0–1.2, 1.6–2.0, 2.6–3.0 m/s** set by drop height. **Taguchi L9 orthogonal array, 27 main runs + 24 confirmation runs per noise level.** Velocity measured from **240 fps slow-motion video (Sony FDR-X1000V) analyzed in Tracker**; acquisition in LabVIEW, post-processing in MATLAB. **ANOVA found neither thickness nor storage modulus statistically significant (p < 0.05) in attenuating peak head acceleration**; only S/N-ratio optimization suggested 3–5 mm as better, confirmed in follow-up runs. Injury context cited: concussion reported from **31.8 g**; concussive impacts averaging **105 ± 27 g** vs subconcussive **26 ± 20 g**.
- **Design relevance:** **Copy this rig.** Suspended instrumented head/limb surrogate + gravity pendulum impactor + action-camera-plus-Tracker for velocity is the exact architecture a senior design team can build for low hundreds of dollars, and it appears in a peer-reviewed OA journal so you can cite it as precedent rather than defending an improvised setup. Also copy the **Taguchi/orthogonal-array design** — it is how you cover material × thickness × velocity in 27 runs instead of hundreds. And take the warning seriously: with a small n and a noisy rig, **plausible material effects came out statistically insignificant.** Design your power before you build.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (hardware, instrumented, statistically analyzed; surrogate not biofidelically validated against a standard).
- **Limitations:** Accelerometer model, range, and **sampling rate are not reported** — a real gap, since a 6–10 ms impact needs several kHz to capture a peak. Dummy head is not a Hybrid III and its biofidelity is asserted by reference to the authors' own earlier work, not independently validated. 0.4 kg impactor at ≤3 m/s is far below a torso-arm impact. Reports acceleration only, not HIC.

### Monteleone-balance — Monteleone, Negrello, Grioli, Catalano, Bicchi & Garabini, 2023
- **Citation:** Monteleone, S., Negrello, F., Grioli, G., Catalano, M. G., Bicchi, A., & Garabini, M. (2023). A method to benchmark the balance resilience of robots. *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, 9, 817870.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2022.817870 — **fetched** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9895781/
- **Open access:** yes (CC-BY), and **the testbed design is released open source**.
- **What it shows:** A complete benchmarking framework — 7 performance indicators, 5 protocols, and an actuated testbed — for how well a free-standing robot resists external pushes and impacts without falling.
- **Hard numbers:** Testbed: max impulsive force **1200 N**; max quasi-static/sinusoidal force **700 N** (350 N at long pendulum setting); max torque **250–350 Nm**; pendulum length adjustable **0.5–1.5 m**; added masses **0–15 kg**; oscillation up to **3–7 Hz**. Sensor resolution: torque **0.4–1 N**, force **0.8 N**, angular encoder **0.09°**. Five protocols: free-fall pendulum impulse (parameterized by energy E and impulse I), sinusoidal displacement, sinusoidal force, quasi-static displacement ramp at **1.5 cm/s**, quasi-static force ramp at **1 N/s**. Validation on the Alter-Ego robot: **>1100 experiments** — 410 runs / 41 conditions (P1), 260 / 26 (P2), 430 / 43 (P3), 10 each (P4, P5). Repeatability: mean SD **≈0.37 N·s** (impulse) and **≈0.2 mm** (displacement). **~4 days** of testing for the frontal experiments on one robot. Modular aluminium structure, commercial sensors, ROS-compatible industrial PC.
- **Design relevance:** This is the missing half of your campaign that the cobot literature does not cover. **Your torso is freestanding and a person will lean on it** — the dominant hazard is not "the arm hits someone," it is "the torso topples onto someone" or "it collapses when someone puts weight on it." This paper gives you the exact protocol pair you need: a **quasi-static force ramp (1 N/s)** to find the lean load at which your base loses stability, and a **free-fall pendulum impulse** to find the impulse it survives. The quasi-static ramp is cheap: a winch, a load cell, and a slow pull.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (hardware benchmark, validated on a real robot, open-source, no human subjects).
- **Limitations:** The full actuated testbed (1200 N, 250–350 Nm) is a serious machine — well beyond a senior-design build. The **degraded version is what you can afford**: a hand-winched quasi-static pull with a load cell and a string-pot, and a simple gravity pendulum. Developed for balancing bipeds/wheeled-inverted-pendulum robots, so the PIs assume an active balance controller; a passively-stable torso on a wide base needs its indicators reinterpreted. *(Reinterpretation is my inference, not theirs.)*

### Hartmann-review — Hartmann, Hamříková, Vysocký, Laciok & Bernatík, 2026
- **Citation:** Hartmann, D., Hamříková, K., Vysocký, A., Laciok, V., & Bernatík, A. (2026). Design, validation, and metrological limits of biofidelic instrumentation in PFL collaborative robotics: A systematic review of longitudinal trends and future paradigms. *Sensors*, 26(13), 3984.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/s26133984 — **fetched** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13363760/ (mdpi.com 403s)
- **Open access:** yes (CC-BY, PMC).
- **What it shows:** The current systematic map of PFMD instrumentation and — most usefully — a consolidated statement of **how wrong these instruments are**.
- **Hard numbers:** **68 publications** from **1099 screened**, covering **2011–2026**; 39 conference papers, 29 journal articles; evidence stratified as 21 high / 42 moderate / 5 low confidence. Publication peak in **2021**, a five-year lag after ISO/TS 15066 (2016). Sampling: **minimum 1000 Hz** required; contemporary systems at **10 kHz and 20 kHz**; transient spectral power concentrated **2–68 Hz**. Uncertainty: strain gauges **±15 N standard uncertainty**; pressure foils **up to ±10 %**, distorted by structural bulging; **force underestimation of 10–25 % during rapid transients**; sensor saturation/clipping documented at high-speed impact. Materials: damping ratio **ξ ≈ 0.07**; tempered steel springs (C67S) with **effective stiffness capped at 60 N/mm**; strain-rate-dependent silicone/urethane gels act as signal modulators. Names ISO/PAS 5672:2023 and ISO 10218-2:2025 as the governing documents.
- **Design relevance:** Your **error budget**, handed to you with citations. If certified instruments underestimate transient force by 10–25 % and pressure film carries ±10 %, then any pass/fail margin narrower than ~30 % is noise. Also settles your DAQ spec: **1 kHz is the floor, and you want ≥10 kHz** given a 6–10 ms impact (Haddadin). The 2–68 Hz spectral content result is what justifies the 100 Hz Butterworth filter everyone uses.
- **Evidence grade:** **D** (systematic review — high quality, but secondary; graded D per this project's rubric, which is about study type, not merit).
- **Limitations:** Reports no costs. Aggregates numbers from heterogeneous rigs, so the uncertainty figures are indicative rather than a specification. Entirely industrial-cobot framed; soft robots appear only at the margins.

### DAntona-VSITD — D'Antona, Farsoni, Rizzi & Bonfè, 2025
- **Citation:** D'Antona, A., Farsoni, S., Rizzi, J., & Bonfè, M. (2025). A variable stiffness system for impact analysis in collaborative robotics applications with FPGA-based force and pressure data acquisition. *Sensors*, 25(13), 3913.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/s25133913 — **fetched** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12251810/
- **Open access:** yes (CC-BY, PMC).
- **What it shows:** A research-built alternative to a commercial PFMD: one adjustable-stiffness mechanism that can be dialled to several body regions, with a high-rate pressure-mapping array — i.e. the design you would copy if you build rather than buy.
- **Hard numbers:** Variable Stiffness Impact Testing Device (VSITD). Stiffness **10–60 N/mm** usable (theoretical max **80 N/mm** at 80° spring rotation); validated settings 10, 25, 30, 35, 40, 50, 55, 60, 75 N/mm; **saturation above ~60 N/mm**, so stiffer regions (back, thigh, shoulder) are out of reach. Pressure: **32 × 32 FSR matrix over 10 × 10 cm²** (XactFSR polymer on PET). DAQ: **Xilinx Artix-7 FPGA**, LTC2311-14 ADC (**±4.1 V differential**), **5 Msps nominal**, **~10–20 kHz effective per element**, **<1 ms** analog-to-digital latency, FT600 USB 3.0 up to 200 MB/s. Force reference: PCB Piezotronics piezoelectric load cells with a 482C conditioner, cross-checked against an **ATI Nano17** F/T sensor. ## A testable campaign sketch

Everything marked **[cited]** comes from a source above. Everything marked **[inferred]** is my engineering judgement and must be defended on its own, not by citation.

**Rig 1 — Biofidelic contact surrogate (the core instrument).**
Build the two-stage stack that IFA describes and Zimmermann characterizes **[cited]**: rigid impact plate → calibrated compression spring → shore-specified elastomer pad → uniaxial load cell → rigid backing. Spring rate selected per body region from the CoboSafe CBSF range, **10–150 N/mm** **[cited, industry]**; for chest/abdomen/upper-arm contacts use the low end, and note that D'Antona's research device saturates above **~60 N/mm** **[cited]**, which is fine because your target regions are soft. Pad per Zimmermann: **SH10A at 21 mm, SH30A at 14 mm, or SH70A at 7 mm** **[cited]**. Sample force at **≥1 kHz**, filter with a **100 Hz Butterworth low-pass** **[cited]** — and prefer **10 kHz** if your DAQ allows, since transients are **6–10 ms** events **[cited, Haddadin]** and the review documents **10–25 % force underestimation during rapid transients** **[cited, Hartmann]**. Add pressure-measurement film at the contact face for distribution **[cited, GTE]**; an FSR matrix gives you pressure-vs-time but needs per-pixel calibration you probably will not do **[cited, D'Antona]** — report it as qualitative distribution only **[inferred]**.

**Rig 2 — Gravity pendulum impactor.**
Copy Alhaddad: suspended instrumented surrogate in a frame, pendulum impactor of known mass, drop height sets velocity, **240 fps video analyzed in Tracker** to measure the actual impact velocity **[cited]**. Use the same pendulum, run in reverse, to measure your arm's **effective mass** by Kirschner's momentum-inversion method **[cited]** — note the pendulum's own effective mass at the contact point must be computed from its inertia and contact radius (Kirschner: **l = 636 mm, m_p,eff = 3.663 kg**) **[cited]**. Whether "effective mass" is even a meaningful parameter for a deforming soft arm is an open question and a legitimate finding either way **[inferred]**.

**Rig 3 — Quasi-static lean/push rig.**
Take Monteleone's quasi-static protocols: **force ramp at 1 N/s** and **displacement ramp at 1.5 cm/s** **[cited]**, applied at chest height to the torso, measuring applied force, torso displacement, and the tipping/collapse point. A hand winch, an inline load cell, and a string potentiometer will do it **[inferred]**. This is the test that addresses "an elderly person puts their weight on it," which is the hazard the cobot literature does not cover **[inferred]**.

**Rig 4 — Pressurized-system integrity (if the design is fluidic).**
Abidi & Cianchetti's hazard list — burst, jet impingement, entrapment **[cited]** — implies a proof-pressure test and a sustained-load hold. Neither has a published protocol in the sources I found; you would be writing it yourself **[inferred]**.

**Measurement matrix.**
Use the ISO/PAS 5672 taxonomy: **push (quasi-static, unconstrained), impact (dynamic, unconstrained), pinch (quasi-static, constrained), crush (dynamic, constrained)** **[cited]**, with **≥500 mm clearance** to qualify as unconstrained **[cited]**. Sample arm configuration and height, not just velocity — Svarny showed force varies **>100 %** across a workspace, and that **27 well-chosen states** are enough to fit a predictive map **[cited]**. Sweep velocity over roughly **0.1–0.5 m/s**, the band where the cobot limits bite **[cited, Svarny]**; add a **1–3 m/s** band on the pendulum rig if you want to characterize the worst-case runaway **[cited, Alhaddad]**. Use an orthogonal-array design (L9/L27) to keep the run count sane **[cited, Alhaddad]**.

**Headline comparison.**
Bare structure vs compliant-covered structure, matched velocities, same surrogate. Svarny's **~40 % transient force reduction** from passive skin is your benchmark to beat or fail to beat **[cited]**. Fit their extended force–velocity model that adds cover stiffness **k_s** and compressible thickness **d_s** in series with the body-part spring **[cited]**.

**Pass/fail criteria.**
Against the measured pain-onset limits, **not** the generic 280 N: for chest/sternum blunt impact the relevant figure is **~110 N impact / 80 N pinch**, and shoulder is **~100 N / 60 N** **[cited, Behrens]**. Quasi-static limits are the tighter case (ISO/TS 15066 applies **280 N for the first 0.5 s, 140 N after** **[cited, Svarny]**). For head impact, if you can generate one: **HIC₃₆ ≤ 650, 3 ms criterion ≤ 72 g** **[cited, Haddadin]** — though Haddadin's result that a light arm produces **HIC₃₆ < 25 at 2 m/s** **[cited]** means you will pass those trivially and should not present it as an achievement. **Apply a margin of at least 30 % below the limit** **[inferred]**, justified by the stacked uncertainties: **±10 % between certified devices** **[cited, Zimmermann]**, **10–25 % transient underestimation** and **±10 % on pressure film** **[cited, Hartmann]**, plus an uncharacterized home-built rig. Then state explicitly that pain-onset limits from a **working-age cohort of 112** **[cited, Behrens]** are **not** validated for elderly or rehab patients **[inferred]** — this is the most important caveat in your entire report and the reviewers will look for it.

**Affordable vs needs a certified lab.**
*Affordable (low hundreds to low thousands of USD):* spring-and-pad surrogate, uniaxial load cell + ≥1 kHz DAQ, gravity pendulum, 240 fps action camera + Tracker **[cited, Alhaddad]**, quasi-static winch pull with load cell, pressure-measurement film (single-use sheets), accelerometer-instrumented 3D-printed surrogate, orthogonal-array test design, open data release **[cited, Bonsignorio]**.
*Needs a lab or a real budget:* certified CoboSafe/KDMG-KOLROBOT PFMD **[cited, GTE/IFA]**; Hybrid III dummy at 20 kHz in an ADAC-class facility **[cited, Haddadin]**; Kistler ±1 kN triaxial load cells and a calibrated four-bar pendulum **[cited, Behrens]**; Monteleone's 1200 N actuated balance testbed **[cited]**; FPGA/piezo pressure-mapping array **[cited, D'Antona]**; DMA for material storage modulus **[cited, Alhaddad]** — though a university materials lab likely has one.
**Check first whether NYU already owns a PFMD or a Hybrid III component** before building anything **[inferred]** — that single email changes the whole plan.

## Verdict for the design team

You can build a credible impact-safety rig for a few hundred dollars, and there is peer-reviewed precedent for every piece of it — the spring-and-elastomer surrogate, the gravity pendulum, the high-frame-rate-video velocity measurement, and the orthogonal-array test design are all published and all cheap. What you cannot do is claim compliance: you have no certified PFMD, no biofidelic calibration against published response corridors, and no accredited lab, so every number you produce is a *relative* comparison, not a *pass*. Lean into that — the strongest result available to you is bare-vs-covered at matched velocity, reported as a percentage force reduction against Svarny's ~40 % benchmark, with your raw data released. Your uncertainty will be worse than the ±10 % that certified devices disagree by and the 10–25 % underestimation those devices already suffer on transients, so do not report three significant figures and do not set a pass/fail margin tighter than about 30 %. The most important number in this file is not 280 N: it is Behrens's ~110 N for blunt impact on the sternum, because a torso contacts chests, and anyone quoting 280 N in your design review has read the wrong row of the wrong table. Two gaps will not be closed by the cobot literature at all, and they are the ones that will actually hurt someone — the quasi-static lean case, where Svarny showed soft covers stop helping, and the pressurized-structure failure modes Abidi and Cianchetti list, so budget for a lean-load ramp and a burst/proof-pressure test or accept that your campaign has a hole in it. Be equally blunt about the population: every limit you will cite comes from working-age subjects reporting pain onset, and your users are elderly and rehabilitation patients with thinner skin, weaker bone, and blunted pain reporting, which means the published numbers are an upper bound you should deliberately undercut, not a target you should hit. Finally, there is no published, freely readable impact-test protocol for ISO 13482 personal-care robots — I looked, and it is not there — so write your risk assessment first and derive your test matrix from it, because under ISO/TR 23482-1 that is what a manufacturer is expected to do anyway.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **C1 overlap (injury biomechanics and limit values).** Behrens 2022's 28-body-region pain-onset table and the ISO/TS 15066 limits are substantively C1's territory; I kept them here only because the test rigs are inseparable from the limits they were built to measure. C1 should own the numbers.
- **Airbag-style impulse spreading and dissipative covers.** Svarny's finding that a soft cover works by prolonging contact duration, and the arXiv work on non-Newtonian shear-thickening protectors on a 42 kg humanoid surviving 3 m drops (arXiv:2601.02857 — I saw the abstract but did **not** verify the full text, so treat that as a lead, not a citation), point to a materials-selection question that belongs with B1.
- **Collision detection and reaction control.** Haddadin's result that detection cannot beat a stiff-contact peak, and Svarny's finding that the active skin contributed almost nothing beyond the passive padding, are really D1 findings about what control can and cannot buy you.
- **Robot skins as sensors.** CoboSkin (variable-stiffness sensing skin) and the FSR/tactile-array work surfaced repeatedly and belong to B2.
- **Balance and tip-over of freestanding robots.** Monteleone's benchmark is arguably its own item — it is not "contact safety" as the cobot world defines it, but for a freestanding torso it may be the dominant hazard. Worth raising as a candidate item the outline does not have.
- **Aerial/impact-resilient compliant arms.** Several hits on compliant arms for aerial physical interaction use similar impact rigs; different domain, possibly useful rig ideas, not pursued.
- **Toy safety standards (EN 71 / ISO 8124).** Alhaddad points out that small social robots fall under toy standards rather than robot standards. Irrelevant at your scale, but a reminder that device classification determines which test regime applies — and yours is genuinely ambiguous.
