# B2 — Soft and compliant sensing for safe contact

**Design decision informed:** How does the robot know it is touching a person, and know where its own soft limbs are, when soft links hide their own state?

**Date:** 2026-09-17. **Method:** 10 distinct web searches (Google Scholar, arXiv, IEEE, Wiley, ACS, PMC, RSC, TUM/DLR repositories). Every citation below was verified either by fetching a page that displays it or by resolving the DOI through the Crossref API (`api.crossref.org/works/<doi>`), which returns authors/venue/volume/pages. Where a paper is paywalled and I could only verify metadata, the hard-numbers field says so explicitly rather than guessing.

**Blunt framing up front:** almost nothing in this literature was built for a *freestanding torso that a person puts weight on*. The tactile-skin literature is about arms that must not hurt you. The soft-proprioception literature is about fingers and 200 mm continuum segments. The gap between those and "an 80 kg human leans on a compliant trunk" is the single biggest transfer risk in this whole topic, and it is not addressed anywhere I found.

### CushSense — Xu et al., 2024
- **Citation:** Boxin Xu, Luoyan Zhong, Grace Zhang, Xiaoyu Liang, Diego Virtue, Rishabh Madan, Tapomayukh Bhattacharjee. "CushSense: Soft, Stretchable, and Comfortable Tactile-Sensing Skin for Physical Human-Robot Interaction." arXiv:2405.03155 [cs.RO], 2024. (Also presented in the IROS 2024 cycle.)
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2405.03155 — **fetched** `arxiv.org/abs/2405.03155` and `arxiv.org/html/2405.03155v1`
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** A fabric-and-hyperelastic-polymer capacitive tactile skin designed explicitly for *caregiving* contact (limb manipulation, bed bathing), built cheaply and validated with a human-subject comfort/safety study rather than only a load cell.
- **Hard numbers:** taxel 3 cm × 3 cm, 9-layer stack; 56 taxels covering four sections of a robot arm; force range to ~55 N, characterized 0–40 N; relative error 0.58 %; hysteresis ~3 N ≈ 5.4 % of range; output rate 100–400 Hz (FDC1004 capacitance-to-digital converter); 0.054 % accuracy drop after 1000 interaction cycles; ~$7 per taxel; user study n = 15 (9 F / 6 M, mean age 22.3), 14/15 preferred CushSense, p < 0.001 for both comfort and perceived safety.
- **Design relevance:** This is the closest published thing to what this project should actually build, and it is open-sourced. It sets the realistic bar for a senior-design skin: 3 cm taxels, ~5 % hysteresis, sub-$10/channel, human-rated comfort. Use it as the baseline design and justify any deviation.
- **Evidence grade:** A (hardware + human subjects, though the "safety" outcome is perceptual, not injury-biomechanical)
- **Limitations:** Validated for a *robot manipulating a limp human limb*, not a human loading the robot. 55 N full scale is far below what a leaning adult applies (a 70 kg person shifting 30 % of body weight is ~200 N). 5.4 % hysteresis on a compliant substrate that is also carrying structural load is untested. Mean subject age 22 — no elderly or patient participants at all.

### Comprehensive robot skin — Cheng et al., 2019
- **Citation:** Gordon Cheng, Emmanuel Dean-Leon, Florian Bergner, Julio Rogelio Guadarrama Olvera, Quentin Leboutet, Philipp Mittendorfer. "A Comprehensive Realization of Robot Skin: Sensors, Sensing, Control, and Applications." *Proceedings of the IEEE*, 107(10):2034–2051, 2019.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/JPROC.2019.2933348 — **fetched** the Proceedings of the IEEE October 2019 issue page; metadata re-confirmed via Crossref
- **Open access:** no (IEEE paywalled; the TUM group's news pages and the earlier T-RO paper carry much of the content)
- **What it shows:** The end-to-end account of the HEX-o-skin / CellulARSkin line: multimodal hexagonal skin cells (proximity, normal force, acceleration, temperature) with local microprocessors, self-organizing network topology, event-driven transport, and whole-body control built on top. It is the reference for "what a real whole-body skin costs you in engineering."
- **Hard numbers:** deployment figure widely reported for this line: 1260 skin cells on the REEM-C humanoid = 11,340 individual sensors, each cell roughly two-euro-coin sized (~3 cm across) with 4 modalities. Per-cell force resolution, latency and drift figures are behind the paywall and I did not verify them — **treat as none reported here.**
- **Design relevance:** The architecture lesson, not the parts list. It tells the team that whole-body skin is a *networking and data-rate* problem as much as a transducer problem: put a microcontroller in each patch, use event-driven reporting (only send on change), and design the topology so a cut wire re-routes. That decision has to be made before any taxels are fabricated.
- **Evidence grade:** B (extensive hardware validation across several robots; no human-subject safety trial in this paper)
- **Limitations:** Rigid-link humanoids. The cells are stiff PCB islands in an elastomer — on a torso that must itself deform under a lean, rigid islands become pressure concentrators and their accelerometer-based self-organization assumes the body geometry does not change. Also: 1260 cells is a multi-person-year build, not a senior design semester.

### iCub capacitive skin — Maiolino et al., 2013
- **Citation:** Perla Maiolino, Marco Maggiali, Giorgio Cannata, Giorgio Metta, Lorenzo Natale. "A Flexible and Robust Large Scale Capacitive Tactile System for Robots." *IEEE Sensors Journal*, 13(10):3910–3917, 2013.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/JSEN.2013.2258149 — **fetched** the open preprint at `ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1411.6837` (arXiv:1411.6837), metadata confirmed via Crossref
- **Open access:** partial (paywalled at IEEE; full author preprint on arXiv)
- **What it shows:** The most numerically honest large-area tactile skin paper available: triangular fPCB modules with a 3D-fabric dielectric instead of elastomer, with embedded thermal sensors specifically because capacitive taxels drift with temperature.
- **Hard numbers:** 10 pressure taxels + 2 temperature sensors per triangular module; taxel area 15.20 mm² (up from 12.56 mm²); pressure range 2–160 kPa with two linear regimes (2–45 kPa at 2.50 fF/kPa, 65–160 kPa at 0.86 fF/kPa); hysteresis ≈ 5 % max (9.1 fF at 28.6 kPa); **stress-relaxation time constant τ ≈ 1 h 18 min**; temperature compensation validated 15–40 °C; 16-bit CDC, 8-bit dynamic range in deployment; output rate 25 Hz; up to 16 modules per bus; full iCub deployment 1868 taxels (104 hands, 610 arms, 440 torso); 1500 taxels on a Barrett WAM.
- **Design relevance:** The τ ≈ 78 min relaxation constant is the number to put on a slide. It means any elastomer/fabric-dielectric capacitive skin will *slowly change its zero* under a sustained load — exactly the load case of a patient leaning for two minutes. Sustained-contact force magnitude from a soft capacitive skin is not trustworthy; contact *presence* and *location* are. Design the control law so sustained force comes from a different sensor (joint torque or a load cell in the base) and the skin only supplies where and when.
- **Evidence grade:** B (thorough bench characterization, robot deployment, no human-subject safety test)
- **Limitations:** 25 Hz is marginal for reacting to an impact; the transducer is a stiff triangular fPCB that will not tolerate the strains a genuinely soft link undergoes; and the two-regime nonlinearity means a single-slope calibration is wrong over most of the range.

### HEX-o-skin — Mittendorfer & Cheng, 2011
- **Citation:** Philipp Mittendorfer, Gordon Cheng. "Humanoid Multimodal Tactile-Sensing Modules." *IEEE Transactions on Robotics*, 27(3):401–410, 2011.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2011.2106330 — **fetched** the TUM research-portal record `portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/humanoid-multimodal-tactile-sensing-modules/`; Crossref-confirmed
- **Open access:** no (TUM portal record and figures are public; PDF is not)
- **What it shows:** The founding HEX-o-skin paper. A hexagonal PCB cell carrying temperature, acceleration and proximity sensors — deliberately *multimodal* rather than a pressure array — embedded in a rapid-prototyped elastomer and wired to neighbours through only four non-crossing conductors, with active re-routing around damaged links.
- **Hard numbers:** four sensing modalities per cell; power+data between neighbours reduced to 4 non-crossing wires; redundant connection to neighbouring modules. Specific cell dimensions, sampling rates and force thresholds are not in the publicly readable record — **none verified.**
- **Design relevance:** Two transferable decisions. (a) Multimodality beats pressure-only: the accelerometer catches taps and impacts that a slow pressure taxel misses, the proximity channel gives pre-touch, and they come on the same board. (b) The four-wire daisy chain is the answer to "how do I wire 200 taxels across two moving arms without a harness the size of the arm." Copy the wiring topology even if you do not copy the transducers.
- **Evidence grade:** B
- **Limitations:** 2011 hardware, rigid PCB islands, and the whole design presumes a rigid substrate underneath to mount to. It also has no normal-force transducer worth the name in this generation — light touch via proximity, impact via accelerometer, but not "how many newtons is this person applying."

### Proximity perception survey — Escaida Navarro et al., 2022
- **Citation:** Stefan Escaida Navarro, Stephan Mühlbacher-Karrer, Hosam Alagi, Hubert Zangl, Keisuke Koyama, Björn Hein, Christian Duriez, Joshua R. Smith. "Proximity Perception in Human-Centered Robotics: A Survey on Sensing Systems and Applications." *IEEE Transactions on Robotics*, 38(3):1599–1620, 2022.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2021.3111786 — **fetched** the full text at `ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.07206` (arXiv:2108.07206); Crossref-confirmed for volume/pages
- **Open access:** partial (author version on arXiv)
- **What it shows:** The only place with a defensible side-by-side of proximity modalities and their actual ranges, and it explicitly ties them to ISO/TS 15066 speed-and-separation monitoring.
- **Hard numbers:** **Capacitive:** 30–40 cm on finger-scale electrodes, up to ~30 cm for 50×100 mm² large-area electrodes, update 20–125 Hz (some kHz), human coupling ~100 pF, strong on conductors and weak on insulators. **Optical reflected-intensity:** 1–300 mm, >1 kHz update, sub-mm resolution, <1 ms measurement. **Optical ToF (VL6180X class):** 10–100 mm, 1 mm resolution, ±1–2 mm repeatability, 7 ms to tens of ms measurement, ~10 cm dead zone. **Triangulation:** to 1.5 m with ~20 cm dead zone. **Inductive:** 150–300 mm, 25 kHz. **Radar FMCW:** 60 GHz / 160 GHz, simultaneous range + velocity. **Acoustic seashell effect:** ~4 mm. **Worked ISO/TS 15066 example (UR10e):** 500 Hz control (2 ms cycle), 4 ms reaction, 100 ms stopping time → 0.236 m protective separation distance excluding human position uncertainty. Survey frames 0–50 cm as the critical HRI band and notes Hall's ~45 cm intimate-space threshold.
- **Design relevance:** This is where the proximity spec comes from. Capacitive is the right pick for this robot: a human body is a big capacitive target, 20–30 cm range on realistically sized electrodes covers the approach envelope, and the electrodes can be printed on the same flexible substrate as the tactile layer. The UR10e worked example also gives the honest reality check — even a fast industrial arm needs 24 cm of separation, and a compliant arm's "stopping distance" is dominated by its own compliance, not its controller.
- **Evidence grade:** D (survey), but the numbers are drawn from graded primary sources and it is the right citation for a spec table
- **Limitations:** Survey of *rigid* manipulators. Capacitive proximity electrodes on a stretching soft link change their own geometry and therefore their baseline capacitance — the survey does not treat the coupled proximity-plus-strain case at all. Self-capacitance also gets swamped by the robot's own moving parts on a torso where arms pass near the trunk.

### GenTact-Prox — Kohlbrenner et al., 2026
- **Citation:** Carson Kohlbrenner, Anna Soukhovei, Caleb Escobedo, Nataliya Nechyporenko, Alessandro Roncone. "Design, Mapping, and Contact Anticipation with 3D-printed Whole-Body Tactile and Proximity Sensors." arXiv:2603.04714v2, 2026.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04714 — **fetched** `arxiv.org/html/2603.04714v2`
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** Fully 3D-printed skin units (conductive PLA electrodes in a PLA "dermis") that do self-capacitance for both direct contact and proximity out to 18 cm, procedurally generated to fit an arbitrary robot's morphology, with an ensemble-ML map of the sensable volume ("perisensory space").
- **Hard numbers:** 5 skin units, 17 sensors total on a Franka Research 3; detection range up to 18 cm, per-sensor spread 2.6–18 cm; update 15–30 Hz on an ESP32-C6; 1 cm grid for perisensory-space mapping; SNR threshold 3.5 for reliable detection; 100 MLPs per unit in the ensemble; **under $25 USD per unit.**
- **Design relevance:** The cheapest credible path to combined tactile+proximity coverage for a student team — a printer, conductive filament and ESP32s, no cleanroom, no liquid metal. The 2.6–18 cm per-sensor range spread is the honest warning: identically printed sensors do not have identical ranges, so per-sensor calibration is mandatory, not optional.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware validated on a real arm; no human-subject trial, and it is a 2026 preprint with no visible peer review yet)
- **Limitations:** 15–30 Hz is slow — at 30 Hz you get one sample every 33 ms, which is fine for a slow approach and useless for an impact. Rigid PLA dermis is not a soft link; it is a shell. And 17 sensors on one arm extrapolates to a large channel count for a torso-plus-two-arms body at the same coverage density.

### Intrinsic sense of touch — Iskandar et al., 2024
- **Citation:** Maged Iskandar, Alin Albu-Schäffer, Alexander Dietrich. "Intrinsic sense of touch for intuitive physical human-robot interaction." *Science Robotics*, 9(93):eadn4008, 2024.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/scirobotics.adn4008 — **fetched** the TUM research-portal record; Crossref-confirmed
- **Open access:** no (science.org returns 403; author copy exists at `elib.dlr.de/206039/` but the PDF did not extract to text for me, so I did not read its numbers)
- **What it shows:** A robot arm (DLR SARA) resolving *where and how* it is being touched — well enough to read handwritten letters and numbers traced on its surface, and to place virtual buttons and sliders anywhere on the structure — using only high-resolution redundant joint force/torque sensors plus manifold learning. No skin at all.
- **Hard numbers:** **none verified.** The abstract-level records give no force resolution, localization accuracy or classification rate that I could confirm from a readable source. Do not cite a number from this paper without reading the PDF.
- **Design relevance:** This is the direct counter-argument to building a skin, and the team must answer it in the design review. If joints can localize contact, a 200-channel skin is over-reach. The answer for *this* robot is that it does not transfer: intrinsic sensing works because a rigid arm is a near-perfect mechanical transmission from contact point to joint torque sensor. Compliant links are a low-pass filter with hysteresis between contact and joint — they destroy exactly the signal this method depends on. Say that explicitly; it is the strongest justification for surface sensing on a soft body.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware-validated, human-operated demonstrations; not a standardized safety test)
- **Limitations:** Requires DLR-grade joint torque sensors (expensive, not off-the-shelf), a stiff kinematic chain, and an accurate dynamic model. All three are absent in this project. Also fundamentally ambiguous under multi-contact, which is the normal condition when someone leans on a torso with both hands.

### Whole-body tactile control — Armleder et al., 2025 (with Armleder et al., 2022)
- **Citation:** Simon Armleder, Florian Bergner, Julio Rogelio Guadarrama-Olvera, Jun Nakanishi, Gordon Cheng. "Real-Time Control of a Humanoid Robot for Whole-Body Tactile Interaction." *Advanced Intelligent Systems*, 7(12):e202500149, 2025. — Companion: Simon Armleder, Emmanuel Dean-Leon, Florian Bergner, Gordon Cheng. "Interactive Force Control Based on Multimodal Robot Skin for Physical Human−Robot Collaboration." *Advanced Intelligent Systems*, 4(2):2100047, 2022.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1002/aisy.202500149 and https://doi.org/10.1002/aisy.202100047 — **both resolved and confirmed via Crossref** (authors, venue, volume, issue, article number, year). Wiley returned 403 to direct fetches, so the content summary below comes from the publisher abstract text surfaced in search, not from the full paper.
- **Open access:** no (Wiley; accepted manuscripts exist at the `am-pdf` endpoints but are also 403)
- **What it shows:** How to actually *use* a whole-body skin: a quadratic-program controller that takes force and proximity feedback from a large-area skin network, clusters sensor activations into "active regions" so the dense data stays real-time, and simultaneously runs whole-body compliance, interaction-force regulation, and proximity-derived distance constraints for obstacle avoidance. The 2022 paper adds the key practical result that stable force control was achieved with an **uncalibrated** skin system.
- **Hard numbers:** **none verified** — paywalled, and I refuse to quote numbers I did not read.
- **Design relevance:** Two architectural decisions, both cheap to adopt. (a) Cluster taxel activations into contact regions before they reach the controller — do not feed 200 raw channels into a control loop. (b) Treat proximity as an inequality constraint on motion, and contact force as a task to regulate, in the same solver. Also, the uncalibrated-skin result is directly reassuring for a student build: you can get useful compliant behaviour before you have a per-taxel force calibration.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware on a humanoid; no injury-metric or human-subject safety validation confirmed)
- **Limitations:** Rigid humanoid with torque-controlled joints, which is the plant this project explicitly is not. A QP controller at whole-body rate is also well beyond a senior-design software budget. Take the two ideas, not the implementation.

### Soft sensors + RNN proprioception — Thuruthel et al., 2019
- **Citation:** Thomas George Thuruthel, Benjamin Shih, Cecilia Laschi, Michael Thomas Tolley. "Soft robot perception using embedded soft sensors and recurrent neural networks." *Science Robotics*, 4(26):eaav1488, 2019.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/scirobotics.aav1488 — **fetched** the authors' archival repository `github.com/UCSDBioinspired/thuruthel2019soft`, which states the citation verbatim; Crossref-confirmed
- **Open access:** no for the paper (science.org 403); **yes for the code and data** on GitHub
- **What it shows:** That an *unstructured, redundant* set of embedded soft resistive sensors plus an LSTM can recover both kinematics and contact force on a soft body — i.e. you do not need to place sensors in a principled pattern or model the material, you need enough of them and a recurrent network to absorb the hysteresis and history dependence.
- **Hard numbers:** **none verified** from a readable source. The repository confirms LSTM architecture and LCR-meter data acquisition but does not state sensor count, estimation error or training-set size. Do not quote an accuracy figure without the PDF.
- **Design relevance:** This is the canonical citation for "how do soft links report their own state," and the *method* is what matters: redundancy over precision, recurrence over static mapping. It also carries the field's central warning — a learned soft-sensor calibration is valid only for the material state it was trained on, so it degrades as the elastomer ages, creeps and takes a set. Budget for recalibration in the build plan, not as an afterthought.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware validated, no human subjects)
- **Limitations:** Finger/actuator scale, benign loading, short experimental horizons. Nothing here tells you how the learned model behaves after a 200 N lean permanently changes the elastomer, or after six months of creep. The drift problem the assignment asks about is *acknowledged* in this literature and largely *unsolved*.

### Bend sensors + IMU fusion — Han et al., 2025
- **Citation:** Dong Heon Han, Mayank Mehta, Runze Zuo, Zachary Wanger, Daniel Bruder. "An Enhanced Proprioceptive Method for Soft Robots Integrating Bend Sensors and IMUs." arXiv:2511.01165v1 [cs.RO], 2025.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01165 — **fetched** `arxiv.org/html/2511.01165`
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** The cleanest quantitative treatment of soft-proprioception drift I found. Bend sensors act as a low-frequency absolute reference that anchors IMU integration drift; two linear Kalman filters (orientation, then position) fuse them. All commercial off-the-shelf parts, no vision, no custom fabrication.
- **Hard numbers:** 12 bend sensors + 6 IMUs; tip position RMSE **16.96 mm = 2.91 % of robot length**; **IMU-only drift ≈ 45° over 900 s**, corrected by the fusion; **56 % RMSE reduction vs IMU-only**; 45 minutes continuous operation without degradation; bend-sensor calibration error 4.72° average RMSE; validated across 3 scenarios (unloaded, external forces applied, obstacle contact).
- **Design relevance:** The most directly buildable proprioception recipe for this project. Two cheap modalities, one Kalman filter, ~3 % length error, and — critically — it was tested *with external forces and obstacle contact applied*, which is the loading condition this robot lives in. Adopt this as the arm-state estimator and spend the saved effort on the skin.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware, quantified, no human subjects; 2025 preprint, peer-review status unverified)
- **Limitations:** A continuum arm, not a load-bearing torso; ~3 % error at the scale tested becomes tens of millimetres on a human-scale arm; and 45 minutes is not a duty cycle, it is a demo. The 4.72° bend-sensor calibration error is itself large enough to matter if you need contact-point geometry.

### Intentional vs. unintentional contact — Wong et al., 2024
- **Citation:** Christopher Yee Wong, Lucas Vergez, Wael Suleiman. "Vision- and Tactile-Based Continuous Multimodal Intention and Attention Recognition for Safer Physical Human-Robot Interaction." *IEEE Transactions on Automation Science and Engineering*, 21:3205–3215, 2024. (Preprint arXiv:2206.11350.)
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/TASE.2023.3276856 — **fetched** `arxiv.org/abs/2206.11350`; journal venue/volume/pages confirmed via Crossref bibliographic query
- **Open access:** partial (preprint on arXiv; journal version paywalled)
- **What it shows:** The direct answer to the assignment's hardest sub-question. Binary tactile alone cannot tell a deliberate touch from a collision; adding continuously-estimated human gaze and posture does. Multimodal classification is significantly better than either modality alone, and a deliberately reduced 5-feature model generalizes better than the full set.
- **Hard numbers:** **F1 = 86 %** for intentional vs. unintentional touch classification; 2 modalities (robot-mounted camera + binary tactile); reduced to 5 features; Baxter platform. Participant count, latency and dataset size not stated in the abstract — **not verified.**
- **Design relevance:** This determines the sensing *stack*, not just the skin. If the robot must distinguish "patient is leaning on me for support" from "patient fell into me," tactile alone will not do it and no amount of taxel density fixes that. You need a second modality carrying intent — gaze/posture from a camera is the published route; contact *dynamics* (rise time, dwell duration, force trajectory shape) is the cheaper route this project should test, because a lean is slow and sustained and a collision is fast and transient. 86 % F1 is also a sobering ceiling: this is a hard problem and it is not solved.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware, real interactions, unverified participant count; not a safety-standard test)
- **Limitations:** Healthy adults interacting with an industrial cobot, not elderly patients. Gaze-based intent is exactly the feature most likely to fail on the target population — dementia, visual impairment, and post-stroke gaze deficits all break the assumption that a person looks at what they mean to touch. Assume the vision route degrades and build the dynamics-based classifier as primary.

### Contact anticipation — Escobedo et al., 2021
- **Citation:** Caleb Escobedo, Matthew Strong, Mary West, Ander Aramburu, Alessandro Roncone. "Contact Anticipation for Physical Human-Robot Interaction with Robotic Manipulators using Onboard Proximity Sensors." *2021 IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS)*, pp. 7255–7262, 2021.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS51168.2021.9636130 — **fetched** `arxiv.org/abs/2110.11516`; venue/pages confirmed via Crossref
- **Open access:** partial (arXiv preprint free)
- **What it shows:** Proximity sensing used not to *avoid* contact but to *prepare* for it — a dynamic contact-thresholding algorithm that lowers the collision-detection threshold in the region where proximity says contact is imminent, so genuine contact is detected earlier and false positives elsewhere are suppressed.
- **Hard numbers:** **none verified** — the PDF did not extract to text and the abstract carries no sensing range, latency or force figures. Franka Emika Panda platform.
- **Design relevance:** The single most useful *idea* for this project's control-sensing interface. An assistive robot expects to be touched, so a system tuned to treat all contact as a fault is wrong. Pre-arming the contact detector where proximity predicts contact is how you get both a low detection threshold (safe) and a low false-alarm rate (usable). It also directly supports the intentional/unintentional split: contact that was *anticipated* by proximity in the approach region is far more likely to be intentional.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware validated across scenarios, no human-subject or standards test)
- **Limitations:** Rigid manipulator with reliable model-based collision detection underneath. On a compliant torso the baseline "expected" dynamics are much noisier, so the thresholds have to be wider and the benefit shrinks. And its proximity sensors were purpose-built lab hardware.

### Adaptive skin sensitivity under ISO/TS 15066 — Rustler et al., 2024
- **Citation:** Lukas Rustler, Matej Misar, Matej Hoffmann. "Adaptive Electronic Skin Sensitivity for Safe Human-Robot Interaction." arXiv:2409.06369 [cs.RO], 2024 (revised 2025).
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06369 — **fetched** `arxiv.org/abs/2409.06369`
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** Compares four strategies for setting protective-skin trigger thresholds on a fully skin-covered cobot, from one static conservative value to a dynamically adapted per-pad threshold, and shows the adaptive setting recovers productivity that the conservative setting throws away — while staying inside the ISO/TS 15066 power-and-force-limiting regime.
- **Hard numbers:** UR10e 6-axis arm fully covered; **11 pressure-sensitive AIRSKIN pads**; thresholds adapted at **25 Hz**; framed against ISO/TS 15066 PFL; transient-collision pick-and-place scenario. Specific force thresholds in newtons and reaction times in ms were not extractable from the abstract — **not verified.**
- **Design relevance:** Answers "what trigger threshold do I set on the skin," which is the question that always gets asked in the design review and never gets a good answer. The point is that a single global threshold is the wrong model: threshold should vary by body region (a torso pad a person is *meant* to lean on needs a totally different trigger than a forearm pad) and by motion state. 11 pads on a whole arm is also a useful sanity check against the 1260-cell fantasy — coarse, safety-rated coverage is a legitimate architecture.
- **Evidence grade:** B (hardware on a certified cobot within a standards regime; preprint, no human subjects)
- **Limitations:** AIRSKIN is a commercial certified safety product with a pneumatic trigger — it is a binary safety switch, not a force sensor, and this project cannot afford it or replicate its certification. The ISO/TS 15066 PFL framing also assumes a rigid robot whose stopping behaviour is characterized, which a compliant torso is not.

### Sensing in soft robotics (review) — Hegde et al., 2023
- **Citation:** Chidanand Hegde, Jiangtao Su, Joel Ming Rui Tan, Ke He, Xiaodong Chen, Shlomo Magdassi. "Sensing in Soft Robotics." *ACS Nano*, 17(16):15277–15307, 2023.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.3c04089 — **fetched** the PMC record `pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10448757/`; Crossref-confirmed
- **Open access:** partial (PMC record accessible)
- **What it shows:** Broad materials-side review of resistive/piezoresistive, capacitive, optical, triboelectric and magnetic soft sensing, with integration and signal-processing strategies.
- **Hard numbers:** scattered rather than systematic — screen-printed resistive curvature sensor **hysteresis 17 %**; PEDOT:PSS hydrogel strain sensor **hysteresis < 1.5 %**; hydrogel strain sensors to 300 % stretch; optical strain sensors 0–120 % range with small hysteresis under cyclic loading. It explicitly lacks a side-by-side table of resolution, drift and sensitivity across modalities — which is itself a finding about the state of the field.
- **Design relevance:** The one-stop citation for justifying a transducer-material choice in the report. The 17 % vs 1.5 % hysteresis spread is the number that matters: material choice moves hysteresis by an order of magnitude, and hysteresis is what kills sustained-force measurement on a soft link. Optical waveguide sensing (low hysteresis, chemically inert) is the honest recommendation if force magnitude from the soft link is a hard requirement; resistive nanocomposite is the honest recommendation if it is not.
- **Evidence grade:** D (review)
- **Limitations:** A materials-science review, not a robotics one. Nearly every cited transducer was characterized on a coupon in a tensile tester, not on a load-bearing structure, and none were tested for the multi-month stability a deployed assistive robot needs.

## Verdict for the design team

Build **coarse, cheap, multimodal skin plus one good proprioceptive estimator, and get your force magnitude from somewhere other than the soft surface.** Concretely: 3 cm-class capacitive taxels on the CushSense pattern (~$7 each, open-sourced, human-comfort-validated, 100–400 Hz) over the contact regions a person actually touches — outer forearms, upper arms, front and sides of the torso — at perhaps 30–60 channels total, not 200 and absolutely not 1260. Add a self-capacitance proximity layer on the same substrate for a 15–25 cm approach envelope (GenTact-Prox shows this is achievable with a 3D printer and an ESP32 for under $25 a unit), and accept that per-sensor range varies 7× so every channel needs individual calibration. For limb state, copy Han et al.: bend sensors plus IMUs through a two-stage Kalman filter got ~3 % of length position error *while external forces were applied*, which is exactly your load case, and it is all off-the-shelf. Do **not** try to measure sustained assistive force through the soft skin — Maiolino's 78-minute relaxation constant and the field's 5–17 % hysteresis range mean a leaned-on capacitive taxel lies about magnitude within a minute; put a real load cell or joint torque sensor in the base and the shoulder and let the skin report only *where* and *when*. Over-reach for a two-semester timeline: whole-body self-organizing skin networks, a QP whole-body tactile controller, learned FEM-based shape estimation, an intrinsic joint-torque-only touch sense, and any attempt at safety certification. The intentional-versus-unintentional problem is the one place you should spend real thought rather than buy a solution: the published multimodal gaze-plus-tactile ceiling is 86 % F1 on healthy young adults, and gaze is the exact feature that fails on stroke and dementia patients — so build the classifier on contact *dynamics* instead (a lean is a slow ramp to a sustained plateau; a collision is a millisecond-scale transient followed by decay), state plainly in the report that this is an unvalidated design hypothesis, and put a rise-time/dwell-duration test in the test plan so the design review has data rather than a claim. One thing this literature will not give you and you must not pretend it did: nobody has published a tactile skin under a 200 N sustained human lean on a compliant trunk, so that is your own characterization experiment and it belongs on the schedule.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **Tomographic skins (EIT / acoustic tomography).** A biomimetic elastomeric skin using electrical impedance and acoustic tomography, *Science Robotics* 2022 (doi:10.1126/scirobotics.abm7187), gets whole-body tactile sensing from a handful of boundary electrodes rather than an array — enormously fewer wires, at the cost of a hard inverse problem and poor localization. Genuinely relevant to B2 but I could not verify its numbers behind the paywall; someone should chase it.
- **Tactile gesture recognition as a command channel.** Jiang et al., "Robot Tactile Gesture Recognition Based on Full-body Modular E-skin," arXiv:2506.18256 (2025), recognizes poke/grab/stroke/double-pat via graph neural networks on a full-body skin. That is an interaction-design and D1-control question more than a sensing one, but a patient tapping the robot to signal "stop" is a real requirement this project has not scoped.
- **Tactile-proprioceptive fusion with pneumatic skin.** Min et al., arXiv:2605.28412 (2026), fuses a pneumatic robot skin (Honeywell HSCDANT005PGAA5 pressure sensors, 1 kHz on a Teensy) with motor-current proprioception; reports 88.6 % static-state RMSE reduction and a ~70 ms static-to-kinetic transition window, but no wrench error in N or Nm. Worth a second look because a *pneumatic* skin is both the sensor and the compliant padding — which suits this project's structure — but the paper is unreviewed and its metrics are in milliamps.
- **pHRI overview.** Farajtabar & Charbonneau, "The path towards contact-based physical human–robot interaction," *Robotics and Autonomous Systems* 182:104829, 2024 (doi:10.1016/j.robot.2024.104829, Crossref-confirmed) — covers perception, planning and control together. Better suited to item D1 than B2, but it is the right framing citation for the introduction.
- **ISO 10218 / ISO/TS 15066 evolution.** A 2026 comparative analysis of ISO 10218-1/2 (2011 vs 2025) and ISO/TS 15066 integration appeared in my searches (arXiv:2602.17822). The 2025 revision of ISO 10218 matters a lot for item C1 and someone on that item should verify and read it — the sensing requirements the standard implies may have moved since the numbers most papers cite.
- **Neuromorphic / nociceptive skin.** A 2025 *PNAS* paper on a robotic electronic skin with active pain and injury perception (doi:10.1073/pnas.2520922122) surfaced repeatedly. Interesting for the "robot protects itself" angle but not for patient safety, and I did not verify it.
- **Magnetic soft proprioception** (Soft Matter 19(1):44, 2023, "Learning 3D shape proprioception for continuum soft robots with multiple magnetic sensors") — RSC blocked my fetch, so it is unverified and deliberately excluded from the ranked list. Magnetic sensing is the main modality I could not properly cover; someone should verify it if the arm design ends up with a continuum segment.
