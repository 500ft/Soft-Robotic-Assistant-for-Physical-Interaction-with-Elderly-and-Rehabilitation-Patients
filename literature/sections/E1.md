# E1 — Rehabilitation robotics clinical evidence and requirements

**Design decision this informs:** what assistive forces, ranges, speeds and protocols are
clinically meaningful, so the engineering spec for a compliant soft-robotic upper body is
*derived* rather than invented.

**Verification method.** Every citation below was confirmed by retrieving a record that shows
it. Where PubMed's web page was unfetchable (cookie wall) I used the NCBI E-utilities
`efetch` endpoint (`eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=<PMID>`),
which returns the indexed record and abstract; that is noted per entry as "eutils PMID …".
Others were fetched from PMC, the publisher, or Crossref. **No number in this file is quoted
from a search-engine snippet.** Anything I could not pull from a retrieved record is marked
`not verified` or `INFERRED`.

## Ranked sources

### 1. Cochrane-RAT — Mehrholz et al., 2018
- **Citation:** Mehrholz J, Pohl M, Platz T, Kugler J, Elsner B. Electromechanical and robot-assisted arm training for improving activities of daily living, arm function, and arm muscle strength after stroke. *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews* 2018;9(9):CD006876.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1002/14651858.CD006876.pub5 — verified via eutils PMID 30175845 (PMCID PMC6513114). The Cochrane Library HTML/PDF returns 403 to automated fetch; the indexed record carries the full abstract including all effect sizes.
- **Open access:** partial (abstract free; full review paywalled outside Cochrane subscription — PMC6513114 exists, check institutional access).
- **What it shows:** The single most authoritative synthesis: across 45 RCTs, robot/electromechanical arm training produces small but statistically reliable improvements in ADL, arm function and arm strength, with high GRADE quality and rare adverse events. It explicitly refuses to claim superiority over dose-matched conventional therapy.
- **Hard numbers:** 45 trials, 1619 participants. ADL: SMD **0.31** (95% CI 0.09–0.52), p=0.0005, I²=59%, 24 studies, 957 participants, high-quality evidence. Arm function: SMD **0.32** (95% CI 0.18–0.46), p<0.0001, I²=36%, 41 studies, 1452 participants. Arm muscle strength: SMD **0.46** (95% CI 0.16–0.77), p=0.003, I²=76%, 23 studies, 826 participants. Dropout risk difference **0.00** (95% CI −0.02 to 0.02), I²=0%. Adverse events "rare". No force, torque or ROM figures.
- **Design relevance:** This is the evidence base the team cites to justify the project existing at all — and the honesty constraint on every claim made about it. An SMD of ~0.3 is a *small* effect. It also sets the safety baseline: a device in this class is expected to produce essentially zero excess dropout and rare adverse events, which becomes a design requirement, not a nice-to-have.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (systematic review + meta-analysis of RCTs).
- **Limitations:** Authors themselves warn results must be read with caution because of variation in training intensity, duration, amount, device type, participant characteristics and outcome measures. Every trial is stroke-specific and therapist-supervised in a clinic. Nothing here transfers to an unsupervised care-home setting, and nothing here covers a *freestanding torso* that a person makes contact with — all included devices are grounded end-effector or exoskeleton machines with the patient seated and attended.

### 2. RATULS — Rodgers et al., 2019
- **Citation:** Rodgers H, Bosomworth H, Krebs HI, van Wijck F, Howel D, Wilson N, et al. Robot assisted training for the upper limb after stroke (RATULS): a multicentre randomised controlled trial. *The Lancet* 2019;394(10192):51–62.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1016/S0140-6736(19)31055-4 — fetched PMC6620612 (full text).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC6620612).
- **What it shows:** The largest, most pragmatic head-to-head test of upper-limb robot therapy. Robot-assisted training with MIT-Manus/InMotion did **not** beat an intensity-matched conventional therapy programme, and did not beat usual care, on the primary functional outcome. It did reduce impairment slightly versus usual care.
- **Hard numbers:** n=770 randomised (robot 257, enhanced upper-limb therapy 259, usual care 254). Dose: **45 min/session, 3×/week, 12 weeks** (36 sessions); actual delivered median **23 h 28 min** robot, **24 h 40 min** EULT. Primary outcome (ARAT "success") at 3 months: robot 103/232 (**44%**), EULT 118/234 (**50%**), usual care 85/203 (**42%**); robot vs usual care adjusted OR **1.17** (98.3% CI **0.70–1.96**) — null. Fugl-Meyer motor, robot vs usual care: adjusted mean difference **2.79** (98.3% CI 0.66–5.01) at 3 months, **2.54** (0.07–5.06) at 6 months. Serious adverse events: 43 in 39 robot participants, 42 in 33 EULT, 29 in 20 usual care; **none related to the trial intervention**.
- **Design relevance:** Sets the session protocol the team should default to (45 min × 3/week × 12 weeks ≈ 24 h) and sets the honest expectation ceiling. Also: the FM gain of ~2.8 points is *below* the clinically important difference (see #11), which the design review will notice if the team doesn't say it first.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (large multicentre RCT, blinded assessors).
- **Limitations:** Moderate-to-severe impairment, 1 week to 5 years post-stroke, four UK centres, supervised throughout. Participants, therapists and analysts unmasked for the intervention itself. Says nothing about elderly non-stroke users, postural support, or unsupervised use.

### 3. ARMin-RCT — Klamroth-Marganska et al., 2014
- **Citation:** Klamroth-Marganska V, Blanco J, Campen K, Curt A, Dietz V, Ettlin T, et al. Three-dimensional, task-specific robot therapy of the arm after stroke: a multicentre, parallel-group randomised trial. *Lancet Neurology* 2014;13(2):159–166.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1016/S1474-4422(13)70305-3 — verified via eutils PMID 24382580.
- **Open access:** no (abstract only).
- **What it shows:** The definitive trial of an *exoskeleton* (ARMin) rather than an end-effector robot. Robot therapy beat conventional therapy on Fugl-Meyer — but by an amount the authors themselves call clinically questionable.
- **Hard numbers:** n=77 enrolled, 73 analysed (38 robot, 35 conventional). Chronic (>6 months), moderate-to-severe arm paresis. Dose: **≥45 min, 3×/week, 8 weeks, 24 sessions**. Primary outcome FMA-UE: F=4.1, p=0.041, **mean difference 0.78 points (95% CI 0.03–1.53)**. No serious adverse events related to the study.
- **Design relevance:** Confirms 45 min × 3/week as the field-standard session. More importantly it is the cleanest available statement that exoskeleton assistance produces a *sub-MCID* impairment change — the team must not promise motor recovery as its primary claim.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (multicentre RCT, masked assessors).
- **Limitations:** Chronic stroke only, n=77, therapists and patients unmasked, Swiss rehabilitation centres. 0.78 FM points is roughly one-fifth of the smallest clinically important difference.

### 4. T-WREX-RCT — Housman, Scott & Reinkensmeyer, 2009
- **Citation:** Housman SJ, Scott KM, Reinkensmeyer DJ. A randomized controlled trial of gravity-supported, computer-enhanced arm exercise for individuals with severe hemiparesis. *Neurorehabilitation and Neural Repair* 2009;23(5):505–514.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1177/1545968308331148 — verified via eutils PMID 19237734.
- **Open access:** no (abstract only).
- **What it shows:** A **passive, purely elastic** gravity-balancing orthosis (five DOF, counterbalanced with elastic bands) delivered clinically real, sustained gains in severe hemiparesis with only ~4 minutes of therapist contact per session. This is the closest published analogue to a low-power compliant assistive arm.
- **Hard numbers:** n=28 chronic stroke, moderate/severe hemiparesis. Dose: **twenty-four 1-hour sessions**; therapist assistance **≈4 min/session**. Fugl-Meyer at 6-month follow-up: T-WREX **+3.6 ± 3.9** vs tabletop control **+1.5 ± 2.7** points (p=0.04). Active reaching ROM and Motor Activity Log improved in both groups. Device is passive — no motor torque figure exists because there is no motor.
- **Design relevance:** The single best argument in the literature that **you do not need high actuator torque to be clinically useful — you need arm-weight support plus feedback**. Also gives the supervision budget: ~4 min of human attention per 60 min session is achievable, which is the realistic target for a semi-autonomous device.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (RCT, blinded rater).
- **Limitations:** Small (n=28), chronic severe hemiparesis only, clinic-based with a therapist on site, no attempt at postural support or trunk contact. The absolute between-group difference (2.1 FM points) is well under the MCID.

### 5. Dose-response — Lohse, Lang & Boyd, 2014
- **Citation:** Lohse KR, Lang CE, Boyd LA. Is more better? Using metadata to explore dose-response relationships in stroke rehabilitation. *Stroke* 2014;45(7):2053–2058.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1161/STROKEAHA.114.004695 — verified via eutils PMID 24867924 (PMCID PMC4071164).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC4071164).
- **What it shows:** Meta-analysis/meta-regression across non-dose-matched RCTs: more scheduled therapy time predicts more improvement, independent of time since stroke.
- **Hard numbers:** Pooled effect of higher vs lower dose **g = 0.35 (95% CI 0.26–0.45)**. Time scheduled for therapy was a significant independent predictor of improvement. Authors state that *time scheduled* is a poor dose proxy and that **active time in therapy or repetitions** would be preferable.
- **Design relevance:** This is the project's real value proposition and it should be written into the requirements as such: a machine that is available for more hours than a therapist is the mechanism of benefit, not superior kinematics. It also tells the team what to instrument — **count repetitions and active time**, not session minutes.
- **Evidence grade:** **A** (meta-analysis of RCTs).
- **Limitations:** Dose is confounded with attention and engagement; trials were not dose-matched by design, so the comparison is inherently unbalanced. Stroke only.

### 6. MIT-MANUS — Krebs, Hogan, Aisen & Volpe, 1998
- **Citation:** Krebs HI, Hogan N, Aisen ML, Volpe BT. Robot-aided neurorehabilitation. *IEEE Transactions on Rehabilitation Engineering* 1998;6(1):75–87.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1109/86.662623 — fetched PMC2692541 (full text); identity confirmed via NCBI esummary (PMID 9535526).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC2692541).
- **What it shows:** The origin document for impedance-controlled, backdrivable rehabilitation robotics, and the only place that states the *design rationale* for the end-effector force target in human terms.
- **Hard numbers:** Endpoint force capability **0–45 N**; the stated design target was **28 N**, chosen because it "corresponds to the arm strength during elbow extension for a weak woman in seated position." Endpoint inertia **1 ± 0.33 kg**, max anisotropy 2:1. Endpoint friction **0.84 ± 0.28 N**. Achievable endpoint impedance **0–2 N/mm** as stated in the retrieved full text (secondary sources quote 0–8 N/mm for later builds — use 0–2 N/mm, that is what the 1998 paper says). Planar module 2 DOF, wrist module +3 DOF. Clinical trial: 20 patients (10 robot, 10 standard); FM-UE change **14.1 ± 9.7** vs **9.9 ± 11.2** (p=0.19, null); Motor Status shoulder/elbow **9.4 ± 5.9** vs **0.8 ± 3.8** (p=0.00065). Training: **4–5 h/week**, ~**1500 repetitions** of the task, ~7 weeks.
- **Design relevance:** **This is the single most important number in this file.** The 28 N end-effector target is the only clinically-argued assistive-force spec in upper-limb rehab robotics, and 45 N is the field's de facto ceiling. A soft arm that can produce 25–30 N at the hand is in-spec; one that produces 5 N is not. The endpoint inertia (~1 kg) and friction (<1 N) numbers are the *transparency* budget — a soft arm that a patient must drag around is worse than no arm.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (hardware validated with patients; the embedded clinical trial is a small RCT but underpowered and the primary FM comparison was null).
- **Limitations:** Planar, seated, grounded end-effector. n=20. Impedance control on a rigid low-inertia linkage is a fundamentally different plant from a soft compliant arm — the transparency figures are an aspiration, not a transferable spec.

### 7. ALS-exosuit — Proietti et al., 2023
- **Citation:** Proietti T, O'Neill C, Gerez L, Cole T, Mendelowitz S, Nuckols K, Hohimer C, Lin D, Paganoni S, Walsh C. Restoring arm function with a soft robotic wearable for individuals with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. *Science Translational Medicine* 2023;15(681):eadd1504.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1126/scitranslmed.add1504 — verified via eutils PMID 36724237.
- **Open access:** no (abstract only; Harvard Biodesign hosts a preprint copy — check biodesign.seas.harvard.edu).
- **What it shows:** The state of the art in *soft* upper-limb assistance with real patients: a textile inflatable shoulder-elevation exosuit that is mechanically transparent when unpowered, works without training, and immediately expands active ROM in people with neuromuscular weakness.
- **Hard numbers:** **150 g added per arm.** n=10 people with ALS; 2 followed over **6 months**. Immediate improvement in active ROM; reduced shoulder EMG and perceived exertion; increased object-holding endurance; restored basic ADL performance. Actuator torque is not stated in the abstract (**not verified** — a related Harvard shoulder actuator has been reported at ~16 Nm at 90° abduction and 136 kPa, but I could not retrieve a primary record confirming that figure, so the team must not cite it).
- **Design relevance:** Proves the central premise: a soft, low-mass shoulder assist is clinically meaningful for weakness even when it provides *no* motor recovery. Sets the transparency requirement — the device must be near-zero-impedance when off. The "works with no training" result is the usability bar for an elderly user.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (hardware validated on patients; single-arm, no control group).
- **Limitations:** ALS, not stroke or frailty; n=10; supervised laboratory sessions; wearable rather than freestanding, so the reaction force goes into the wearer's own torso, not into a floor-standing base. The transparency claim depends on the suit being worn, which a freestanding torso cannot replicate.

### 8. Scapular-stretch-robot — Isobe et al., 2026
- **Citation:** Isobe K, Hirokawa M, Suzuki Y, Kadone H, Shimizu Y, Suzuki K. Shoulder-wearable soft robot for assisting older adults in scapular stretching. *Wearable Technologies* 2026;7:e8.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1017/wtc.2026.10044 — fetched PMC13280861 (full text).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC13280861).
- **What it shows:** The only retrieved study that applies a soft robot to **healthy older adults** for passive ROM/stretching (not stroke rehab), and the only one that publishes an explicit force safety envelope for that population.
- **Hard numbers:** n=9 men, mean age **70.9 ± 2.6 y** (range 68–74). Target actuator-side cable tension **98.0–137.3 N (10–14 kgf)**; delivered force lower because of cable friction. Safety limit **F_max = F_ref + 19.6 N**. **Emergency stop within 25 ms.** Loading phase **≈8 s** (deliberately slower than the ~2 s of manual therapy), hold **≤5 s**, **10 stretches per session**. Robot-assisted stretch produced no significant difference from manual therapist stretching on scapula–spine distance; younger-adult comparison showed a 5.1 mm (7.1%) shortfall. Experimenter required within 2 m throughout.
- **Design relevance:** The most directly transferable requirement source in this file for *this* project. It gives (a) an assistive force band for elderly passive ROM at the shoulder, (b) an explicit overload margin (+19.6 N over reference), (c) a **25 ms e-stop latency requirement**, and (d) a movement-speed requirement: assistive motion for older adults should be roughly **4× slower than manual therapy** — of order 8 s to load a stretch, not 2 s.
- **Evidence grade:** **C** (pilot/feasibility, n=9, healthy older adults, no control condition beyond within-subject manual comparison).
- **Limitations:** Nine healthy men, all 68–74, no frailty, no pathology, no women. Wearable, not freestanding. Supervised with an operator within 2 m — explicitly *not* an unsupervised-care-setting result. Force is reported at the actuator, not at the skin interface, so the actual contact load on the shoulder is unknown.

### 9. Soft-UL-review — Bardi et al., 2022
- **Citation:** Bardi E, Gandolla M, Braghin F, Resta F, Pedrocchi ALG, Ambrosini E. Upper limb soft robotic wearable devices: a systematic review. *Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation* 2022;19:87.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1186/s12984-022-01065-9 — fetched PMC9367113 (full text).
- **Open access:** yes (CC BY 4.0).
- **What it shows:** 69 soft upper-limb devices across 105 papers. The field is overwhelmingly single-DOF, bench-tested and immature; almost nothing has been tested on patients.
- **Hard numbers:** 69 devices / 105 articles. Actuation: pneumatic **52%**, cable+DC motor **29%**, passive **6%**, SMA **6%**, spring blades **3%**, hybrid **4%**. DOF: single **56%**, two **28%**, three-plus **<16%**. Joint targets: elbow **51%**, shoulder **45%**, wrist **33%**, forearm **10%**. Tested on people with disability: **7 devices**; on healthy subjects: **50**. TRL: 3 → 9%, 4 → 45%, 5 → 30%, 5/6 → 10%, 6 → 6%. Tracking accuracy achieved: shoulder flexion RMSE **2.12°**, shoulder abduction **3.05°**, elbow flexion **2.9–4.9°**, wrist **<3°**. Hand/palm contact forces **did not exceed 2.2 N**. Interface pressure distribution peak **20–40 kPa**. One device flagged as too heavy at **6.5 kg**. Only one formal clinical trial found: 20 sessions, 15 stroke survivors, hybrid exosuit.
- **Design relevance:** Two requirements fall straight out of it. (1) **Interface pressure at the skin must sit in the 20–40 kPa band or below** — that is the empirical envelope soft devices actually operate in. (2) The achievable joint-angle tracking accuracy for a soft arm is **2–5° RMSE**; do not write a ±1° spec. It also confirms the honest positioning: at TRL 4–5, this project is in the same bracket as almost the entire published field.
- **Evidence grade:** **D** (systematic review of engineering literature, not of clinical trials).
- **Limitations:** Reviews wearables, not freestanding torsos. Only 7 of 69 devices ever met a patient. Says nothing about long-term skin tolerance, and the 20–40 kPa figure is a peak-pressure observation, not a validated safety threshold.

### 10. ARMin-III-hardware — Nef, Guidali & Riener, 2009
- **Citation:** Nef T, Guidali M, Riener R. ARMin III — arm therapy exoskeleton with an ergonomic shoulder actuation. *Applied Bionics and Biomechanics* 2009;6(2):127–142.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1080/11762320902840179 — metadata and abstract verified via Crossref API and the Semantic Scholar Graph API (both fetched); the full text is Gold OA CC-BY per Unpaywall, but every hosting mirror I tried (Wiley, T&F, Hindawi, IOS) returned **403** to automated retrieval.
- **Open access:** yes (Gold OA, CC BY) — but the team must download it manually.
- **What it shows:** The mechanical design of the exoskeleton used in the Klamroth-Marganska RCT, including a shoulder actuation principle that moves the humeral head rather than assuming a fixed glenohumeral centre.
- **Hard numbers:** **3 actuated DOF at the shoulder + 1 at the elbow**, plus an optional module for forearm pro/supination and wrist flexion/extension (6 actuated DOF total). Five devices manufactured. **Joint torque limits, velocities and ROM: not verified** — these are in Table 5 of the paper, which I could not retrieve. Do not cite a torque number for ARMin III until someone opens the PDF.
- **Design relevance:** Sets the DOF allocation argument for a shoulder that must not fight the patient's scapulohumeral rhythm. A rigid exoskeleton needs 3 shoulder DOF plus humeral-head translation to avoid joint misalignment; a soft arm that only contacts the limb externally partly sidesteps this problem, which is a genuine argument in the project's favour and should be made explicitly.
- **Evidence grade:** **D** (design/description paper; the clinical validation is entry #3).
- **Limitations:** Rigid exoskeleton, clinic-mounted, patient strapped in. Misalignment compensation is the central problem for exoskeletons and largely irrelevant to an external-contact soft assistant — cite it for the contrast, not the solution.

### 11. FM-MCID — Page, Fulk & Boyne, 2012
- **Citation:** Page SJ, Fulk GD, Boyne P. Clinically important differences for the upper-extremity Fugl-Meyer Scale in people with minimal to moderate impairment due to chronic stroke. *Physical Therapy* 2012;92(6):791–798.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.2522/ptj.20110009 — verified via eutils PMID 22282773.
- **Open access:** no (abstract only).
- **What it shows:** Anchor-based estimate of how much Fugl-Meyer change a therapist actually regards as meaningful.
- **Hard numbers:** n=146. Estimated **CID for UE-FM: 4.25 to 7.25 points**, depending on the movement facet. ROC AUC 0.61–0.70.
- **Design relevance:** The acceptance criterion. If the team ever runs an efficacy claim, the target is ≥4.25 FM points — which means **RATULS (2.79) and the ARMin trial (0.78) both fell short**, and the team should say so in the design review before the reviewer does.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (anchor-based psychometric study, not an RCT).
- **Limitations:** Mild-to-moderate chronic stroke only; anchor is the *treating therapist's* global rating, not the patient's; AUCs of 0.61–0.70 are weak discrimination.

### 12. UL-MCID-early — Lang, Edwards, Birkenmeier & Dromerick, 2008
- **Citation:** Lang CE, Edwards DF, Birkenmeier RL, Dromerick AW. Estimating minimal clinically important differences of upper-extremity measures early after stroke. *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation* 2008;89(9):1693–1700.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1016/j.apmr.2008.02.022 — verified via eutils PMID 18760153 (PMCID PMC2819021).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC2819021).
- **What it shows:** MCIDs for the battery a clinic would actually run, estimated early after stroke, split by dominant/non-dominant side.
- **Hard numbers:** n=52. Grip strength MCID **5.0 kg** (dominant) / **6.2 kg** (non-dominant). ARAT **12** / **17** points. Wolf Motor Function Test function score **1.0** / **1.2**. Motor Activity Log quality **1.0** / **1.1**. MCID indeterminate for composite strength, WMFT time and accelerometry duration of use.
- **Design relevance:** Tells the team which instruments a clinic will use and what threshold each must clear. Note the ARAT MCID of 12–17 points is *far* above the ~5.7 figure that circulates in secondary sources — use 12–17 for early stroke and cite this paper. Also: accelerometry duration-of-use has **no** usable MCID, so don't build the evaluation plan around wear-time logging alone.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (anchor-based psychometric substudy of an RCT).
- **Limitations:** n=52, acute/subacute (9.5 → 25.9 days post-stroke), inpatient, single trial cohort. Authors state larger samples are needed and that MCIDs may shift with time post-stroke.

### 13. Reps-observed — Lang et al., 2009
- **Citation:** Lang CE, MacDonald JR, Reisman DS, Boyd L, Jacobson Kimberley T, Schindler-Ivens SM, Hornby TG, Ross SA, Scheets PL. Observation of amounts of movement practice provided during stroke rehabilitation. *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation* 2009;90(10):1692–1698.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1016/j.apmr.2009.04.005 — verified via eutils PMID 19801058 (PMCID PMC3008558).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC3008558).
- **What it shows:** What conventional therapy actually delivers, measured by observation rather than claimed. It is very little.
- **Hard numbers:** 312 observed PT/OT sessions, 7 sites. Task-specific functional UE movements occurred in only **51%** of sessions addressing the upper limb. Mean **32 repetitions/session (95% CI 20–44)**. Gait: 357 steps/session (95% CI 296–418). No patient or therapist factor explained the variance.
- **Design relevance:** **This is the competitive benchmark.** The bar to beat is 32 upper-limb repetitions per session. Any device that can supervise 150+ repetitions in 45 minutes is delivering ~5× the practice dose of a human therapist, and *that* is the defensible claim — not better kinematics.
- **Evidence grade:** **B** (prospective observational, convenience sample).
- **Limitations:** Convenience sample of 7 US sites in 2009; practice patterns may have shifted; observers counted repetitions with no standardised definition across movement categories.

### 14. Rep-dose-feasibility — Birkenmeier, Prager & Lang, 2010
- **Citation:** Birkenmeier RL, Prager EM, Lang CE. Translating animal doses of task-specific training to people with chronic stroke in 1-hour therapy sessions: a proof-of-concept study. *Neurorehabilitation and Neural Repair* 2010;24(7):620–635.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1177/1545968310361957 — verified via eutils PMID 20424192 (PMCID PMC3235711).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC3235711).
- **What it shows:** 300+ repetitions per hour is feasible in chronic stroke without excess pain or fatigue, and produced a large ARAT gain.
- **Hard numbers:** n=15 enrolled, 13 completed. Target **300+ repetitions/session (3 tasks × 100)**; achieved mean **322 repetitions/session**. Protocol: **1-hour sessions, 3×/week, 6 weeks (18 sessions)**. Attendance **97%**. Pain and fatigue ratings low. ARAT improved **+8 points** on average, maintained at 1 month. Impairment measure did **not** improve.
- **Design relevance:** The repetition target for the requirements table: **300 repetitions/hour**, i.e. roughly **one assisted movement every 12 s** including rest. That is a hard cycle-time requirement for the arm — pneumatic fill/vent time, controller settling and re-positioning must all fit inside a ~12 s budget. Note this is ~10× the observed conventional dose (#13).
- **Evidence grade:** **C** (single-arm proof-of-concept, n=13, no control group).
- **Limitations:** No control group; chronic stroke with sufficient residual function to complete task repetitions — a frail, severely impaired or cognitively impaired elderly user will not sustain 322 reps/hour. Impairment did not change, so the mechanism of the ARAT gain is unclear.

### 15. Sarcopenia-EWGSOP2 — Cruz-Jentoft et al., 2019
- **Citation:** Cruz-Jentoft AJ, Bahat G, Bauer J, Boirie Y, Bruyère O, Cederholm T, et al. Sarcopenia: revised European consensus on definition and diagnosis. *Age and Ageing* 2019;48(1):16–31.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.1093/ageing/afy169 — verified via eutils PMID 30312372 and full text fetched from PMC6322506 (Table 3 cut-off points).
- **Open access:** yes (PMC6322506).
- **What it shows:** The consensus operational definition of sarcopenia, with numeric cut-offs — i.e. the quantitative definition of the user population's weakness.
- **Hard numbers (Table 3):** Grip strength **<27 kg (men) / <16 kg (women)**. Chair stand **>15 s for five rises**. Appendicular skeletal muscle **<20 kg (men) / <15 kg (women)**; ASM/height² **<7.0 (men) / <5.5 kg/m² (women)**. Gait speed **≤0.8 m/s**. SPPB **≤8**. Timed-Up-and-Go **≥20 s**. 400 m walk: non-completion or **≥6 min**.
- **Design relevance:** Defines the user. A sarcopenic user generates **<27 kg / <16 kg** of grip — so any handle, grasp interface or "hold on here" affordance must work at forces well below that, and cannot rely on the user gripping to stay safe. The five-rise chair-stand cut-off (>15 s) sets the sit-to-stand timing the device must accommodate: assume **≥3 s per rise**, not the ~1.7–2 s of an able adult (see #16).
- **Evidence grade:** **D** (consensus statement, though built on a large evidence base).
- **Limitations:** European reference populations; cut-offs are diagnostic thresholds, not engineering safety limits. Says nothing about tolerance to applied external load.

### 16. STS-torques — Lau & Mombaur, 2024
- **Citation:** Lau JCL, Mombaur K. Can lower-limb exoskeletons support sit-to-stand motions in frail elderly without crutches? A study combining optimal control and motion capture. *Frontiers in Neurorobotics* 2024;18:1348029.
- **DOI/URL:** 10.3389/fnbot.2024.1348029 — fetched PMC11024314 (full text).
- **Open access:** yes.
- **What it shows:** Optimal-control computation of the joint torques a sit-to-stand actually costs for a 50th-percentile elderly woman, and the finding that a current commercial exoskeleton (TWIN) is **under-actuated** for crutch-free STS.
- **Hard numbers:** Hip torque **120.03 Nm** with exoskeleton / **95.79 Nm** without; knee **137.26 Nm** / **111.79 Nm**. Residual human contribution needed: hip **8.03 Nm**, knee **49.26 Nm**. TWIN motor capacity: hip **112 Nm**, knee **88 Nm** — insufficient. Motors required for full assistance: hip **≥121 Nm**, knee **≥140 Nm**. Phase durations: optimal-control lifting phase **1.67–2.00 s**; measured passive (no crutches) sitting **2.50 s** / lifting **1.80 s**. Peak crutch ground reaction force **330.3 N**; peak foot force (crutchless) **898.0 N**. Total modelled system mass 83.1 kg.
- **Design relevance:** **The single most important reality check in this file for the postural-support ambition.** Full sit-to-stand assistance costs >120 Nm at the hip and >140 Nm at the knee. That is an order of magnitude beyond what a soft pneumatic upper body can produce, and it is at the *lower* limb, which this project does not even actuate. The honest scope for this project is *upper-body stabilisation and a handhold reaction force* — the ~330 N crutch reaction figure is the number that matters, because it is the load a freestanding torso would have to take through its base if a user pushes off it. **Design the base and torso structure to react ≥330 N applied at hand height without tipping**, and state explicitly that the device does not provide sit-to-stand lift.
- **Evidence grade:** **C** (simulation/optimal control with a single-participant motion-capture pilot; the participant was 25 years old, not elderly).
- **Limitations:** One motion-capture subject, aged 25, experienced with the exoskeleton; the "frail elderly" is a de Leva-based anthropometric model, not a real patient. Sagittal plane. Crutch forces come from a young user's technique.

## Verdict for the design team

Robot-assisted upper-limb therapy does **not** beat conventional therapy when the two are matched for time, and the team should say that out loud in the first slide rather than have a reviewer say it in the last. RATULS — 770 patients, the biggest trial there is — found robot training no better than an intensity-matched therapy programme on the primary outcome (44% vs 50% success, OR 1.17, 98.3% CI 0.70–1.96), and the ARMin trial's win over conventional therapy was 0.78 Fugl-Meyer points, roughly a fifth of the 4.25-point minimum that a therapist would call meaningful. The Cochrane review's pooled effects (SMD 0.31–0.46) are real, high-GRADE, and small, and they are mostly comparisons against *less* therapy, not against *equal* therapy — which is the tell: Lohse's meta-regression (g=0.35) says the active ingredient is dose, and Lang's observational data says conventional therapy delivers a pitiful **32 upper-limb repetitions per session** while Birkenmeier showed **322 per hour** is achievable. That is the defensible thesis for this project — not better movement, more of it, cheaper, for more hours than a human will stand there — and it reframes the spec from "recover motor function" to "deliver 300 supervised repetitions an hour with a 4-minute human-attention budget." "Meaningful assistive force" is numerically modest and well within reach of soft actuation: **28 N at the hand** is the clinically-argued target (MIT-MANUS chose it as the elbow-extension strength of a weak seated woman), **45 N** is the ceiling, and assist-as-needed modulation happens in a **±6 N** band — meanwhile arm-weight support alone, with *zero* actuator torque, produced sustained gains in the T-WREX RCT, which is the strongest hint in this literature that the project should lead with gravity compensation (≈10 Nm shoulder, ≈3 Nm elbow, both INFERRED and both needing measurement) rather than trajectory-driving torque. Kill the sit-to-stand ambition now: crutch-free STS costs >121 Nm at the hip and >140 Nm at the knee, a commercial lower-limb exoskeleton already fails that test, and this project doesn't even actuate the legs — the honest scope is a stable handhold, and the structural requirement that falls out of it is that the torso and base react **≥330 N at hand height without tipping**. Design for a user who cannot grip (sarcopenia cut-offs are <27 kg men / <16 kg women, so the device must never depend on the user holding on), whose shoulder hurts about a third of the time after stroke, whose skin tears at 11.6% prevalence in long-term care — meaning cuff *shear* is the injury mode to engineer against, not peak normal pressure — and who needs assistive motion roughly **four times slower** than a therapist moves (8 s to load a stretch, not 2 s). Finally, note what every source in this file has in common and what the project does not: all of them were supervised, all of them were clinic- or lab-based, the Isobe protocol literally required an operator within 2 m with a 25 ms e-stop, and **not one retrieved study validates unsupervised assistive physical contact with a frail elderly person** — so the requirements table above is a *supervised-use* spec, and any claim about autonomous home or care-home operation is currently unsupported by clinical evidence.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **pHRI safety standards and injury thresholds (→ C1).** I deliberately did not chase ISO 13482 (personal care robots) or ISO/TS 15066 pain-onset pressure limits; the Isobe 25 ms e-stop and the Bardi 20–40 kPa interface pressure band are the only safety-adjacent numbers I verified, and neither is a standards-derived limit. C1 needs to supply the real thresholds — and specifically needs to resolve whether the 20–40 kPa observed band is anywhere near an injury limit for aged skin, because I could find no primary source that says.
- **Misalignment compensation (→ B1, A2).** Nef 2009 and Atkins 2024 both spend their design effort on humeral-head translation and joint-centre misalignment, which is the dominant structural problem for rigid exoskeletons. Verdel 2022 measured a real misalignment of 0.21 ± 0.07 rad (~12°). An external-contact soft arm largely sidesteps this — that is a genuine architectural argument for the project and B1 should pick it up.
- **Passive joint impedance as a control target (→ D1).** Filius 2023 found passive joint impedance is up to 60% of the passive elbow moment and that gravity compensation alone leaves 20–47% of anti-gravity EMG on the table. That is a controller requirement, not a clinical one, and belongs to D1.
- **Error augmentation / divergent force fields.** Several retrieved records point to error-*augmentation* paradigms outperforming pure assistance for motor learning — i.e. the robot should sometimes resist. The Arantes controller already spans −6 N to +6 N for this reason. Nobody in this outline owns that question.
- **Passive elastic gravity balancing as the primary architecture (→ A1, A2).** T-WREX had no motors and beat the control condition at 6 months. If A1 concludes soft actuation can't reach torque targets, the clinically-validated fallback is not a stiffer actuator — it's springs.
- **Metabolic and fatigue outcomes rather than motor recovery.** Proietti (ALS) and Zhang (Nature Comms 2026, 13.6% metabolic cost reduction walking in 69–85 year olds) both show soft assistance winning on *fatigue and endurance* endpoints while not claiming recovery. If the team wants a measurable win it can actually hit, endurance and perceived exertion are softer targets than Fugl-Meyer.
- **Instrumentation as the product.** Lohse explicitly asks the field to report active time and repetitions instead of scheduled minutes. A device that simply *counts reliably* fills a stated gap in the clinical literature, independent of whether its actuation is any good.
