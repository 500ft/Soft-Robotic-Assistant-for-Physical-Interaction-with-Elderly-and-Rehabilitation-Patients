# B1 — Structural design of soft and compliant humanoid torsos and arms

**Design decision informed:** how to build a freestanding compliant upper body that carries load and supports a leaning person without becoming rigid — and where the load path actually goes.

**Method note:** 13 distinct web searches (Scholar/arXiv/IEEE/Nature/Frontiers/Soft Robotics/ScienceDirect/Crossref/Semantic Scholar). Every entry below was verified by fetching a page or API record that shows the citation; the exact source fetched is named per entry. Numbers are flagged where they came from a secondary page rather than the paper itself. Papers I could not verify were dropped, not reported.

---

### Baloo — Johnson, Clawson & Killpack, 2024/2025
- **Citation:** C. C. Johnson, A. Clawson, M. D. Killpack, "Baloo: A Large-Scale Hybrid Soft Robotic Torso for Whole-Arm Manipulation," arXiv:2409.08420 [cs.RO], submitted Sept 2024, revised Mar 2025.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08420 (fetched arXiv abstract page)
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** A human-scale hybrid soft-rigid torso with two meter-long pneumatically actuated soft arms that performs whole-arm (not fingertip) grasping of large heavy objects, using passive compliance to distribute contact force instead of modelling it.
- **Hard numbers:** two ~1 m pneumatic arms; **19 kg payload at the end effector**; 30 whole-body grasping trials, 100% success across varied object shapes/weights; strength-to-weight ratio stated as competitive with similar-size rigid robots. Torso joint count and arm mass not given on the abstract page.
- **Design relevance:** This is the closest existing analogue to your whole system — a freestanding soft torso plus two compliant arms that actually carries kilograms. It is the existence proof that hybrid (rigid skeleton + soft pneumatic actuation) reaches useful load, and it is evidence that the *dynamic* load case is solvable. Steal the architecture: rigid structural spine, soft actuated links, whole-arm contact.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** Payload is a hanging/grasped load at the end effector, not a lateral leaning load on the torso, and not a static hold for minutes. No reported test of an off-axis moment applied to the torso, which is exactly your load case. Preprint; peer-review status unclear.

### Inflated-beam collapse — McFarland & Coad, 2023
- **Citation:** C. McFarland, M. M. Coad, "Collapse of Straight Soft Growing Inflated Beam Robots Under Their Own Weight," arXiv:2307.00089v1 [cs.RO], June 2023.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2307.00089v1 (fetched full HTML)
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** Derives and experimentally validates when a fabric inflated beam folds under its own weight, giving a closed-form structural limit for any pressurised soft link.
- **Hard numbers:** critical collapse moment **M_collapse = PπD³/8**; tested D = 2.43, 3.24, 4.04, 4.85 cm; P = 2.07–13.8 kPa; collapse lengths 0.3–1.5 m; horizontal case L = D·√(P/(8tρg)); TPU-coated ripstop nylon t = 0.031 mm, ρ = 2199 kg/m³, E = 1.3 GPa.
- **Design relevance:** This is the single most useful equation in the packet. Run your own load case through it before choosing any inflatable link. Example with their formula: a Ø150 mm fabric arm at 20 kPa gives M_collapse ≈ 26 N·m. A patient leaning 200 N at 0.5 m from the shoulder applies ~100 N·m. You would need ~75 kPa (11 psi) at that diameter, or ~Ø230 mm at 20 kPa, to not fold. A 75 kPa tube is not soft to the touch any more — it feels like a car tyre. That trade is the whole argument against a fully inflatable arm here.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** Self-weight collapse of a straight cantilever, not a joint-articulated arm under an external human lean; no repeated-load or creep data; fabric-specific constants.

### Protective soft skins — Svarny et al., 2022
- **Citation:** P. Svarny, J. Rozlivek, L. Rustler, M. Sramek, O. Deli, M. Zillich, M. Hoffmann, "Effect of Active and Passive Protective Soft Skins on Collision Forces in Human-Robot Collaboration," *Robotics and Computer-Integrated Manufacturing*, 78, 102363, 2022.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rcim.2022.102363 (fetched arXiv:2203.09872 abstract page, which states the published venue and DOI)
- **Open access:** partial (arXiv preprint open; journal version paywalled)
- **What it shows:** Systematic measurement of how a soft cover changes real collision forces on three manipulators, with a model extension that accounts for cover stiffness and thickness.
- **Hard numbers:** 2250 collision measurements; passive skin lowered peak impact force **~40%** overall — reported as 40% (UR10e), 45% (KUKA LBR iiwa), 46% (KUKA Cybertech) in transient contact; measures impact force, contact duration, clamping force and impulse.
- **Design relevance:** Quantifies exactly what "padded rigid arm" buys you: roughly a 40% cut in transient peak force, and nothing for the sustained/clamping case. If you go rigid-structure-plus-cover, this is your safety number and it is not a substitute for compliant actuation.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** Industrial arms, not a torso; transient impact, not a person resting their weight on the cover; quasi-static clamping is where covers help least, and leaning is quasi-static.

### Kenshiro / Kengoro design principles — Asano, Okada & Inaba, 2017
- **Citation:** Y. Asano, K. Okada, M. Inaba, "Design principles of a human mimetic humanoid: Humanoid platform to study human intelligence and internal body system," *Science Robotics*, 2(13), eaaq0899, 2017.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/scirobotics.aaq0899 (verified via Semantic Scholar Graph API record: DOI, venue, volume, year, authors, PMID 33157878)
- **Open access:** no (publisher-closed; abstract elided in the API record)
- **What it shows:** The definitive statement of the musculoskeletal-humanoid design philosophy: rigid human-mimetic skeleton, multifunctional structural members, tendon-driven sensor-integrated muscle modules, compliance placed in the actuation rather than the structure.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified from a page I could fetch. Kengoro specs widely cited as 1.7 m, 56 kg, 116 muscle modules, 174 DOF — the 56 kgf figure is confirmed in Kawaharazuka et al. 2024 below; treat the rest as secondary until someone reads the PDF.
- **Design relevance:** This is the third architecture option in clean form — rigid load-bearing skeleton with compliance entirely in tendon actuation. It is the only one of the three that has demonstrated human-scale whole-body contact behaviour. It also tells you where the load path goes: through bones, in compression, to the floor. Same answer you want.
- **Evidence grade:** A (for the architecture argument), C (for numbers, which I could not verify at source)
- **Limitations:** Not designed to be leaned on, and not designed for safety around frail users; the tendon redundancy that makes it compliant also makes it very hard to control and to certify. Enormously outside a senior-design budget and build time.

### Bone-muscle modules — Kawaharazuka et al., 2024
- **Citation:** K. Kawaharazuka, S. Makino, M. Kawamura, Y. Asano, Y. Kakiuchi, K. Okada, M. Inaba, "Human Mimetic Forearm Design with Radioulnar Joint using Miniature Bone-Muscle Modules and Its Applications," arXiv:2408.09934v1, 2024.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09934 (fetched full HTML)
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** Concrete mechanical design of the actuator modules that make a musculoskeletal arm work, embedded inside the bone structure itself.
- **Hard numbers:** module 32.0 × 40.0 × 126 mm, **0.30 kgf per module**, 2× 60 W BLDC per module, **424 N continuous max winding tension**, gear ratios 84:1 and 157:1, Ø8 mm pulley, 116 mm/s no-load winding; forearm = 8 muscles in 4 modules, 6 DOF; 560 N (56.5 kgf) load cell; Kengoro total mass **56 kgf**.
- **Design relevance:** Gives you real tendon force numbers to size against. 424 N per muscle is the order of magnitude a tendon-driven compliant arm needs to hold assistive load — and note that a 157:1 gear ratio is *not* backdrivable, so compliance there comes from tendon elasticity and force control, not from the transmission. Useful counterweight to the "just make it backdrivable" instinct.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** Forearm scale; no data on sustained holding, thermal limits under static load (a leaning patient is a stall-torque condition), or structural margin.

### Lightweight hybrid soft arm — Ohta et al., 2018
- **Citation:** P. Ohta, L. Valle, J. King, K. Low, J. Yi, C. G. Atkeson, Y.-L. Park, "Design of a Lightweight Soft Robotic Arm Using Pneumatic Artificial Muscles and Inflatable Sleeves," *Soft Robotics*, 5(2), 204–215, 2018.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1089/soro.2017.0044 (verified via Semantic Scholar API and EuropePMC full abstract, PMID 29648951)
- **Open access:** partial (bronze OA PDF at Liebert)
- **What it shows:** An explicitly bioinspired soft-rigid hybrid arm: rigid-ish internal structure actuated by custom McKibben muscles, wrapped in inflatable sleeves for impact cushioning, deliberately trading pure softness for accuracy and usable weight.
- **Hard numbers:** **7 DOF** (2 shoulder, 2 elbow, 2 wrist, 1 grasp); custom McKibben PAMs with binary + proportional valves; potentiometer per joint; servomotors for wrist/hand. Inflatable sleeve pressure ~20 kPa giving 3–5 cm of compressible padding (this sleeve figure came from a search-result snippet of the SAGE full text, not from a page I fetched end to end — verify before quoting). Payload and arm mass not stated in the abstract.
- **Design relevance:** The cleanest published statement of your actual architecture: structure and precision from the rigid elements, compliance from pneumatic muscles, contact safety from an inflatable/padded sleeve. Directly transferable layering strategy.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** Arm only, not a freestanding torso; no load-bearing test; no static hold data; pneumatics mean a compressor on the floor, which is a real system-level cost for a clinic.

### King Louie — Best, Wilson & Killpack, 2015
- **Citation:** C. M. Best, J. P. Wilson, M. D. Killpack, "Control of a Pneumatically Actuated, Fully Inflatable, Fabric-Based, Humanoid Robot," *IEEE-RAS Int. Conf. on Humanoid Robots (Humanoids)*, 2015.
- **DOI/URL:** http://radlab.byu.edu/uploads/4/4/7/0/4470886/humanoids_2015.pdf (fetched PDF, text extracted locally)
- **Open access:** yes (author copy)
- **What it shows:** The reference fully-inflatable humanoid: entire structure, not just the actuators, is pressurised fabric. Demonstrates that MPC/LQR can position such a robot well enough to do a pick-and-place task.
- **Hard numbers:** **14 DOF** humanoid (two 5-DOF arms + torso joint); ballistic nylon fabric with internal bladders; **structural chamber pressurised to only 1–2 psi (7–14 kPa)**; antagonistic joint actuators 0–25 psi, limited to 0–17 psi in practice due to pinching; arm link lengths from DH table: 0.18, 0.32, 0.28, 0.14, 0.28 m; task success 8/10.
- **Design relevance:** This is your fully-soft option, and the numbers kill it for your use case. A 1–2 psi structural chamber cannot take a lean — put McFarland & Coad's M = PπD³/8 against 10 kPa and the collapse moment is trivial. Fully inflatable buys safety and lightness and gives up load path entirely.
- **Evidence grade:** B (excellent platform description; no structural load testing reported)
- **Limitations:** No payload, stiffness, or collapse data in the paper. Positional accuracy is poor and the paper does not claim otherwise. Nothing here about static human contact loads.

### Positive-pressure layer jamming — Do, Wu, Zhao & Okamura, 2023
- **Citation:** B. H. Do, S. Wu, R. R. Zhao, A. M. Okamura, "Stiffness Change for Reconfiguration of Inflated Beam Robots," arXiv:2307.03247, 2023. DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2307.03247.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03247v1 (fetched full HTML)
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** Embedded layer jamming inside an inflated beam, stiffened by positive pressure rather than vacuum, letting you switch sections of a soft arm between compliant and load-bearing.
- **Hard numbers:** Ø55 mm × 250 mm beam: unjammed **1.07 N** to displace 10 mm at 6.9 kPa vs jammed **6.68 N** — a **624% stiffness increase**; **663%** at 20.7 kPa. Layer stack (15 paper layers, 0.1 mm each, 35 mm wide, 1.82 mm total, 150 mm span): flexural modulus **263.2 kPa unjammed → 3038.6 kPa jammed (≈11.5×)**. Positive-pressure jamming within 2.1–4.7% of vacuum jamming. 4-segment robot, 0.25 m per section.
- **Design relevance:** The honest scale check on stiffness-tunable structures. An 11× modulus change sounds transformative until you notice the absolute number: 6.68 N to move the tip 10 mm. That is a fraction of the force a leaning person applies. Jamming is a *mode-switching* technology for a soft arm's own posture, not a way to make a soft arm hold a human.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** Small-scale beams; paper layers, not a durable engineering laminate; nothing about sustained load, creep, or cycle life. Scaling jamming force to human-lean loads is unproven.

### Foam-covered manipulators — Zeng & Bone, 2013
- **Citation:** L. Zeng, G. M. Bone, "Design of elastomeric foam-covered robotic manipulators to enhance human safety," *Mechanism and Machine Theory*, 60, 1–27, 2013.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mechmachtheory.2012.09.010 (verified via Crossref API record and Semantic Scholar API: authors, journal, volume, pages, year)
- **Open access:** no
- **What it shows:** A design method for selecting foam covering properties (elastic modulus, viscosity) so that human-robot impact force stays under a stated injury threshold — i.e. treats the cover as a designed structural element, not an afterthought.
- **Hard numbers:** the widely-quoted target is keeping impact force below a **50 N** human pain-tolerance limit; that figure came from a search-result snippet of the ScienceDirect abstract, not a page I fetched in full. **Do not put 50 N in a report until someone reads the paper.** No other numbers verified.
- **Design relevance:** If you choose rigid structure + soft covering, this is the paper that tells you how to size the foam rather than guessing. Pairs directly with Svarny et al.
- **Evidence grade:** B (citation solid; numbers unverified at source — paywalled)
- **Limitations:** Impact case, industrial manipulator geometry. A patient leaning is a sustained compressive load on foam, which means creep and bottoming-out, neither of which this addresses.

### RIBA / ROBEAR — Mukai et al., 2010 (+ RIKEN 2015)
- **Citation:** T. Mukai, S. Hirano, H. Nakashima, Y. Kato, Y. Sakaida, S. Guo, S. Hosoe, "Development of a nursing-care assistant robot RIBA that can lift a human in its arms," *IEEE/RSJ Int. Conf. on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS)*, pp. 5996–6001, 2010. DOI 10.1109/IROS.2010.5651735.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2010.5651735 (verified via Crossref API and Semantic Scholar API: full author list, venue, pages, DOI). ROBEAR specs: https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2015/20150223_2/ (fetched).
- **Open access:** no (paper); ROBEAR press release open.
- **What it shows:** The only robot family that has actually lifted and held adult human body weight in its arms — rigid arms, urethane-foam soft exterior, capacitive rubber tactile skin, low-gear-ratio backdrivable actuators.
- **Hard numbers:** verified from RIKEN: **ROBEAR mass 140 kg**, RIBA-II 230 kg; actuator units use a **low gear ratio for backdrivability**; torque sensors plus Smart Rubber capacitive tactile sheets on arms and chest. The frequently quoted **61 kg lifting capacity** for RIBA appears only in news coverage, not in a source I could fetch — flag it as unverified.
- **Design relevance:** This is the reality check on your whole project. The only machine that has held a human's weight did it with a rigid structure, a heavy stiff base, soft covering, and tactile sensing — not with a soft structure. Note the mass ratio: 140–230 kg of robot to lift ~60 kg of person. Freestanding stability against a lean is a base-mass and footprint problem before it is a compliance problem.
- **Evidence grade:** B (system is real and well documented; the specific numbers I could verify are mass and sensing, not load)
- **Limitations:** Nothing soft-structured about it; huge and expensive; programme discontinued in 2015. Its arms cradle a passive body rather than resist a lean, which is a different load direction.

### Blue (quasi-direct drive arm) — Gealy et al., 2019
- **Citation:** D. V. Gealy, S. McKinley, B. Yi, P. Wu, P. R. Downey, G. Balke, A. Zhao, M. Guo, R. Thomasson, A. Sinclair, P. Cuellar, Z. McCarthy, P. Abbeel, "Quasi-Direct Drive for Low-Cost Compliant Robotic Manipulation," *IEEE ICRA*, 2019; arXiv:1904.03815.
- **DOI/URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.03815 (fetched abstract page)
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** A human-scale 7-DOF arm made safe by low gear ratio and force control rather than by soft materials, at a build cost a student team can approach.
- **Hard numbers:** **2 kg payload**, **7 DOF**, **< $5000** build cost, 7.5 Hz position-control bandwidth, ~4 mm repeatability.
- **Design relevance:** The realistic actuation answer for your budget. Compliance from a backdrivable transmission is cheap, robust, controllable, and does not collapse under static load the way a pressurised structure does. But note the payload: 2 kg. Getting assistive force out of a QDD arm means giving up backdrivability or adding gravity compensation/bracing.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** Arm only; no torso; low payload; 4 mm repeatability; low bandwidth. Backdrivable means the arm also backdrives *under the patient's load*, which is the wrong behaviour when someone is leaning — you need a brake or a self-locking load path for the static case.

### Blower-powered inflatable joints — Niiyama et al., 2021
- **Citation:** R. Niiyama, Y. Seong, Y. Kawahara, Y. Kuniyoshi, "Blower-Powered Soft Inflatable Joints for Physical Human-Robot Interaction," *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, 8, 720683, 2021.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2021.720683 (fetched Frontiers full text; citation confirmed via Crossref API)
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** Very-low-pressure, continuously blower-inflated fabric joints for a "hugging robot," with a torque model validated across sizes, plus contact detection from internal pressure change.
- **Hard numbers:** operating pressure **< 300 Pa** (50–100 Pa minimum to hold shape); joint internal diameter 70–500 mm, lengths to 3 m; nylon fabric ~70 g/m²; prototype uses 5 linear actuators (200 mm stroke, 22:1); puncture-tolerant because continuously blown.
- **Design relevance:** The extreme soft end of the design space and a useful sensing idea — internal pressure as a contact sensor, free. But 300 Pa is two orders of magnitude below the pressures in McFarland & Coad's collapse tests. This structure holds up a fabric limb and nothing else. Use the sensing trick; do not use the structure for load.
- **Evidence grade:** A
- **Limitations:** No payload data, no load capacity, continuous blower noise and power, and structurally irrelevant to a leaning load.

### Tensegrity compliant spine (Flexinoid) — Ali & Abdullah, 2025
- **Citation:** A. R. Ali, H. S. Abdullah, "Development of a compliant spine mechanism for enhanced humanoid robotics locomotion," *Scientific Reports*, 15, 44646, 2025.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-32165-w (fetched PMC12749162 full text)
- **Open access:** yes
- **What it shows:** A 5-segment tensegrity spine (rigid struts in compression, TPU cables in tension) with series elasticity in the tendon paths, replacing a rigid humanoid trunk.
- **Hard numbers:** 5 vertebral segments, PETG printed, 50 mm struts, 1.75 mm TPU cables, **12 DOF**; **15 kg passive static load supported unpowered**; **~7.8 kg active load** during manipulation; flexion −30° to 65°, lateral bend ±30°; per-vertebra 25° flexion, ±10° lateral; 2 motors at 1.89 N·m.
- **Design relevance:** The most directly relevant torso result in the packet, and the key insight is the split between 15 kg passive and 7.8 kg active. A tensegrity torso can *hold* load when the tendons are locked and carries roughly half that when it has to actuate. If you build a compliant torso, design the static lean case as a passive/braked state, not an actuated one.
- **Evidence grade:** B (peer-reviewed and open, but single prototype, small motors, open-loop control, very recent with no independent replication)
- **Limitations:** Aimed at locomotion, load applied axially, not as a lateral lean; 15 kg is far short of a person's partial body weight (a leaning adult easily puts 20–40 kg through the contact); no fatigue or creep data on TPU cables, which will relax.

### ECCE1 anthropomimetic torso — Marques et al., 2010
- **Citation:** H. G. Marques, M. Jäntsch, S. Wittmeier, O. Holland, C. Alessandro, A. Diamond, M. Lungarella, R. Knight, "ECCE1: the first of a series of anthropomimetic musculoskeletal upper torsos," *IEEE-RAS Int. Conf. on Humanoid Robots (Humanoids)*, 2010.
- **DOI/URL:** https://archive.air.in.tum.de/Main/Publications/marques2010.pdf (fetched PDF, text extracted locally)
- **Open access:** yes (author copy)
- **What it shows:** The original compliant anthropomimetic upper torso: hand-moulded thermoplastic "bones," complex non-ideal joints, DC motors pulling elastic tendons, compliance deliberately built into the body rather than the controller.
- **Hard numbers:** verified in the PDF text: reference platform ~**1 m height, ~22 kg, 53 DOF**; engineered 1/2/3-DOF joints; 2-DOF shoulder arrangement fixed to the trunk, 1-DOF elbow hinge, 2-DOF wrists; simulation model with 43 nodes. **No payload, stiffness, or load-capacity numbers are reported anywhere in the paper.**
- **Design relevance:** Useful as the cautionary case. It is the purest "compliance in the body" torso and it reports no load capability at all, because it has essentially none — the elastic tendons that make it safe also make it unable to hold a pose against external force.
- **Evidence grade:** C (design description, no quantitative structural evaluation)
- **Limitations:** Not load-bearing, not freestanding under external load, hand-made and unreproducible, and the control problem it creates was never solved by the project.

## Verdict for the design team

Build a **soft-rigid hybrid: a rigid, grounded structural load path with compliance in the actuation and a designed soft covering at every contact surface.** The evidence is not close. Every system in this literature that has actually supported human-scale load is rigid-structured — RIBA/ROBEAR (140–230 kg of rigid machine to handle a ~60 kg person), Flexinoid's tensegrity spine (15 kg passive, and only because rigid struts carry the compression), Baloo (19 kg, hybrid, rigid torso frame). Every fully soft structure reports either no load data at all (ECCE1, King Louie, Niiyama's blower joints) or numbers off by an order of magnitude from your load case (Do et al.: 6.68 N to deflect a jammed beam 10 mm). Do the arithmetic yourself with McFarland and Coad's collapse moment M = PπD³/8 — a Ø150 mm fabric arm at 20 kPa folds at ~26 N·m, while a patient leaning 200 N at 0.5 m applies ~100 N·m, and the pressure you'd need to survive it makes the arm hard to the touch, which defeats the point. So: soft where the person touches, rigid where the force goes to the floor. Jamming and stiffness tuning are worth one paragraph in your report and no build time; they switch a soft arm's own posture, they do not hold people. Two things the papers say that your team will otherwise learn the hard way: backdrivable transmissions (Blue, ROBEAR) are the right compliance mechanism but they backdrive under a static lean too, so the lean case needs a brake, a self-locking joint, or a passive mechanical stop — design that state explicitly rather than treating the lean as a disturbance; and the freestanding requirement is a base-mass, footprint and tipping-moment problem before it is a compliance problem, which is why ROBEAR weighs 140 kg and nobody in this literature has built a light freestanding torso that resists a lateral human load. Budget your safety case on Svarny's ~40% peak-force reduction from a passive cover — real, useful, and not enough on its own, since a lean is quasi-static and covers help least there.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **Control of soft/inflatable arms** (MPC and LQR on King Louie; Killpack group's model-predictive work) — a substantial literature, and the reason fully-soft arms are usable at all. Whoever owns the control topic should start at Best et al. 2015 and Johnson/Killpack's Baloo control section.
- **Tactile and pressure-based contact sensing as structure** — RIBA's Smart Rubber capacitive skin and Niiyama's "internal pressure as contact sensor" both give whole-body contact detection essentially for free from the structure itself. Directly relevant to a safety topic, not to mine.
- **ISO/TS 15066 collision force limits and the injury-threshold literature** — Svarny et al. and Zeng & Bone both depend on a body-region pain/injury threshold. Your safety/standards topic needs this and it is a separate, well-developed literature.
- **Musculoskeletal state estimation and body-schema learning** (Kawaharazuka's JSK work) — a large arXiv body on the fact that tendon-driven compliant bodies cannot be modelled analytically and need learned body schemas. This is the hidden cost of the musculoskeletal architecture and belongs in a controls/feasibility topic.
- **Soft growing / everting "vine" robots** — the whole Coad/Okamura line. Structurally interesting, irrelevant to a static load case, but it is where the best quantitative fabric-structure mechanics lives.
- **Thermal and stall limits of tendon actuators** — Kengoro's evaporative-perspiration cooling exists because holding a static load stalls the motors. A leaning patient is exactly that condition, and I saw no paper that characterises it for an assistive arm. This looks like a genuine gap.
