# D1 — Control of compliant arms for assistive physical contact

**Design decision informed:** what control architecture delivers a commanded assistive force through a compliant, hard-to-model arm while staying provably gentle?

**Method note (read this before trusting anything below).** Every entry was verified by fetching a page that shows the record: publisher page, Crossref REST record, Europe PMC REST record, Semantic Scholar Graph API record, or the author-hosted PDF (extracted locally with `pdftotext`). The fetched source is named per entry. Where a paper is paywalled and I could only reach an abstract/metadata record, the "Hard numbers" field says so instead of guessing. **No number below is inferred, reconstructed from memory, or copied from a search-result snippet without a fetch.** Ranking is by usefulness to *this* robot, not by prestige.

### CollisionSurvey — Haddadin, De Luca & Albu-Schäffer, 2017

- **Citation:** S. Haddadin, A. De Luca, A. Albu-Schäffer, "Robot Collisions: A Survey on Detection, Isolation, and Identification," *IEEE Transactions on Robotics*, 33(6), 1292–1312, 2017.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2017.2723903 — verified by fetching the TUM research-portal record (https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/robot-collisions-a-survey-on-detection-isolation-and-identificati/), which shows authors, journal, volume, issue, pages, article number 8059840 and the DOI.
- **Open access:** No (IEEE paywalled; the TUM record is metadata + abstract only).
- **What it shows:** The reference taxonomy for the collision *event pipeline* — detection → isolation → identification → classification → reaction — using **proprioceptive sensors only** (motor current / joint torque + position), with the generalized-momentum residual as the workhorse estimator. Covers both rigid and flexible-joint (elastic-transmission) arms.
- **Hard numbers:** none reported in the record I fetched (metadata + abstract only). The abstract claims experimental comparison of the algorithms but the numeric results sit behind the paywall.
- **Design relevance:** This is the architecture spine for the "provably gentle" half of the question. It tells you that a torso+arm robot does **not** need a skin, a camera, or a wrist F/T sensor to know it hit someone: a model-based residual on the actuator side is enough, and the same residual gives you a *direction* to retreat along. Build your safety layer as residual → threshold → reaction-strategy switch.
- **Evidence grade:** A (definitive survey, top venue, by the people who invented the method; downgraded from A+ only because I could not read the experimental numbers).
- **Limitations:** Every method assumes you have a usable rigid/flexible-joint dynamic model `M(q)q̈ + C q̇ + g = τ`. A soft torso with a person leaning on it violates that: the "model error" from unmodeled body compliance, air-pressure dynamics and hysteresis will swamp the contact residual. On soft hardware the residual becomes a *change detector*, not a force estimator, and a sustained lean is exactly the signal it is worst at (see next entry's threshold discussion).

### DLR-Reaction — De Luca, Albu-Schäffer, Haddadin & Hirzinger, 2006

- **Citation:** A. De Luca, A. Albu-Schäffer, S. Haddadin, G. Hirzinger, "Collision Detection and Safe Reaction with the DLR-III Lightweight Manipulator Arm," *2006 IEEE/RSJ Int. Conf. on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS)*, Beijing, pp. 1623–1630, 2006.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2006.282053 — verified by fetching the author-hosted green-OA PDF at http://www.diag.uniroma1.it/~labrob/pub/papers/IROS06_DLR.pdf (full text extracted locally) plus the Semantic Scholar record for the DOI.
- **Open access:** Yes (green OA, author copy at DIAG Sapienza).
- **What it shows:** The concrete recipe: build a residual `r` from total energy or generalized momentum, threshold it component-wise, then switch the controller into one of four reaction strategies (0 = none, 1 = stop, 2 = zero-gravity float, 3 = reflex torque `τ = K_R · r`, 4 = admittance reaction on the motor side). Extends the whole thing to elastic joints, which is the closest rigid-robot analogue to a compliant arm.
- **Hard numbers:** High-level control loop **1 ms**, local joint control **333 µs**. Detection observer gain `K_I = 25·I`; reflex gains `K_R = 1.4·I` (strategy 3) and `0.05·I` (strategy 4). Detection thresholds set at **10 % of each joint's max output torque**, giving "reliable detection without false alarms and with just a one-step delay (**1 ms**)", but the current-control communication path added **4–6 ms** more, i.e. real end-to-end latency ≈ **5–7 ms**. Hardware: 13.5 kg arm, joint stiffness **10–15 × 10³ Nm/rad**, max joint torques 180 / 80 / 30 Nm, end-effector speed > 4 m/s. Impacts at cruise speeds 10–100 °/s; human collisions at contact speeds **up to ~1.5 m/s**. With no detection (strategy 0) the arm pressed the target to **35 Nm** at joint 4; all reactive strategies drove contact torque to zero. Human trials were **qualitative only** — the paper explicitly says instrumented testing would be needed to quantify impact forces.
- **Design relevance:** Gives you numbers you can design against: the threshold-as-%-of-max-torque heuristic, the fact that comms latency (4–6 ms) dominates the estimator latency (1 ms), and the observation that **strategy 3 (reflex torque, i.e. become an admittance the human can push) felt smoothest to humans** — which is exactly the behaviour a lean-on torso needs. Copy this structure; do not copy the gains.
- **Evidence grade:** A (real hardware, explicit gains, explicit latency budget, honest about what was not measured).
- **Limitations:** Direct transfer risk is high. A person *leaning* on the torso is a slow, sustained, quasi-static contact — the worst case for a residual with a 10 %-of-max-torque threshold, because the lean load can look like model error or payload. The DLR arm is a stiff-link/elastic-joint machine; nothing here says the residual is well-conditioned when the *links themselves* deform. And the 1 ms loop assumes a hard-real-time joint bus; a hobby-grade servo bus at 50–100 Hz throws the whole latency argument away.

### HoganImpedance — Hogan, 1985

- **Citation:** N. Hogan, "Impedance Control: An Approach to Manipulation: Part I—Theory," *ASME Journal of Dynamic Systems, Measurement, and Control*, 107(1), 1–7, 1985. (Companion papers: Part II—Implementation, 107(1), 8–16; Part III—Applications, 107(1), 17–24.)
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1115/1.3140702 — verified by fetching the MIT-hosted PDF (https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.mit.edu/dist/5/1384/files/2025/02/1985-impedance-control-an-approach-to-manipulation-part-I-theory.pdf) and reading the title block and abstract directly; Part II/III page ranges cross-checked on the ASME Digital Collection listing.
- **Open access:** Partial (author/institution-hosted copy of Part I is free; the ASME versions are paywalled).
- **What it shows:** The founding argument. "Control of position or force alone is inadequate; control of dynamic behavior is also required." Because manipulation is nonlinear, impedance and admittance are **not** interchangeable, and — the sentence that decides your architecture — *given the environment contains inertial objects, the manipulator must be an impedance.* Also defines the nonlinear Norton-equivalent that separates commanded motion from commanded impedance.
- **Hard numbers:** none reported (theory paper).
- **Design relevance:** A human leaning on your torso is an inertial, energy-storing environment. Hogan's result says the robot should present itself as an impedance (accept motion, produce force) rather than an admittance (accept force, produce motion) *at the contact*. For this project that argues for: command a virtual spring-damper about a reference posture, and let the assistive force fall out of the deflection — not a force-servo loop chasing a setpoint. The Norton separation also gives you the clean split your team wants: one module plans the equilibrium trajectory, another sets stiffness/damping.
- **Evidence grade:** A (foundational; 40 years of downstream validation).
- **Limitations:** Assumes you can *render* the impedance you command — i.e. actuator torque authority and bandwidth exceed the impedance you're asking for. A pneumatic/tendon soft arm cannot render an arbitrary impedance; its physical impedance is largely fixed by its construction and pressure, so much of your "impedance design" happens in the mechanical design phase, not the control code. Treat Hogan as the specification language, not as an implementable controller.

### AdmittancePHRI — Keemink, van der Kooij & Stienen, 2018

- **Citation:** A. Q. L. Keemink, H. van der Kooij, A. H. A. Stienen, "Admittance control for physical human–robot interaction," *The International Journal of Robotics Research*, 37(11), 1421–1444, 2018.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364918768950 — verified two ways: the Semantic Scholar Graph API record for the DOI, and the full green-OA PDF from the University of Twente repository (https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/files/29087621/admittance.pdf), text extracted locally.
- **Open access:** Yes (green OA, UTwente repository).
- **What it shows:** The most practically useful single document on the impedance/admittance trade-off. States the trade-off cleanly and then *engineers* it: "For admittance controlled devices it is easier to render stiff virtual surfaces and a challenge to render low inertia… Impedance control, on the other hand, is a better candidate to render low inertia but not to render stiff virtual surfaces." Produces **seven design guidelines** (feed-forward, avoid force filtering, compensate post-sensor inertia, add virtual damping, modify the velocity reference, raise velocity-loop bandwidth, optimise for robot stiffness) each tied to a positive-real / passivity condition for coupled stability.
- **Hard numbers:** Worked baseline: making a **10 kg** device with damping feel like a **2 kg** pure inertia — a **factor-5** inertia reduction — is the stated performance target, and the guidelines exist because naive admittance loses passivity well before that. Example parameters used: `G_f = 5`, `µ_ff = 10 kg`, `β_ff = 2 Ns/m`, differential gain `k_d = 25 Ns²/m`. Notes industrial current-controller bandwidth is "commonly > 2 kHz". **No human subjects** — the paper is analysis + guidelines.
- **Design relevance:** Decides the sensing question. Admittance control needs a real force sensor at the interaction port and a stiff, high-bandwidth inner velocity loop. Your torso has neither cheaply. Guideline 7 ("optimise for robot stiffness") and the internal-flexibility analysis are the direct warning: **structural compliance between the force sensor and the actuator destroys admittance-control passivity**, which is the defining condition of a soft arm. This paper is the strongest single argument in the pile for *not* doing admittance control on this robot.
- **Evidence grade:** A (rigorous, open, immediately actionable, and it tells you when its own method fails).
- **Limitations:** Single-DOF, idealised-stiff analysis; the authors say so. It gives you conditions to *avoid*, not a controller for a soft multi-segment torso. Zero human-subject data, so nothing here speaks to comfort or perceived safety.

### ProiettiALS — Proietti et al., 2023

- **Citation:** T. Proietti, C. O'Neill, L. Gerez, T. Cole, S. Mendelowitz, K. Nuckols, C. Hohimer, D. Lin, S. Paganoni, C. J. Walsh, "Restoring arm function with a soft robotic wearable for individuals with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis," *Science Translational Medicine*, 15(681), eadd1504, 2023.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.add1504 — verified by fetching the Europe PMC REST record (core result, PMID 36724237), which returned the full author list, journal, volume/issue, year and verbatim abstract. Crossref record fetched as a cross-check.
- **Open access:** No (Europe PMC reports subscription-only).
- **What it shows:** A textile inflatable shoulder-assist wearable evaluated on **10 people with ALS**, i.e. exactly the clinically fragile, variable-impairment population this project claims. Immediate improvement in active range of motion, improved ADL performance **with no training**, reduced shoulder EMG and perceived exertion, increased holding endurance, and — the important design property — the device is **mechanically transparent when unpowered**.
- **Hard numbers:** **150 g added per arm**; **10 participants** with ALS at differing impairment levels; **2 participants followed for 6 months** showing compensation for continued deterioration. Force-tracking error, bandwidth and commanded-stiffness figures: not reported in the abstract, and the full text is paywalled — so **not verified**.
- **Design relevance:** The single best evidence in this set that soft, low-authority, *non-force-servoed* assistance produces measurable clinical benefit in the target population. Two design rules fall out: (1) unpowered mechanical transparency is a safety feature and a usability feature — design your torso so a powered-off failure is a benign failure; (2) assistance can be delivered as a slowly-modulated pressure/torque profile rather than a closed-loop force setpoint and still be worth something to a patient.
- **Evidence grade:** A (human subjects, clinical population, top translational venue).
- **Limitations:** A wearable is grounded on the user's own skeleton; your torso is grounded on the floor and must resist a lean without tipping. Nothing here addresses balance, tipping, or the reaction load path through a freestanding base. Also shoulder *elevation assist* is a single-DOF, mostly gravity-compensating task — far easier than a commanded assistive force in an arbitrary direction. n = 10, single-arm study, no control group.

### UFIC — Haddadin & Shahriari, 2024

- **Citation:** S. Haddadin, E. Shahriari, "Unified force-impedance control," *The International Journal of Robotics Research*, 43(14), 2112–2141, 2024. (Builds on C. Schindlbeck, S. Haddadin, "Unified passivity-based Cartesian force/impedance control for rigid and flexible joint robots via task-energy tanks," *ICRA 2015*, pp. 440–447, https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2015.7139036.)
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/02783649241249194 — verified via the Semantic Scholar Graph API record for the DOI (title, authors, journal, volume, pages, full abstract, CC-BY-NC hybrid status). The ICRA 2015 predecessor verified independently via the Crossref REST record (DOI, pages 440–447).
- **Open access:** Partial (hybrid, CC-BY-NC per the S2 record; the SAGE PDF endpoint refused my fetch, so I could not read the body).
- **What it shows:** How to get *exact force regulation* and *compliance* out of one controller instead of switching between them, with passivity guaranteed by **energy tanks**, for both rigid and flexible-joint robots, and with either measured or model-estimated contact force. Explicitly attacks the failure mode that should scare this project: abrupt, dangerous motion on **contact loss** — the thing that happens when the person leaning on your torso suddenly stands up.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified (abstract only; the experimental section is behind the SAGE paywall and my fetch returned HTTP 403). The abstract claims simulation plus "various experiments" and rigorous stability analysis for arbitrary passive environments.
- **Design relevance:** This is the *right* answer to the assigned design question and almost certainly the wrong answer for a senior-design timeline. If you do implement force tracking on top of impedance, steal exactly two ideas: the energy tank (a scalar state that bounds how much energy the controller may inject) and the contact/non-contact stabilisation shaping function (ramp the force command in and out instead of switching).
- **Evidence grade:** B (top journal, rigorous, but I could not verify a single experimental number, and it has no human-subject validation).
- **Limitations:** Assumes a serial rigid or flexible-joint robot with a trustworthy dynamic model and joint torque sensing or good contact-force estimation. Your soft torso has none of those. Implementing UFIC correctly is a graduate-thesis-scale task.

### TankVSC — Ferraguti, Secchi & Fantuzzi, 2013

- **Citation:** F. Ferraguti, C. Secchi, C. Fantuzzi, "A tank-based approach to impedance control with variable stiffness," *2013 IEEE Int. Conf. on Robotics and Automation (ICRA)*, pp. 4948–4953, 2013.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2013.6631284 — verified via the Crossref REST record (DOI, exact title, all three authors, container title, pages 4948–4953, year).
- **Open access:** No.
- **What it shows:** The canonical energy-tank construction: time-varying stiffness can inject energy and destabilise an interaction, so you attach a virtual tank fed by dissipated power and only permit stiffness changes the tank can pay for. Result: **passivity for any choice of stiffness matrix**, in free motion and in contact.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified — I confirmed the bibliographic record and the method's claim via the Crossref record and the paper's widely-quoted abstract statement, but did not reach the full text, so I report no experimental figures.
- **Design relevance:** If your team wants "gentle when the person is passive, firmer when they need a real assist," you are doing variable stiffness, and variable stiffness is where naive impedance control stops being provably safe. The tank is about **twenty lines of code**: one scalar energy state, integrate dissipated power in, subtract the cost of stiffness increases, clamp stiffness when the tank empties. This is the single highest value-per-line safety mechanism available to you.
- **Evidence grade:** B (well-established, heavily cited, but numbers unverified here and the demonstration is a rigid manipulator).
- **Limitations:** Passivity guarantees the robot injects no unbounded energy. It does **not** guarantee the *forces* stay below an injury threshold, and it does not guarantee the robot stays upright. A perfectly passive torso can still be passively too stiff for a frail person. You need a force/torque cap on top of the tank, not instead of it.

### AR3n — Pareek, Nisar & Kesavadas, 2023

- **Citation:** S. Pareek, H. Nisar, T. Kesavadas, "AR3n: A Reinforcement Learning-Based Assist-as-Needed Controller for Robotic Rehabilitation," *IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine*, 2023. Preprint arXiv:2303.00085.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2303.00085 (IEEE version: https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10158787/) — verified by fetching the arXiv abstract page (authors, journal-ref "IEEE Robotics and Automation Magazine, 2023") **and** the accepted-version PDF, whose text I extracted locally to read the human-subject section.
- **Open access:** Yes (arXiv accepted version).
- **What it shows:** Assist-as-needed assistance modulated by an RL policy against a *virtual patient model*, so the controller does not need per-patient tuning or a per-patient physical model — the standard objection to classical AAN. Benchmarked head-to-head against a conventional rule-based error-reduction (ER) controller.
- **Hard numbers:** **8 healthy subjects** (5 M / 3 F, mean age 26, range 19–33), IRB #15990, single session, three trials each (baseline / training / post). Robot sampled at **1000 Hz**; the RL environment runs at **50 Hz** with a history window n = 25. Reward weights λ_T = 1, λ_N = 0.4 chosen experimentally. SAC beat PPO on training reward. The paper concedes the study was "conducted on a fairly small pool of healthy subjects." Force-tracking error in newtons: not reported in those terms.
- **Design relevance:** Useful mainly as a *negative* result for your scope. It confirms the real problem in AAN is the patient model, and it solves that with an RL policy trained in simulation — which needs a simulator of your plant. You do not have a trustworthy simulator of a soft torso. The transferable idea is much cheaper: **penalise assistance magnitude and assistance rate-of-change in whatever rule you use**, which is what makes AR3n's profiles smooth versus ER's step-like bursts.
- **Evidence grade:** B (real human subjects with numbers, open preprint; downgraded for n = 8 healthy, single session, magazine-tier venue, and a rigid planar device).
- **Limitations:** Healthy young subjects, a handwriting task on a stiff planar manipulandum, and an artificially induced "impairment" (non-dominant hand + mirrored axes). None of that is an elderly person leaning on a compliant torso. The 50 Hz policy rate is fine for assistance modulation but far too slow for a safety reflex — do not put the learned policy in the safety path.

### ShoulderExosuit — Zhou et al., 2024

- **Citation:** Y.-M. Zhou, C. J. Hohimer, H. T. Young, C. M. McCann, D. Pont-Esteban, U. S. Civici, Y. Jin, P. Murphy, D. Wagner, T. Cole, N. S. Phipps, H. Cho, F. Bertacchi, I. Pignataro, T. Proietti, C. J. Walsh, "A portable inflatable soft wearable robot to assist the shoulder during industrial work," *Science Robotics*, 9, 2024.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/scirobotics.adi2377 — verified via the Semantic Scholar Graph API record for the DOI (full author list, journal, volume, year, abstract). The Science site itself returned HTTP 403 to my fetch.
- **Open access:** No (S2 reports CLOSED).
- **What it shows:** A shirt-form inflatable shoulder assist driven by a **kinematics-based controller** (IMU-driven, not force-servoed) reduced shoulder muscle activity by up to 40 % in simulated industrial tasks, with minimal change to joint angles, and the controller was robust to equipment malfunction. Piloted with real factory workers.
- **Hard numbers:** up to **6.6 N·m** shoulder assist torque; **up to 40 %** reduction in agonist muscle activity (anterior/middle/posterior deltoid, biceps brachii); assistance cycled on/off **6 times per minute**. Exact participant count not stated in the record I fetched — the abstract says "human participant evaluations" plus factory-worker pilot testing, so I list it as **not verified**.
- **Design relevance:** The blunt lesson for your team: a **kinematics-triggered, open-loop-in-force** assist delivered a 40 % measurable physiological benefit with no force feedback loop at all. If your success metric is "measurable assistive force," a well-characterised pressure→force map plus a posture-triggered state machine may hit your spec, and it is a tenth of the engineering of a closed-loop force controller.
- **Evidence grade:** B (strong venue, real users, good numbers; downgraded because it is a wearable, closed-access, and the subject count is unverified).
- **Limitations:** Same grounding problem as ProiettiALS — the suit reacts against the wearer's skeleton. 6.6 N·m against your own shoulder is not the same problem as reacting a leaning adult's body weight through a freestanding base. Assistance cycling at 0.1 Hz says nothing about your reflex bandwidth needs.

### AAN-EMG — Arantes, Bressan, Borges & McGibbon, 2023

- **Citation:** A. P. Arantes, N. Bressan, L. R. Borges, C. A. McGibbon, "Evaluation of a novel real-time adaptive assist-as-needed controller for robot-assisted upper extremity rehabilitation following stroke," *PLOS ONE*, 18(10), e0292627, 2023.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0292627 — verified by fetching the full open-access text at PMC (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10566685/).
- **Open access:** Yes (CC-BY, PMC10566685).
- **What it shows:** An EMG-driven adaptive AAN controller that assists when the user is *trying* (EMG present) but not moving, and progressively switches to **resistance** as performance improves — an explicit anti-slacking design. Tested on real stroke patients with EMG + EEG instrumentation.
- **Hard numbers:** **5 chronic stroke participants** (mean age ~66, Fugl-Meyer 36–65). Assistance/resistance authority band **−6 N to +6 N**. EMG trigger threshold **20 %** of baseline maximum entropy amplitude; six upper-extremity muscles monitored; 9-channel EEG, beta band 16–24 Hz. Significant EMG increase during training (paired t = 3.73, p = 0.02); EEG changes non-significant.
- **Design relevance:** Two directly usable facts. First, a **±6 N** authority band was clinically meaningful for stroke patients — that is a defensible order of magnitude for your assistive-force spec, and it is small enough to be safe. Second, intent detection does not require a force sensor at the contact: a crude EMG-or-effort gate plus a deadband is enough to distinguish "the user is trying" from "the user is slacking." A load-cell threshold in your torso can play the same role.
- **Evidence grade:** C (real patients, fully open, honest reporting — but n = 5, pilot, no control group, and the primary outcome was an EMG surrogate, not function).
- **Limitations:** Planar horizontal arm task on a rigid haptic device. Says nothing about compliant plants, contact safety, or a person putting body weight into the robot. n = 5 cannot support any efficacy claim; cite it for the force magnitude and the controller logic, never as evidence that AAN works.

### SoftModelBased — Della Santina, Katzschmann, Bicchi & Rus, 2020

- **Citation:** C. Della Santina, R. K. Katzschmann, A. Bicchi, D. Rus, "Model-based dynamic feedback control of a planar soft robot: trajectory tracking and interaction with the environment," *The International Journal of Robotics Research*, 39(4), 490–513, 2020.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364919897292 — verified via the Semantic Scholar Graph API record for the DOI (authors, journal, volume, pages, verbatim abstract, bronze-OA status).
- **Open access:** Partial (S2 reports bronze OA at SAGE; the PDF endpoint refused my fetch).
- **What it shows:** The key structural result for controlling soft arms: under a **piecewise constant curvature (PCC)** assumption, soft-robot dynamics can be mapped *exactly* onto an equivalent rigid robot with elastic joints — which means the entire elastic-joint control literature (including De Luca's residual and Hogan's impedance) becomes importable. Presents the first closed-loop *dynamic* controllers for a continuous soft robot: trajectory tracking and **surface following**, both designed to preserve the robot's natural softness during interaction.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified (abstract only; SAGE returned HTTP 403). The abstract reports analytic proofs, extensive simulation, and experiments on a physical planar soft robot, but no figures I could read.
- **Design relevance:** This is your justification for treating each soft arm as an elastic-joint rigid arm in the controller, and for using PCC as the state parameterisation rather than a full Cosserat rod. "Surface following while preserving softness" is structurally the same problem as "maintain contact force against a torso a person is leaning on."
- **Evidence grade:** B (top journal, important idea, but planar-only, single lab, and unverified numbers).
- **Limitations:** PCC assumes constant curvature per segment and breaks under exactly the load case you care about — a **concentrated external load mid-segment**, i.e. someone leaning on the middle of an arm. It is planar; your torso is spatial and must also handle gravity in a way a planar testbed never does. Model identification for a real soft arm is a semester of work by itself.

### SoftForceTracking — Tang, Xin, Wang & Laschi, 2024

- **Citation:** Z. Tang, W. Xin, P. Wang, C. Laschi, "Learning-Based Control for Soft Robot–Environment Interaction with Force/Position Tracking Capability," *Soft Robotics*, 11(5), 767–778, 2024.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1089/soro.2023.0116 — verified via the Semantic Scholar Graph API record for the DOI (authors, journal, volume, pages, abstract).
- **Open access:** No (S2/Unpaywall report no OA copy).
- **What it shows:** Simultaneous **force and position** tracking on a soft manipulator with no prior model of either the robot's dynamics or the environment: probabilistic MPC feedforward plus a non-parametric learned feedback term, adapting online. Demonstrated against a **manikin**, which is the closest thing in this pile to your use case.
- **Hard numbers:** none verified (paywalled; the abstract claims it outperformed comparable data-driven alternatives but gives no figures I could read).
- **Design relevance:** The existence proof that force tracking on a soft arm against a human-shaped target is possible without a model — and simultaneously the warning about what it costs: a probabilistic MPC plus online non-parametric learning is not a senior-design deliverable. Cite it to justify the *goal*; do not cite it as a plan.
- **Evidence grade:** C (right problem, right venue, but zero verified numbers, single platform, no human subjects — a manikin is not a person).
- **Limitations:** A manikin is passive, rigid-ish and does not push back, get startled, or shift its weight. Data-driven controllers trained on one environment degrade silently when the environment changes; on a robot a frail person leans on, a silent degradation is a safety event. Nothing in this approach is provably gentle.

### VICReview — Abu-Dakka & Saveriano, 2020

- **Citation:** F. J. Abu-Dakka, M. Saveriano, "Variable Impedance Control and Learning—A Review," *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, 7, 590681, 2020.
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2020.590681 — verified by fetching the full open-access article at Frontiers.
- **Open access:** Yes (Frontiers, CC-BY).
- **What it shows:** The map of the variable-impedance field, split into variable impedance *control* (VIC), *learning* (VIL) and *learning control* (VILC). Its most useful line for your team is the admission that **"stability in VIC is not a trivial problem"** — and that the two credible routes to a guarantee are passivity theory (no environment model needed) and Lyapunov constraints on the impedance matrices.
- **Hard numbers:** **none reported** — verified by reading the full text; the paper is methodological taxonomy with no stiffness ranges, bandwidths or benchmark figures anywhere.
- **Design relevance:** Use it to scope, not to build: it tells you that if you vary stiffness you must pair it with a passivity mechanism (→ TankVSC), and it lets you honestly say in your report that you surveyed the VIC landscape and deliberately chose the simplest rung.
- **Evidence grade:** B (comprehensive, open, well-organised; it's a review, so it is evidence *about* the literature, not about hardware).
- **Limitations:** Rigid-manipulator-centric. Almost nothing on soft plants, nothing on human subjects, and no numbers at all — so it cannot settle any quantitative design question for you.

### RodControlReview — Alessi et al., 2024

- **Citation:** C. Alessi, C. Agabiti, D. Caradonna, C. Laschi, F. Renda, E. Falotico, "Rod models in continuum and soft robot control: a review," arXiv:2407.05886 (submitted 8 July 2024; revised 2026).
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2407.05886 — verified by fetching the arXiv abstract page (title, all six authors, dates, abstract).
- **Open access:** Yes (arXiv preprint).
- **What it shows:** Survey of Cosserat-rod and reduced-order rod theories specifically from a **control** standpoint — which rod formulations are cheap enough to sit inside a controller, and how they feed both model-based and learned control for manipulation and physical interaction.
- **Hard numbers:** none reported (review).
- **Design relevance:** Bounds your modelling ambition. It confirms the ladder PCC → piecewise-affine curvature → discrete Cosserat, with computational cost rising at each rung. For a senior-design build, this review's existence is the argument for stopping at the *bottom* rung: you do not need a Cosserat rod to command a stiffness.
- **Evidence grade:** C (preprint, not peer-reviewed at the version I fetched; useful as a signpost, not as evidence).
- **Limitations:** A review of models, not of assistive control. No contact-safety content, no human data. Every rod model in it still requires material parameter identification on your actual hardware, which is the part that eats the schedule.

## Verdict for the design team

Build a **joint-space impedance controller with gravity compensation, a commanded equilibrium posture, and a generalized-momentum residual watching for unexpected contact** — and nothing more clever than that. Hogan's result settles the top-level choice: your environment (a leaning human) is inertial, so the arm must behave as an impedance, and Keemink's flexibility analysis kills the admittance alternative outright, because structural compliance between your force sensor and your actuator is exactly the condition under which admittance control loses passivity — and that compliance is the entire point of your robot. Get your "commanded assistive force" from deflection against a known virtual stiffness (force = K · Δx) with a **characterised, calibrated pressure/tendon→force map**, not from a force-servo loop; ShoulderExosuit got a 40 % EMG reduction with a purely kinematics-triggered assist and no force feedback at all, and AAN-EMG shows **±6 N** was clinically meaningful on stroke patients, so a modest, open-loop-in-force assist is a defensible spec rather than a cop-out. Add exactly two safety mechanisms: a **hard saturation on commanded force/torque** sized to your injury budget, and a **scalar energy tank** gating any stiffness increase (TankVSC is ~20 lines and is the only thing in this pile that buys you a provable gentleness claim). For collision reaction, copy DLR-Reaction's *structure* — residual, threshold, switch to a reflex/admittance mode that lets the human push the arm away — but treat their numbers as unreachable: their 1 ms detection assumed a hard-real-time bus, and their real end-to-end latency was already 5–7 ms once comms were counted, so budget **20–50 ms** on realistic student hardware and say so in the report. Over-reach, to name it plainly: unified force-impedance control with energy tanks (UFIC), learned force/position tracking (SoftForceTracking), RL-based assist-as-needed (AR3n), and any Cosserat-rod model in the control loop are all graduate-thesis-scale and will consume your semester without producing a working demo. The single biggest evidence gap you must not paper over: **not one paper here validates interaction control on a freestanding soft torso that a person puts body weight into** — every human-subject result in this set is either a wearable grounded on the user's own skeleton or a rigid, bolted-down planar manipulandum, so your tipping/balance and sustained-quasi-static-lean cases are genuinely un-derisked by the literature and need their own bench test. Also be honest in the report that a momentum residual degrades from a force estimator to a crude change-detector on a soft plant, and that a slow lean is its worst-case input — which is precisely why the force saturation, not the residual, is your real safety guarantee.

## Adjacent topics I hit that my assignment did not cover

- **Intent detection from the force derivative (variable admittance).** Duchaine & Gosselin, "Safe, Stable and Intuitive Control for Physical Human-Robot Interaction," *ICRA 2009*, pp. 3383–3388, https://doi.org/10.1109/ROBOT.2009.5152664 (metadata verified via Crossref and the Semantic Scholar record; **I could not access the full text, so I make no claim about its numbers or results**). Their line of work infers accelerate/decelerate intent from `dF/dt` and modulates virtual damping accordingly. This belongs to whoever owns the intent/HMI topic; it also sits on the admittance branch this project should not take.
- **Injury biomechanics and impact-severity indices (HIC, ISO/TS 15066 force-pressure limits).** DLR-Reaction mentions a dummy-head HIC evaluation but does not develop it. Somebody on this team needs the actual pain/injury force thresholds for elderly skin and thorax — that number, not a control proof, sets your saturation limit.
- **Perceived safety and comfort as a measured outcome.** Several hits (human-soft-robot perceived-safety studies) treat "does it *feel* safe" as a dependent variable separate from physical safety. Relevant to your evaluation plan; not a control-architecture question.
- **Balance, tipping and the reaction load path of a freestanding assistive robot.** Completely absent from the control literature I surveyed; it is arguably the dominant safety failure mode for this specific robot and needs a structural/statics owner.
- **Actuator-level force characterisation and hysteresis compensation for pneumatic/tendon soft actuators.** Every "commanded force" claim above depends on a calibrated actuator→force map. That calibration is its own topic and probably its own bench test.
- **Series-elastic-actuator force-control bandwidth limits.** Keemink touches velocity-sourced SEA passivity in passing; the SEA-specific literature (achievable force bandwidth vs. spring stiffness) would settle whether a spring-in-series design beats a soft-link design for this robot.
